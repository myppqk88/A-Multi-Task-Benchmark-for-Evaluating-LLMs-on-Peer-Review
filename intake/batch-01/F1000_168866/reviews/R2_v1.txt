The manuscript offers a timely and compelling examination of the extent to which economics education curricula in Indonesia genuinely equip students with the financial competencies required for contemporary economic life. The topic is highly relevant, particularly in light of the increasing global emphasis on financial literacy as a foundational life skill and the persistent critique that higher education institutions often privilege theoretical knowledge at the expense of practical, actionable financial capabilities. The study’s qualitative content‑analysis design is appropriate for its exploratory aims; however, the methodological narrative would benefit from greater precision and transparency. In particular, the manuscript should elaborate on the criteria used to select curriculum documents, the sampling strategy for educator interviews, and the analytic procedures employed to ensure rigor—such as coding reliability, triangulation, or reflexive validation.
The findings are insightful and highlight a substantive gap between curricular content and the competencies required for effective financial decision‑making. The identification of underdeveloped areas—investment diversification, risk management, and digital financial tools—is particularly significant, given the rapid evolution of financial technologies and the growing complexity of personal finance. The students’ expressed desire for experiential learning modalities, including simulations, case‑based instruction, and industry collaboration, adds an important dimension to the analysis; however, the discussion would be strengthened by situating these insights within established theories of curriculum design, competency‑based education, and experiential learning. Moreover, the manuscript would benefit from a more explicit engagement with international benchmarks for financial literacy education, which could help contextualize Indonesia’s curricular gaps within broader global trends.
The conclusion appropriately underscores the need for more applied, digitally oriented, and industry‑linked financial literacy training. Nonetheless, the implications section remains somewhat general and could be enhanced by offering more concrete, actionable recommendations for curriculum developers, policymakers, and higher education leaders. The acknowledgment of methodological limitations is appropriate, though the authors might further articulate how future research—particularly multi‑institutional, mixed‑methods, or longitudinal studies—could deepen the evidence base and enhance the generalizability of the findings.
Overall, the manuscript addresses an important and underexplored dimension of economics education in Indonesia and demonstrates clear potential for scholarly contribution. Strengthening the theoretical framing, enhancing methodological clarity, and deepening the analytical discussion would significantly elevate the manuscript’s rigor and suitability for indexing in a Scopus‑indexed journal
