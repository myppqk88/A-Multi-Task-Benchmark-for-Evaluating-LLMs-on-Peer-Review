Authors’ Response:
A general definition of virtue was presented in Page 2, Line 13-15: They conceptualized virtue as “a property of the whole person and the life that person leads” (p. 87), which is a personal strength appreciated by the whole society (Peterson & Seligman 2004).
Specific definitions of the three virtues were added in Page 3, Line 12-16: The relationship virtue reflects “the love, concern, and gratitude of a person toward others”; vitality reflects “the curiosity and zest for creativity of an individual”; and conscientiousness is “an intrapersonal virtue that describes people who persist in achieving goals and exhibit self-control,” which reflects the individual orientation of the virtues (Ho et al. 2014a).
A new section named “CURRENT STUDY” (Page 4, Line 8-19) was added in the revision for forming the hypotheses of the present study.

Comments 3
The authors compare 3 groups. One group is curiously missing - those with indirect exposure and PTSD. If there is a reason why this group is omitted, this should be acknowledged and discussed.

Authors’ Response:
In our study, Participants were screened through the Life Events Checklist (Page 6, Line 1-5), who indicated at least one traumatic event as 1 = “happened to me” were defined as direct trauma samples, whereas respondents who indicated 2 = “witnessed it” and/or 3 = “learned about it” were indirect trauma samples. 
The indirect exposure and PTSD group was excluded because they did not meet the criterions of PTSD (Page 5, Line 11-15): Responses of the diagnosed PTSD participants also fulfilled the criterions of DSM-IV, including a) “history of a traumatic stressor,” b) “persistent re-experiencing of the traumatic event,” c) “persistent avoidance of stimuli associated with the trauma and numbing of general responsiveness,” and d) “persistent symptoms of increased arousal.”. Accordingly, the indirect trauma group partly failed to meet the above criterions.

Authors’ Response:

Comments 5
The authors note in the introduction that the factor structure of virtues is unstable and open to exploration. Therefore, I was wondering why they themselves did not conduct a factor analysis in their study.

Authors’ Response:
Our previous publication have already done this work and revealed a three-virtue structure among Chinese (Page 3, Line 8-12): Duan et al. (2012b) adopted the combined emic and etic approaches to select 96 cross-culturally equivalent items from the original 240-item Values In Action Inventory of Strengths by using a Chinese population (Ho et al. 2014b). Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses revealed 3 virtues, namely, relationship, vitality, and conscientiousness (Duan et al. 2013). Furthermore, a very recent study concluded that the current three-virtue solution might be universal. We cited this work in the revised MS (Page 3, Line 16-18): What’s more, independent research teams around the world likewise revealed the similar virtue structures in McGrath (in press) considered that the current three-factor solution should be universal.

Comments 6
While it seems like this paper's findings regarding virtues and PTG may be important, the authors need to significantly expand their discussion of the theoretical meaning of these results. For example, why are certain virtues more predictive of PTG than others? what is the unique nature of each of these specific virtues? etc.
Comments 7
This paper deals with the important topic of PTG and its correlates. It has several strengths, most notably a large sample size and an interesting set of predicting variables (virtues). However, the main weakness of this paper is the very limited attention given to theoretical aspects of the selected variables, and the meaning of the study's results. In order for this paper to be published, I recommend that the authors conduct a major revision, in which they would significantly add to the theoretical scope of this paper. Also, the rationale behind the specific division into 3 study groups needs to be much better explained. What did the authors expect to find regarding the various combinations of PTSD and exposure, and why? 

Authors’ Response:
Thanks for reviewer’s suggestion to reconstruct the introduction and discussion section. According to these suggestions, we revised the two parts:
The theoretical and practical significances of the current study were elaborated in the revised MS: (Page 4, Line 17-19) The current results will clarify the contributions of virtues in traumatic situations, which may facilitate a strength-based approach in both research and practice in the future; and (Page 9, Line 1-5) Accordingly, personal virtues may facilitate rebounding for individuals who experienced trauma. Individuals with different endorsed virtues often occupy different psychological resources (e.g., optimism, emotional control, and gratitude). Thus, they can maximize the use of different resources (i.e., diverse virtues) in various contexts. 
For each virtue, possible explanations were gave to explain why are certain virtues more predictive of PTG than others, especially in different contexts (Page 9-10).
For the unique nature of these specific virtues, please refer to our above response to Comments 1.
The rationale behind the specific division into 3 study groups was explained in the introduction. First, Page 3, Line 19-25 indicated the reasons to distinguish direct and indirect trauma group: the research conducted by Peterson et al. (2008) examined only the relationship among trauma samples, which accounted for 56% of the entire population. However, previous studies argued that stress-related life events could likewise facilitate stress-related growth, which is assessed by Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI), but to a lesser extent than traumatic events (e.g., LoSavio et al. 2011). The level of perceived stress that resulted from events might be the key cause of PTG, rather than the objective and specific events per se. Second, Page 3-4, Line 25-4 indicated the potential effect of PTSD: several individuals who underwent trauma may develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), whereas others may not (Yehuda & Flory 2007). The above study failed to consider the influence of PTSD on PTG in the trauma samples. An inverted-U curve was also found between PTG and PTSD, which suggested that PTG decreased after a moderate level of PTSD (Levine et al. 2008).
