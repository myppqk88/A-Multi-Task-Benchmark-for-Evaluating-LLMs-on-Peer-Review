We sincerely thank the reviewers for their thoughtful and constructive comments, which have greatly enhanced the quality and clarity of this manuscript. Detailed responses to each point raised in the review comments are provided below, addressing the suggestions and concerns individually.
This study conducts an investigation into the factors influencing luxury product adoption on Instagram, utilising a mixed-methods approach that integrates traditional statistical analysis with machine learning models.  The examination of Instagram marketing for luxury goods in the emerging market of India highlights a significant gap in existing literature and is highly pertinent to the field. Advanced analytical techniques involve the integration of machine learning models. The discovery that "surface-level" engagement metrics, such as time spent and story ads, lack significance, whereas psychological factors like trust, prestige, and personalisation are strong predictors, represents a crucial and actionable contribution.  This challenges traditional marketing metrics.
Nonetheless, the study is considerably constrained by a significant limitation: The research is hindered by an absence of a unified theoretical framework, resulting in fragmented hypothesis testing. The theories are relevant; however, they are not cohesively integrated. It is essential to address these fundamental issues for the paper to achieve the standards of a high-quality indexing.
Response:
We acknowledge the need for a more unified theoretical framework. While our study draws on the Theory of Planned Behavior, Social Identity Theory, and the Elaboration Likelihood Model, we have integrated these into a cohesive conceptual model. This model visually and theoretically links Instagram engagement, brand trust, purchase intention, sentiment, and adoption, clarifying how these constructs interrelate. This addresses the concern that the hypotheses appear fragmented and has been added to the literature review section as subsection 2.2 with the proposed conceptual framework.
The sample size (n=205) is insufficient for a machine learning approach and is exclusively from India, which complicates the applicability of the findings to other cases.
Response:
We recognize that a sample size of 205 may limit the generalizability of machine learning results. In the limitations section, we have explicitly stated that the sample size, while sufficient for initial insights, will be expanded in future research to improve model robustness and cross-cultural applicability. We also note that the study is focused on India, and future work will include diverse geographic contexts. (Please Refer page no – 30)
The lack of clarity in the definition and examples of "luxury products" may indicate that individuals had in mind a variety of distinct categories of products.
Response:
We have clarified the definition and examples of luxury products in the methodology section. The study focused on high-end fashion and accessories, such as luxury handbags, watches, apparel, and footwear, consistent with prior studies (e.g., Ko et al., 2019). This specificity has been explicitly stated to avoid ambiguity.
The study examines six distinct hypotheses utilising four varied analytical methods.  A comprehensive conceptual model illustrating the interrelations among the constructs of engagement, trust, purchase intention, and adoption is lacking.  The theoretical and empirical distinctions between H2 (Purchase Intention) and H4 (Adoption) remain ambiguous.  This results in the paper appearing as a collection of distinct mini-studies instead of a unified work.
Response:
A conceptual model diagram has been introduced to illustrate the relationships between engagement, psychological constructs, content types, sentiment, and adoption. This will help distinguish between purchase intention (H2) and adoption (H4), with adoption positioned as the ultimate behavioral outcome influenced by intention, trust, and other mediators.
The results are presented clearly with helpful visualizations. However, the interpretation often oversteps the data by implying causality. The conclusions do tie the elements together but are framed with more certainty than the methodological design permits.
Response:
We agree that causal claims are not supported by our cross-sectional design. The revised manuscript has more carefully been framed findings as predictive or associative, not causal. Language implying causation has been replaced with terms like "predictive association" or "influence," particularly in the results and discussion sections.
Omitted Variable Bias: Unmeasured factors, such as general consumer materialism, brand affinity, and cultural trends, are likely to affect both the independent variables, including perceived brand trust, and the dependent variable, which is adoption intention.
Response:
We acknowledge that unmeasured variables like materialism or brand affinity could influence results. This has explicitly been discussed as a limitation, and we have suggested that future studies include such constructs to better account for confounding effects.
Discussion and Limitations: A specific subsection should be incorporated into the discussion and limitations sections to explicitly address the issue of endogeneity, clarifying that the results demonstrate predictive power rather than causal relationships.
Response:
A new subsection has been added to the limitations addressing endogeneity. We have clarified that our models identify predictive relationships but cannot establish causality due to the research design and potential omitted variables.
Future Research: The authors ought to propose methodologies for subsequent studies to determine causality, including longitudinal designs, field experiments, or the application of instrumental variables.
Response:
We have expanded the future research section to include specific methods for establishing causality, such as longitudinal designs, field experiments, or instrumental variable approaches. This will provide a clearer pathway for subsequent studies to build on our findings.
