      It’s a good point that the notion of authoritarianism should be better explained. I now did
      so. The main reference in my works on this topic is Frankurt School critical theory and its
      concept of authoritarianism. I point out some details, including references. These are
      philosophers, so as suggested by the reviewer, I am going more into philosophy by
      engaging with the Frankurt School authors and their works, Arendt, and others:
      “Given we are experiencing new forms of authoritarianism such as digital authoritarianism,
      the question arises of how we understand authoritarianism. I follow the Frankfurt School-
      approach for conceptualising authoritarianism (including authors such as Wilhelm Reich,
      Erich Fromm, Theodor W. Adorno, Max Horkheimer, Herbert Marcuse, and Franz L.
      Neumann). To understand authoritarianism, Wilhelm Reich argued for an approach that
      integrates critical psychology, political economy, and ideology critique. In 1936, the
      Frankfurt School released its study on authority and family. The goal was to understand
      where and how fascism originated (Horkheimer 1936/1987). Fromm defined the
      authoritarian personality as an individual who “exercises aggression against the defenseless
      and sympathy with the powerful“ (Fromm 1936/2020, 24). Fromm characterised the
      authoritarian personality as necrophilic. He shows that Hitler was a necrophile and that the
      “necrophile demands annihilation” (Fromm 1973/1997, 406), that Stalin was a mental and
      physical sadist, and that both stand for human destructiveness that is characteristic for
      authoritarianism that is opposed to humanism. Authoritarianism includes top-down-
      leadership structures, nationalism, the friend/enemy-scheme, and repression/violence
      (Fuchs 2022a). Authoritarians define outsiders and scapegoats who are blamed for the ills of
      society and social systems and against whom they propagate the use of repressive or even
      violent means. The advantage of the notion of authoritarianism, as utilized by the Frankfurt
      School, is that it is neither micro nor macro, but micro, meso, and macro: Authoritarianism
      and fascism are not organised at single levels, but take place at encapsulated levels where
      each level presupposes and goes beyond the underlying level: the individual, the group,
      social movements/parties, social systems/Institutions, and society (Fuchs 2024, 2022a).
      Fascism is a particular, radical form of authoritarianism. Its central feature is that it favours,
      propagates, uses, and institutionalises violence as the main political means and
      organisational principle of society. Violence is the fascists’ ultima ratio. The fascist “cannot
      help feeling surrounded by traitors, and so continuously threatens to exterminate them”
      (Adorno 1975, 78). In fascism, politics “grows out of the barrel of a gun” (Arendt 1970, 37),
      while in a democracy, politics grows out of the “generative force of communicative
      freedom” (Habermas 1996, 151). “Terror becomes total when it becomes independent of all
      opposition; it rules supreme when nobody any longer stands in ist way. If lawfulness is the
      essence of non-tyrannical government and lawlessness is the essence of tyranny, then
      terror is the essence of totalitarian domination“ (Arendt 1951/1973, 464).
      Arendt (1951/1973) draws a distinction between authoritarianism and totalitarianism:
      “authority, no matter in what form, always is meant to restrict or limit freedom, but never to
      abolish it. Totalitarian domination, however, aims at abolishing freedom, even at
      eliminating human spontaneity in general, and by no means at a restriction of freedom no
      matter how tyrannical” (405). While for Arendt the problem is totalitarianism, for the
      Frankfurt School it is authoritarianism. Arendt sees totalitarianism as rooted in political
