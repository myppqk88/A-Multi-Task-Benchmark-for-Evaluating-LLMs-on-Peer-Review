       This article makes an exceptionally relevant and timely theoretical contribution to the
       contemporary debate on digital democracy and its ability to withstand authoritarian challenges.
       The author presents an innovative and robust conceptual framework that successfully combines
       systems theory, critical sociology, digital political economy and democratic theory.
       The article articulates a critical genealogy of the concept of resilience, tracing its evolution from
       systems theory (Holling) to socio-political applications and moving beyond the
       structuralist–functionalist approach that dominates existing literature. Privileging a perspective
       centred on human agency over systemic properties is a significant epistemological contribution
       that restores centrality to collective political action.
       The systematisation of literature on resilient democracy in section 3 is impressive in its
       completeness and analytical rigour. Rather than merely cataloguing existing definitions, the
       author critically identifies their limitations and proposes an original synthesis that integrates
       subjective and objective dimensions, structures, and practices. The conceptual distinction between
       'democratic resilience' and 'resilient democracy' is particularly valuable, as it clarifies
       terminological ambiguities in the literature.
       From a media studies perspective, the article provides an in-depth analysis of threats to digital
       democracy, ranging from technological monopolies and fake news to algorithmic surveillance and
       digital acceleration. It goes beyond superficial descriptions to identify the underlying structural
       logics of commercial colonisation, state control and the ideological distortion of the digital public
       sphere.
       The proposal of multidimensional strategies for resilient digital democracy (Table 3) constitutes
       the work's most innovative core. Integrating environmental, technological, political and economic-
       cultural dimensions demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of the complexity of digital
       democratic infrastructures.
       The argument in favour of cooperative platforms, digital public service media, and free software
       as infrastructures of resilience is particularly convincing. The author avoids naive techno-
       optimism, lucidly recognising the limits and contradictions of these models (e.g. tendency to
       remain marginal, risks of bureaucratisation, possible authoritarian co-optation), and proposing
       concrete solutions such as public-commons partnerships that transcend public-private logic.

       The critical perspective on the European Union is illuminating. The periodisation of EU digital
       policies identifies a precise historical trajectory and proposes an alternative centred on European
       democratic digital sovereignty.

       In summary, this is an exceptionally mature work of theory that significantly advances the state of
       the art in resilient digital democracy, offering refined conceptual tools and concrete strategic
       guidance.
