       REPORT
       Review of “What is and how do we achieve a resilient digital democracy?” By Christian Fuchs


       Major issues:

                The article makes an important contribution and is suitable for indexing; however, some

    revisions should be addressed prior to indexing.
           The aim of this article is to address the following questions: What is a resilient digital
    democracy? How can such a form of digital democracy be achieved? These questions necessarily
    involve the concept of democracy itself, not only the notion of resilience, as well as the digital
    dimension, which implies that authors who write on information and communication technologies
    should be engaged.
           In introduction, the author affirms, “The world is today politically highly polarised. Multiple
    crises have cascaded into a negative dialectic where global neoliberal capitalism has turned
    against liberal democracy, which has resulted in the emergence and success of new authoritarian
    movements.”
           Limiting the debate about resilient democracy and digital democracy to global neoliberal
    capitalism versus liberal democracy ignores the broader political discussion between left-wing and
    right-wing ideologies, such as socialism and liberalism.
           In the world, for example, in Latin America, the debate involves the left wing and the right
    wing. The left wing is identified as being socialist and the right wing as being liberal, and there are
    also extreme right-wingers.
           The author discusses digital democracy, but the article does not engage with authors who
    specifically address information and communication technologies. In addition, the article lacks
    references to political philosophers who are central to the concept of democracy.
    The author could have cited philosophers who write about consciousness, morality, and norms. By
    citing only sociologists, the analysis fails to capture the full dimension of the human being as a
    self-conscious agent endowed with consciousness and unconsciousness, reason, and reflection. As
    a result, the article presents a conception of the human being that is exclusively social.
    This is only a suggestion for the author and does not imply rejection of the article if it is not
    accepted.

    Minor issues:
    In the following, I offer some more specific comments.
    On page 3: Holling and Hofkircher are cited, but the author did not mention the year of the book,
    for he made a paraphrase.
           The author should indicate the year of publication of the works cited.

    On page 4: “The system boundary the delaminates[1] the system from its environment and the
    system’s dynamic (the behaviour that the elements in relation to each other show as reaction to
    inputs) continue to exist.”
           It is better to use the verb “to delimit” or “limit”. There is also a mistake in using the article
    “the” before “delaminates”; it should be “that”.
    The author should correct the phrase as follows: “The system boundary that limits (or delimits)...”

    On page 9: the author affirms, “Political disruption means that digital democracy platforms are
    censored, monitored, outlawed, or that violence is used against digital democracy operators or
    users.”
    The sentence suggests that the government cannot restrict digital platforms when they are
    committing crimes?
           A democratic government is fully justified in imposing fines, platform bans, or
    imprisonment when digital platforms are used to commit serious crimes, including the
    production, distribution, or display of illegal content involving children.
    The author should clarify this definition.

    On page 10: “[...] Digital democracy is economically disrupted when platforms that advance and
    support authoritarianism dominate the public sphere, or there is a lack of funding. The online spreading
    of fake news, deep fakes, hate speech, disinformation and post-truth culture, the formation of online
    echo chambers, and digital tabloidisation..[...]”.
    It feels like there are human beings behind those acts; the author did not name who really would
    like to threaten democracy. In the beginning, he asserts that “where global neoliberal capitalism
    has turned against liberal democracy, which has resulted in the emergence and success of new
    authoritarian movements.”
     But that threat to democracy only comes from neoliberal capitalism? What about the extreme
    liberals who share most of the same ideology as the liberal one? What about the extreme left who
    share the same ideology as his mates who are not extreme ones?
    We should not overlook the fact that neoliberalism is an ideology that emerged from classical
    liberalism.
    In my opinion, it is not “the neoliberals,” “the authoritarians,” “the far-left,” or “the liberals” as
    monolithic blocks that spread disinformation. Instead, specific actors within these spectrums,
    especially those with authoritarian, populist, or radical tendencies, adopt disinformation as a
    strategic tool. The current digital ecosystem, shaped by algorithms that reward extreme
    engagement, enables these tactics to spread rapidly, regardless of ideological origin.
    So, I would like the author to clarify this sentence.

    On page 10: “Authoritarianism and digital fascism”.
    I think there is no polarisation between neoliberal capitalism and liberal democracy, for neoliberal
    capitalism is just a vert of liberal democracy.
    So, who are the authoritarians and fascists? Neoliberal capitalists? Do neoliberal capitalists have
    sides or just want to make more money? So, I think the polarisation is between left and right or
    socialists and liberals. And among them, there are authoritarians and fascists.
    So, I would like the author to clarify in the text who the authoritarians and fascists are.

    On page 11: “The basic idea of digital autonomy [...] and that this can best be enabled by
    platforms that are autonomous from the power of private individuals’ money, political
    dictatorship, and ideological bias.
           When discussing ideological bias, we are referring to a type of cognitive bias. So, what kind
    of cognitive bias is it? The text needs a footnote to explain which cognitive bias this is.

    On page 12: “Authoritarianism means the dominance and rule of single persons or groups over
    the rest of the population.”
    Hannah Arendt, for example, defines authoritarianism as a regime in which power is concentrated
    in a single authority, whether an individual, a party, or the military, that does not tolerate
    opposition while allowing relatively autonomous spheres of private life. What is decisive is the
    suppression of political freedom and plurality, not the total penetration of private life
    characteristic of totalitarian regimes.
    The concept of authoritarianism used here is not referenced in the text. Which author
    defines it as the domination exercised by a group or individual? Or is this the author’s own
    definition?

    Page 13: “However, there can also be democratic forms of fascism (Amlinger & Nachtwey, 2025)
    where democracy is used to advance fascism.”

    The phrase “democratic forms of fascismo” is a contradiction, as fascism is fundamentally anti-
    democratic. The standard definition of fascism is a political ideology and regime characterized by
    extreme nationalism, mass mobilization, anti-liberalism, anti-pluralism, and the concentration of
    power in an authoritarian leader.
    I suggest the author revise this statement.
