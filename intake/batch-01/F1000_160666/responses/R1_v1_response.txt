Observation 1:
First, the discussion on dialectical methodology and its foundations is somewhat limited. It would be valuable to acknowledge Hegel’s framework limitations, as highlighted by postcolonial critiques. For instance, Fanon (1952, Black Skin, White Masks) emphasizes the need for ruptures rather than synthesis, and Hegel’s master-slave dialectic notably overlooks actual anti-slavery revolts (see Buck-Morss 2009,
Hegel, Haiti and Universal History
). Hegel’s system tends to absorb contradictions into synthesis, neglecting how structural violence creates and perpetuates irreconcilable antagonisms. Acknowledging the value of post-colonial and post-Hegelian dialectics could strengthen the argument (see Laura Doyle, 2020,
Inter-imperiality
; Laura Doyle et al., 2025,
Decolonial Constellations
). While it is possible that this dialectical approach has been addressed in another published paper, discussing post-Hegelian dialectical methodology here could help address some limitations, as the attention to the dynamic interaction between thesis and antithesis.

Observation 2:
Second, the article refers to the tendency to preserve dominant cultures but does not closely define the inter-ethnic relations among the major groups (i.e. Amhara, Tigray, Oromo, and Somali) that have shaped Ethiopian state formation and its contestations. To clarify the theoretical argument, it would be beneficial to provide concrete historical examples where the “vicious circle of contradiction and conflict” is evident.
Response 2:
In the revised article, the contradiction between these major ethnic groups and its ramifications has been included. Certain practical evidence and events that show the unending dialectical contradiction are also included in the revised article. Violent conflict that emanated from these contradictions is also included in the revised article.
Observation 3:
Third, the analytical rigor and contribution of the dialectical lens is not always clear. In this context, the authors seem to interpret dialectics more as a vicious circle than a virtuous one. It is important to note that dialectical ontology should not be solely equated with the negative aspects of this process. This leads me to critique the conceptualization of synthesis as the endpoint of contradiction rather than recognizing its mutual constitution and intersubjectivity (as the postcolonial critique does).  A more grounded empirical analysis would also help to shed light on the theorized argument.
