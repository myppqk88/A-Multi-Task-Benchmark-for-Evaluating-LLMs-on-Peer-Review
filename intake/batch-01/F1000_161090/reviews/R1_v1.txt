This study explored how individuals’ physiological and psychological characteristics affect their preferred speeds of bubble tube stimulation in sensory rooms, using a simulated upward-moving visual task. Results showed that people with higher interoceptive sensitivity preferred slower speeds, while those with higher anxiety or less accurate time perception preferred faster speeds. Visual discomfort also played a role, with those experiencing more discomfort tending to prefer slower visual motion. A combination of interoceptive accuracy, anxiety levels, time estimation ability, and visual sensitivity significantly predicted preferred stimulation speed. The findings highlight the importance of personalized sensory environments tailored to individual needs for optimal therapeutic impact.
1. Terminology.  Use “autism” instead of “ASD” throughout for a more inclusive and widely accepted terminology, especially in line with neurodiversity research.
2. Avoid Ableist Language. Refrain from using medicalized or deficit-based terms such as “impairment.” Instead, adopt terminology grounded in the social model of disability, which focuses on societal barriers rather than individual deficits.
3. Identity-First Language. Use “autistic individuals” rather than “individuals with autism” to align with current preferences within the autistic community and neurodiversity literature.
4. Improve Rationale Structure. Streamline the background by clearly introducing the key constructs (e.g., interoception, time perception, anxiety, visual discomfort), then presenting a focused rationale for the current study. Avoid repeating the aim multiple times.
5. Reference Gaps. In the paragraph starting “However, the precise mechanisms by which the bubble tubes induce relaxation remain poorly understood…”, add references to prior sensory integration or multi sensory environments research to contextualize the claim.
6. Existing Research. Although this is a novel area, relevant adjacent literature does exist. Draw clearer parallels to studies in sensory processing, neurodivergent experience, and multi-sensory therapeutic environments.
7. Conceptual Clarity
Interoception. Define which aspects of interoception are relevant (e.g., accuracy, awareness, sensibility), and clarify why they matter for this context. Reference foundational literature (e.g., Garfinkel et al., 2015; Critchley & Garfinkel, 2017).
Time Perception. Explicitly explain how time perception relates to interoception and anxiety in this study. Currently, it appears disconnected.
Anxiety and Visual Discomfort. Define anxiety more clinically (e.g., state vs. trait), and expand discussion of visual discomfort with more references. Both constructs are complex and under-contextualized in the paper.
8. Participant Characteristics. Clarify whether participants were asked about clinical diagnoses (e.g., anxiety, neurodevelopmental conditions), given their relevance to the research aims. If not assessed, provide a justification.
9. Stimuli Choice. Justify the use of Random Dot Motion (RDM) stimuli, particularly in the context of interoception and neurodiversity research, where its utility is not well-established.
10. Measures.  Provide a clearer, consolidated presentation of the scales and tasks used to measure anxiety, interoception, time perception, and visual discomfort—ideally in a dedicated table with psychometric properties and validation references.
11. Open Science Considerations. While SPSS is a common statistical tool, open science practices would benefit from clear data and protocol sharing. Explicitly state where readers can access the materials.
12. Statistical Rationale. Explain why Spearman’s correlation was used (e.g., non-normal distribution), and clarify whether regression models were corrected for multiple comparisons. Justify the model selection process more clearly.
13. Sample Size Concerns. The relatively small sample (N=50) may limit the power and generalizability of the regression analysis. Discuss limitations related to sample size and possible risks of overfitting or underpowered findings.
14. Streamline Results. Reporting of statistics (e.g., means, SDs, p-values, CI) could be streamlined for clarity. Use visual aids (tables or figures) to consolidate key outcomes for easier interpretation.
15. Limitations Section: Explicitly address the study’s limitations, including sample size, generalizability, reliance on self-report measures, and the exploratory nature of the design. This is currently underdeveloped and needed for transparency.
