To address this comment, we expanded the methodology section with detailed explanations:
This study employed a multi-stage stratified sampling approach to ensure diverse representation across different demographic segments. The research was conducted in Lang Son and Cao Bang provinces, which were selected due to their relatively low digital payment adoption rates. In the first stage, we identified key districts within each province where digital payments were increasingly promoted. In the second stage, a random sampling technique was used to select individual respondents who had prior exposure to digital payments. Eligibility criteria included: (1) individuals aged 18 and above, (2) those who had engaged in at least one digital transaction in the past six months. This approach ensured a mix of urban and rural respondents, capturing diverse perspectives on digital payment
The sample size of 509 valid responses was determined based on guidelines for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM).Hair et al. (2019) recommend a minimum of 200 cases for SEM analysis, with a ratio of 10:1 per observed variable. Given that our model includes multiple constructs, a sample exceeding 500 was targeted to enhance statistical power and model stability. The sample size follows recommendations from Kline (2023), which suggest that larger samples improve the robustness of SEM path estimates. This ensures that factor loadings and path coefficients remain stable, reducing the risk of sampling bias.
To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the survey instrument, a three-stage validation process was conducted before data collection. First, the questionnaire was developed based on established scales from previous studies on Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and Trust in Digital Payments (Ajzen, 1991; Davis, 1989; Gefen et al., 2003). To assess content validity, the survey items were reviewed by three fintech experts and two academic researchers specializing in technology adoption and digital payments. Their feedback helped refine the wording and ensure the constructs accurately captured user perceptions in the Vietnamese context. Second, a pilot study was conducted with 100 participants from Thai Nguyen and Cao Bang to evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire. Participants were asked to highlight any ambiguous or difficult-to-understand questions. Based on their feedback, minor wording adjustments were made to improve clarity without altering the intended meaning of the items. Finally, statistical validation of the instrument was planned before hypothesis testing. Specifically, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) would be employed to assess construct validity, while Cronbach’s alpha, Composite Reliability (CR), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) would be used to evaluate internal consistency and reliability. The results of these tests are reported in the Results section. By implementing this rigorous validation process before the main data collection, the study ensured that the measurement instrument was robust and suitable for examining the proposed research model.

4.1. Measurement model
This study follows a two-step SEM approach as recommended by Hair et al. (2019). In the first step, the measurement model was assessed using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to ensure construct validity, reliability, and overall model fit. Only after the measurement model demonstrated an acceptable fit was the structural model estimated. The second step involved testing the structural model, where maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) was applied to examine the relationships among latent variables. MLE was selected due to its robustness in handling non-normality and its effectiveness when the sample size exceeds 200, as supported by Kline (2018).
Before proceeding with SEM model testing to evaluate the hypotheses, we conducted EFA and CFA to assess the variables' construct and confirm the reliability and validity of the measurement model. The results from the structural model estimation support most of the hypothesized relationships, confirming the theoretical foundations of the Trust-TAM-TPB model in the context of digital payment adoption. Notably, perceived ease of use (PEU) was found to have a significant positive impact on trust (β = 0.404, p < 0.001). This finding aligns with previous research (Gefen et al., 2003), which suggests that when users perceive a digital payment system as easy to use, they are more likely to develop confidence in its reliability and security. Additionally, trust was shown to significantly enhance perceived usefulness (PU) (β = 0.478, p < 0.001), reinforcing the notion that users find digital payment systems more beneficial when they have confidence in their security and operational effectiveness. A key theoretical confirmation from the findings is the strong influence of attitude (ATT) on digital payment adoption (DPA) (β = 0.186, p = 0.002). This insight suggests that beyond promoting usefulness, digital payment providers should emphasize trust-building strategies and behavioral incentives to encourage attitudinal change and subsequent adoption. Overall, the findings validate the core structure of the Trust-TAM-TPB model while revealing context-specific nuances that should be considered in digital payment adoption strategies. We added in Section 5.2: Theoretical Contributions, as it builds upon the study's findings The previous body of literature concerning intentions to adopt digital payments has primarily relied on established theories such as TRA, TAM, TPB, UTAUT, and UTATU2 (Kabir et al., 2017; Susanto et al., 2022). In line with this existing research, the findings of this study provide significant theoretical contributions by extending the TAM and TPB with Trust in the context of digital payment adoption. While previous studies have separately examined TAM (Davis, 1989) and TPB (Ajzen, 1991) in technology adoption, the integration of Trust as a key factor offers a more comprehensive framework for understanding digital payment behavior. The results confirm that Trust is not only an antecedent of PU but also directly influences ATT, SN, and PBC. This study’s empirical findings validate the C-TAM-TPB model (Combined TAM-TPB with Trust) by demonstrating that Trust plays a dual role in digital payment adoption. First, Trust enhances PU (β = 0.478, p < 0.001), indicating that users who perceive digital payments as secure and reliable also find them more beneficial. Second, Trust has a direct positive impact on ATT (β = 0.751, p < 0.001) and SN (β = 0.362, p < 0.001), confirming that when users trust digital payment providers, they develop a more positive perception of the technology and are more influenced by social endorsement. These relationships highlight that Trust acts as a bridge between technological perceptions (TAM) and behavioral intentions (TPB), addressing a critical gap in prior research (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). A particularly noteworthy finding of this study is the negative relationship between PU and ATT (β = -0.131, p = 0.024). This contradicts traditional TAM assumptions (Wu & Chen, 2005) and suggests that, in Vietnam’s northern mountainous regions, users may recognize the benefits of digital payments but still resist adoption due to ingrained cash-based habits. This finding underscores the importance of Trust as a mediating factor, as higher levels of trust in digital payments can mitigate resistance even when usefulness alone does not generate positive attitudes. By integrating Trust into TAM and TPB, this study advances existing technology acceptance theories in financial technology adoption. The C-TAM-TPB model provides a more holistic perspective, recognizing that digital payment adoption is not solely driven by ease of use and usefulness but is equally influenced by trust, social expectations, and perceived behavioral control. This aligns with recent research emphasizing the increasing role of security, privacy, and trust in financial technology adoption (Giang et al., 2024; Siagian et al., 2022). The theoretical implications of this study extend beyond digital payments and offer valuable insights into broader fintech adoption, mobile banking, and e-wallet services. As emerging markets continue to transition from cash-based to digital economies, trust-centered frameworks will become increasingly important in explaining user behavior toward digital financial services.
