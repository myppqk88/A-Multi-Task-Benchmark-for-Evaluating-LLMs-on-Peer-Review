1.1. We revised the abstract to highlight the uniqueness of this research and clearly define the methodology used.
Background
Digital payment systems are pivotal in the digital economy, relying on the interplay between internet technology and e-vendors. While the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) have been extensively used to explain technology adoption, the role of trust in financial technology adoption remains underexplored. This study addresses this gap by developing an extended Trust-TAM-TPB model, providing a comprehensive framework to analyze digital payment adoption in emerging markets.
Methods
A quantitative approach was adopted, analyzing survey data from 509 respondents using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). The extended model examines both technological factors (perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use) and trust-related factors (trust’s influence on behavioral intention via subjective norms, attitude, and perceived behavioral control).
Results
Findings confirm that trust is a significant determinant of digital payment adoption, influencing both perceived usefulness and subjective norms. However, a negative relationship was found between perceived usefulness (PU) and attitude (ATT) (β = -0.131, p = 0.024), suggesting that while users recognize the benefits of digital payments, their attitudes may still be shaped by traditional cash-based habits and security concerns. These insights challenge traditional TAM assumptions and emphasize the importance of trust in driving adoption.
Conclusions
This study contributes to technology acceptance literature by integrating trust into the TAM-TPB framework and highlighting its dual role in shaping both perceived usefulness and behavioral intention. Practically, the findings suggest that policymakers and financial institutions should prioritize trust-building strategies, including fraud prevention measures, financial literacy programs, and transparent transaction policies, to accelerate digital payment adoption in developing economies. These insights are particularly relevant for Vietnam’s Northern mountainous regions, where digital payment penetration remains low.

5.1. Discussion results
TAM model
Our findings align with prior literature on technology adoption and trust, reinforcing the idea that perceived ease of use (PEU) and trust (TR) are critical for overcoming digital payment resistance (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). The negative PU → ATT relationship observed in this study suggests that usefulness alone is not sufficient in encouraging adoption, especially in economies with entrenched cash-based behaviors. This aligns with knowledge-sharing theories, where social influence and trust networks play a crucial role in facilitating the adoption of new technologies (Chow & Chan, 2008; Wang & Noe, 2010).
TPB model
Findings related to subjective norms (SN) further support the knowledge-sharing perspective, as social influence plays a key role in shaping user trust in digital payments. Reliable information from trusted sources like friends, family, or communities boosts confidence, reinforcing prior research that suggests trust-based peer recommendations are essential for technology adoption (Bock et al., 2005; Lin, 2007).
Trust and TAM -TPB relationship
This study extends the literature by showing that trust acts as a bridge between technological perceptions (TAM) and behavioral intentions (TPB), further reinforcing the interconnected role of technology and trust in financial decision-making. The dual role of trust in shaping both perceived usefulness (PU) and behavioral control aligns with broader fintech research, emphasizing that trust is a prerequisite for digital financial engagement (Nguyen & Ao, 2022; Siagian et al., 2022).
5.2. Theoretical contributions
In line with this existing research, the findings of this study provide significant theoretical contributions by extending the TAM and TPB with Trust in the context of digital payment adoption. While previous studies have separately examined TAM (Davis, 1989) and TPB (Ajzen, 1991) in technology adoption, the integration of Trust as a key factor offers a more comprehensive framework for understanding digital payment behavior. The results confirm that Trust is not only an antecedent of PU but also directly influences ATT, SN, and PBC.
This study’s empirical findings validate the C-TAM-TPB model (Combined TAM-TPB with Trust) by demonstrating that Trust plays a dual role in digital payment adoption. First, Trust enhances PU (β = 0.478, p < 0.001), indicating that users who perceive digital payments as secure and reliable also find them more beneficial. Second, Trust has a direct positive impact on ATT (β = 0.751, p < 0.001) and SN (β = 0.362, p < 0.001), confirming that when users trust digital payment providers, they develop a more positive perception of the technology and are more influenced by social endorsement. These relationships highlight that Trust acts as a bridge between technological perceptions (TAM) and behavioral intentions (TPB), addressing a critical gap in prior research (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002).
A particularly noteworthy finding of this study is the negative relationship between PU and ATT (β = -0.131, p = 0.024). This contradicts traditional TAM assumptions (Wu & Chen, 2005) and suggests that, in Vietnam’s northern mountainous regions, users may recognize the benefits of digital payments but still resist adoption due to ingrained cash-based habits. This finding underscores the importance of Trust as a mediating factor, as higher levels of trust in digital payments can mitigate resistance even when usefulness alone does not generate positive attitudes.
Moreover, this study extends the application of knowledge-sharing theories to digital payment adoption. Trust is a critical element in the diffusion of new technologies, as it reduces uncertainty and encourages users to rely on social proof before making adoption decisions (Chow & Chan, 2008; Wang & Noe, 2010). This suggests that digital payment behavior is not just influenced by individual decision-making but also by social endorsement and peer-driven knowledge exchange.
By integrating Trust into TAM and TPB, this study advances existing technology acceptance theories in financial technology adoption. The C-TAM-TPB model provides a more holistic perspective, recognizing that digital payment adoption is not solely driven by ease of use and usefulness but is equally influenced by trust, social expectations, and perceived behavioral control. This aligns with recent research emphasizing the increasing role of security, privacy, and trust in financial technology adoption (Giang et al., 2024; Siagian et al., 2022). The theoretical implications of this study extend beyond digital payments and offer valuable insights into broader fintech adoption, mobile banking, and e-wallet services. As emerging markets continue to transition from cash-based to digital economies, trust-centered frameworks will become increasingly important in explaining user behavior toward digital financial services.
5.3. Practical implications
Trust-building mechanisms, such as transparent data privacy policies, fraud prevention systems, and community-driven endorsements, can help accelerate digital payment adoption. Policymakers should implement regulations that ensure consumer rights are protected in digital transactions, reinforcing public confidence in fintech solutions.
Additionally, leveraging peer-driven adoption strategies can be highly effective. Encouraging early adopters to act as digital payment ambassadors within their communities could create a cascading trust effect, increasing acceptance among hesitant users. Service providers should integrate referral programs and social proof elements into their marketing strategies to leverage the influence of subjective norms on digital payment adoption.
Practically, this suggests that policymakers and service providers should implement trust-enhancing strategies, such as fraud prevention measures, peer-driven digital literacy campaigns, and transparency in financial transactions. Given that knowledge-sharing networks play a role in adoption, leveraging community-based programs to increase trust and familiarity with digital payments could prove highly effective in cash-dependent regions.
5.4. Limitations
It also should explore the role of trust in different financial ecosystems and investigate how digital literacy influences adoption. Longitudinal studies could provide further insights into how user trust and digital payment behavior evolve over time in emerging markets. Finally, qualitative research could provide deeper insights into the psychological and social barriers that prevent consumers from adopting digital payments. Conducting interviews or focus groups with users hesitant to adopt digital payment systems could help identify specific pain points and guide targeted intervention strategies.
6. Conclusion
A particularly noteworthy finding of this study is the negative relationship between PU and ATT . This contradicts traditional TAM assumptions (Wu & Chen, 2005) and suggests that, in Vietnam’s Northern mountainous regions, users may recognize the benefits of digital payments but still resist adoption due to ingrained cash-based habits. This finding underscores the importance of Trust as a mediating factor, as higher levels of trust in digital payments can mitigate resistance even when usefulness alone does not generate positive attitudes. Finally, qualitative research could provide deeper insights into the psychological and social barriers that prevent consumers from adopting digital payments. Conducting interviews or focus groups with users hesitant to adopt digital payment systems could help identify specific pain points and guide targeted intervention strategies.
Furthermore, this study provides valuable insights into the integration of knowledge-sharing frameworks within digital payment adoption. Trust is not just a factor influencing user acceptance but also plays a role in how information about digital payments is shared within communities. This suggests that adoption strategies should not only focus on improving system usability but also on fostering trust through social influence and community-based interventions.
From a theoretical perspective, this study refines the Trust-TAM-TPB model, positioning trust as a bridging factor between technology acceptance and behavioral intention. This integration offers a more comprehensive framework for understanding digital payment behavior in regions with low fintech penetration.
Practically, these findings suggest that financial institutions, policymakers, and technology developers should prioritize trust-building strategies. Strengthening fraud protection measures, launching targeted financial literacy programs, and leveraging peer recommendations could accelerate adoption. Service providers must not only improve system usability but also foster trust through transparent policies, consumer protection mechanisms, and community engagement initiatives.
Finally, the study highlights the need for further research to explore the dynamic nature of trust in digital payments. Given the rapid evolution of financial technology, longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how user perceptions shift over time, allowing policymakers and service providers to adjust their strategies accordingly.
