       The manuscript presents the results of a systematic literature review (SLR) of existing review
       papers on entrepreneurial competences, complemented by the inclusion of grey
       literature—mainly documents from the European Commission—and by the use of the Delphi
       methodology to validate and refine the resulting framework. The paper is clearly written, with its
       theoretical foundation solidly grounded in established frameworks such as EntreComp and EICAA.
       The author’s ambition to bridge the theoretical, methodological, and practical dimensions of
       entrepreneurship education is noteworthy.
       The combined use of an SLR and the Delphi method provides a coherent research design: the SLR
       establishes an evidence base, while the Delphi rounds add expert judgement and interpretive
       depth. The inclusion of grey literature strengthens the article’s purpose, partly mitigating the
       consequences of the SLR’s literature selection having been concluded before 2021. It also has the
       merit of linking conceptual discussions with policy realities and practical implementation.
       Despite these merits, there are serious structural and ethical issues that need to be addressed
       before this article can remain available online in its current form. As indicated in the ethics
       statement, a large portion of the material derives from the EICAA project, coordinated by a
       consortium. A closer analysis of the project’s outcomes—particularly the WP2 deliverables,
       including The Systematic Literature Review of Existing Tools for Entrepreneurial Assessment and
       Inventory of Tools and The EICAA Competence Framework—shows that substantial parts of the
       information presented here already appear in those publicly available documents.
       This raises several concerns:
            1. Lack of explicit acknowledgment of project sources
               The EICAA project and its deliverables are not cited in the text or listed in the bibliography,
               even though the author contributed to these documents. This omission compromises
               transparency and represents a serious ethical breach. The introduction must clearly situate
               the article within the EICAA project and properly acknowledge its intellectual and
               documentary sources.
            2. Relevance and currency
               The EICAA project dates back to 2021, and the SLR conducted as part of that project—and
               now forming the basis of this article—only considers literature published up to that year.

            For a study submitted in 2025, this temporal gap significantly undermines its relevance,
            particularly in a fast-evolving field such as entrepreneurship education and competence
            frameworks. As already noted, the use of grey literature partly mitigates this limitation, but
            it remains advisable to undertake a more robust search, even within grey sources, to
            update and complement the findings with studies published since 2021, ensuring that the
            paper reflects the current state of knowledge.
         3. Overlap with previously published or presented work
            The paper “The EICAA Competence Framework: A Suggestion for EntreComp 2.0” (EAIR 45th
            Annual Forum, 2023) presents content and arguments that closely resemble sections of the
            current article. This overlap requires explicit acknowledgement. The author is not listed
            among the contributors of that 2023 paper, yet the similarities suggest extensive use of
            shared or derivative material.
         4. Authorship and originality
            Given that the literature review and the EICAA framework were produced collaboratively
            across partner institutions, the article must clarify whether it contains any original research
            conducted solely by the author. The absence of co-authors should be justified, and the
            manuscript should explicitly delineate which sections represent the author’s individual
            contribution.
    If these issues are fully addressed, several additional academic points also require attention.

    1. Structure and missing sections
    The article does not follow a conventional academic format. Sections discussing the implications of
    the findings, the limitations of the study, and directions for future research are absent. These
    omissions make the manuscript resemble a project deliverable rather than a research article. The
    author should include these sections to align the paper with accepted academic standards.

    2. Outdated and incomplete references
    The reference list relies heavily on older sources, many of which are more than ten years old. More
    recent research on entrepreneurial competences should be incorporated.
        ○ For example, the statement “The ability to act and think entrepreneurially is a crucial
          endowment in today’s knowledge-based society” is supported by references more than
          thirteen years old.
        ○ The following statements lack references altogether:
              ○  “However, an operational setting that allows for the ‘capture’ of entrepreneurial
                 competence profiles of learners has not been established yet.”
              ○  “As a result, Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) and businesses lack a coherent,
                 instrumental basis for assessing individual entrepreneurial competences and accurately
                 addressing specific competence development needs through education and training
                 interventions in response to the assessment results.”
        ○ Similarly, these important definitions require supporting citations:
              ○  “A competence framework (CF) is a structured (often hierarchical) model that outlines the
                 specific competences required for effective performance in an educational or professional
                 setting. A competence framework provides a list of the competences required by an
                 individual or group to perform successfully in each domain. Developing CF is essential for
                 any training plan. Therefore, it is especially necessary to develop competence-based
                 training strategies.”
        ○ The document cited regarding entrepreneurship as a key competence dates from 2006,
          although a more recent and authoritative source exists: Council of the European Union (2018).

    3. Reference to contextual factors
    The section on contextual factors requires clearer explanation. The sources of the competence
    structures used in the analysis must be explicitly referenced so that readers can trace how these
    frameworks were selected, adapted, and interpreted.

    4. Timeliness of the SLR
    As noted, the SLR concludes in 2021. The author must clarify whether the findings have been
    updated or revalidated with more recent sources. A systematic review that omits four years of
    potentially significant developments cannot credibly claim to represent the current state of
    knowledge.

    5. Scope and inclusiveness of the SLR
    The decision to restrict the corpus to review papers with more than 100 citations is
    methodologically problematic. Although the intention may have been to focus on influential
    studies, this criterion conflates citation frequency with quality, introducing a clear bibliometric
    bias.
    Such an approach privileges older, English-language, and mainstream studies while marginalising
    newer or less cited works that might offer innovative or critical insights. Consequently, the
    SLR—and the subsequent Delphi process based on it—risks reinforcing established paradigms
    rather than broadening conceptual diversity.
    The author should explicitly acknowledge this limitation and, ideally, relax or remove the citation
    threshold. Citation counts should serve a descriptive purpose only, not as inclusion criteria.
    Addressing this issue would significantly strengthen the paper’s methodological and
    epistemological robustness.

    6. Methodological transparency
    The paper would benefit from a more detailed description of the SLR and Delphi procedures.
    Missing information includes:
       ○  the period during which the Delphi panels were conducted;
       ○   the number and expertise of the participants;
       ○   the number of rounds carried out;
       ○   the criteria used to determine consensus; and
       ○   the specific protocols used for coding and content analysis.
    For instance, the paper notes that the content analysis protocol in NVivo was written, shared, and
    refined before coding began, yet it provides no details about the researchers involved, their
    profiles, or the nature of the decisions taken. Likewise, the questionnaire used to interpret
    competences identified through the SLR should be made available—either as an appendix or as
    supplementary material.

    EntreComp 2.0” (EAIR 45th Annual Forum, 2023) presents a far more detailed account of the
    adopted Delphi procedures than the author provides here. To quote:
    “To ensure clarity and coherence in the rubric statements, we engaged in multiple online Delphi
    rounds involving all consortium partners, supplemented by offline inputs. Each change was
    subjected to deliberation, discussion, and voting. Ultimately, decisions were taken by majority vote
    or deferred to the analysis of the forthcoming pre-test (PT1). By following a rigorous methodology
    and harnessing the expertise of various stakeholders, including senior researchers, we
    successfully developed and enhanced the rubric system. This system then served as the
    foundation for designing a self-assessment survey of entrepreneurial competences (the EICAA
    Competence Monitor, CM), which was refined in several iterations following a Delphi process and
    multiple pre-tests.”
    Including a comparable level of methodological detail in the present paper would significantly
    enhance its transparency and credibility.

    7. Engagement with recent and policy-relevant literature
    The review would be considerably strengthened by engaging more directly with current
    European policy debates on entrepreneurship education. In particular, Banha et al. (2025), “A
    critical analysis of the Portuguese Framework for Entrepreneurship Education considering the
    EntreComp competence framework” (European Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship), provides
    an up-to-date example of how competence frameworks are being interpreted and implemented in
    national contexts. Incorporating this work would situate Teodoro’s analysis within ongoing
    European policy discussions and reinforce its practical and comparative relevance.

    8. Final assessment
    This article has the potential to make a valuable contribution, and its publication in this outlet
    would be welcome. However, substantial academic, methodological, and ethical revisions are
    required before it can be considered suitable for indexing.
    Given the current overlap with previous project outputs, the lack of acknowledgement of co-
    authorship, the outdated evidence base, and the incomplete methodological description, the
    paper in its present form warrants major revisions.
