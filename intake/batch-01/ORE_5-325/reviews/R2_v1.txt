       Dear Author,

       thank you for the opportunity to review your manuscript on Cuban return migration and the
       revaluation of work, business, and moral economies in the post-2012 reform context. The topic is
       timely and relevant, speaking to debates on return migration, transnationalism, and the
       moral–economic dimensions of livelihood in conditions of crisis and transformation. The long-
       term ethnographic perspective has clear potential to yield rich, situated insights. At the same time,
       the manuscript would benefit from revisions to enhance conceptual positioning (explicit research
       gap and contribution), structural coherence (clear separation of Methods, Results, Discussion, and
       Conclusions), methodological transparency (sampling, data collection, analytic procedures, ethics),
       and consistency in academic register and citation style. Below I provide detailed, constructive
       suggestions aimed at strengthening clarity, rigor, and the overall scholarly impact of the work.

       Abstract
          1. The phrasing “about 15–20 returnees” is too general. The manuscript should provide a
             precise number of participants (e.g., “18 individuals”) and—if relevant—the gender
             distribution. Additionally, the abstract refers to “men,” whereas the Methods mention
             “mainly men”; it is recommended to harmonise this description.
          2. A brief, unambiguous statement of how the data were collected and analysed is missing. It
             is recommended to specify the sampling approach, techniques, and mode of analysis.
          3. The abstract states the aim but does not explicitly indicate the research gap or the novel
             contribution (e.g., the moral–performative dimension of the economic reintegration of
             returning men in the context of the most recent reforms). It would be worthwhile to add

        4. The abstract should signal limitations (e.g., small, purposive sample; focus on men) and
           future directions (e.g., gender comparisons; differentiation by host country/type of
           business); it is suggested to add 1–2 sentences.
        5. The current abstract is divided into sections (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions).
           According to the guidelines, it should be a continuous text.
    Introduction
        1. The introduction is written in the first person (“I…”), giving it an autobiographical character.
           It is recommended to use an impersonal style and focus on the research problem and state
           of the art rather than the author’s research trajectory.
        2. The introduction includes field vignettes and respondent quotations (e.g., Wilson, Yusniel,
           Saúl), which belong to the Results section. The empirical material should be moved to
           Results or shortened to 1–2 motivational sentences without quotations.
        3. The introduction does not clearly articulate why this problem requires analysis under
           current conditions (post-2012 reforms, dynamics of returns) nor situate it in the current
           state of knowledge; it would be worthwhile to clarify this.
        4. The introduction does not explicitly state which gap in the literature the article addresses
           nor what is novel; it is recommended to add a short paragraph.
        5. The introduction lacks a clear study aim, which makes it difficult to assess the adequacy of
           subsequent methods and analyses; it is suggested to include a one-sentence aim at the end
           of the introduction.
        6. Citations are incidental and descriptive; a concise synthesis of key strands (return migration,
           transnationalism, moral economy) and positioning of the paper’s contribution is missing; it
           would be worthwhile to add a brief review.
        7. Inconsistency and non-conformity of citation style. Various formats occur (author–year
           with/without page numbers, “&” vs “and”, footnotes). It is recommended to standardise to
           the required style.
        8. The introduction does not end with a brief outline of the manuscript’s organisation, which
           typically indicates the scope and sequence of sections; this reduces navigability and
           transparency of the analytical flow; it is suggested to supply the missing information.
        9. Information such as “fieldwork started in 2005… I started undertaking fieldwork…” would be
           better placed in Methods; only minimal context should remain in the introduction.
    Methods and Results
        1. The Methods section contains empirical material and interpretations (e.g., descriptions of
           practices “doing negocios, becoming dueño,” vignettes of Roberto/Joel/Jorge). These
           elements should be moved to a separate Results section; Methods should be limited to the
           project description, sample, data collection procedures, mode of analysis, ethics, and
           limitations.
        2. There is an inconsistency between “men” (in the introduction/abstract) and “mainly men” (in
           Methods). A precise sample size (N) and exact gender structure (e.g., “N=18; 18 men” or
           “N=18; 16 men, 2 women”) should be provided and used consistently throughout the
           manuscript.
        3. The formula “about 15–20 returnees” is insufficient. The Methods section should provide a
           single unambiguous N with justification of sample size (e.g., inclusion/exclusion criteria,
           point of saturation).
        4. Mentions of “fieldwork and complementary research” do not amount to a clear project plan.
           It is recommended to present the chronology and phases (sites, field periods, stages of data
           collection) and the relation between “main” and “complementary” components.

        5. The description of participant selection is insufficient: it lacks clarity on the procedure used
           (e.g., purposive sampling, snowball sampling), inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment
           channels, and sources of field contacts. It would be worthwhile to add this information to
           enable assessment of transferability and credibility.
        6. Participant observation and repeated informal conversations (without recordings) were
           used. It is suggested to add the approximate number and duration of
           conversations/meetings, contexts (domestic/occupational), language and documentation
           mode (timing of field-note writing), and whether a conversation guide was used.
        7. The text presents vignettes of several individuals (e.g., Joel) while a larger sample is
           declared. It is recommended to clarify how many cases are presented in detail and how
           material from the remaining participants was incorporated into the analysis (e.g.,
           contribution to themes/codes).
        8. The mode of analysis is missing (e.g., inductive thematic analysis, iterative coding, abductive
           approach), tools (e.g., CAQDAS), code scheme, validation procedures (triangulation, peer
           debriefing), and criteria for ending analysis (saturation). A full description of the analysis is
           recommended.
        9. Ethics and participant protection: consents are indicated only generally; standard elements
           are missing—type of consent (oral/written), approving body (ERC/ethics committee),
           pseudonymisation, data storage, handling of sensitive data. It would be worthwhile to add
           these details.
       10. Mode of exposition (impersonal style and academic register). First-person formulations
           dominate. It is recommended to use impersonal forms (“was conducted/was observed/was
           coded”).
       11. The statement “Most had returned and undergone the formal repatriation process between
           2013 and 2020.” should be specified: how many individuals, when, and whether repatriation
           status was an inclusion criterion.
       12. In qualitative research, strict quantitative replicability is not required; the standards are
           transparency, credibility, confirmability, and transferability. The current description would
           benefit from a clearer audit trail (details of sampling, analytical procedures, and decisions).
       13. Inconsistencies in the citation system occur in Methods and elsewhere in the manuscript. It
           is recommended to standardise to the required style.
       14. Case proportions. The Joel vignette is disproportionately extended relative to others, which
           disrupts representativeness and structure. In Methods it would be advisable to avoid
           extensive single-case exposition; move it to Results and balance across cases.
       15. The narrative style dominates over procedural description. It would be worthwhile to
           discipline the sections by using short, precise sentences describing
           who/what/when/how/why.
    Discussion
        1. There is no standalone Discussion section. Interpretations are scattered within results;
           there is no synthetic treatment of findings against the literature, no articulation of
           theoretical/practical implications, study limitations, or future directions. It is recommended
           to create a separate Discussion that links the findings to the aim and research gap,
           compares them with prior work, presents limitations (e.g., purposive sampling, male-
           skewed sample, two locations, no recordings), and outlines future research paths.
    Conclusion
        1. The use of “I hope…” lowers the formal academic register. It is recommended to use
           impersonal and conclusive formulations (e.g., “The article shows that…”).
        2. Breach of the division of functions between Discussion and Results. The Conclusions contain

           extended interpretations, empirical examples, and theoretical frames (references to Fischer,
           Zigon, Callon), which should be moved to Discussion. The final section should be limited to a
           concise synthesis of findings, contribution, implications, and future research.
        3. Lack of synthesis of key findings. Instead of a concise set of 3–5 summary points, the section
           repeats illustrative narratives; it is suggested to present clear bullet-pointed claims.
        4. Underspecified contribution to the literature. The novelty of the paper is not stated
           explicitly; it is recommended to articulate the contribution clearly.
        5. Lack of articulation of limitations and research agenda. Project limitations (purposive
           sampling, male-skewed sample, two locations, no recordings) and specific proposals for
           future work (gender comparisons, differentiation by host country and sector, post-2020
           trajectories) are not signalled; it would be worthwhile to add these.
        6. Redundancy of narrative material. Recalling detailed vignettes (Humbe, Joel, Jorge)
           duplicates results content and overextends the Conclusions; it is recommended to remove
           single-case examples from Conclusions.
        7. Inconsistency and occasional nature of citations. Bibliographic references in Conclusions
           are sporadic and not formatted consistently; it is recommended to standardise citation style
           according to the guidelines.
        8. The Conclusions do not relate the findings directly to the study aim (no clear aim in the
           Introduction), which reduces argumentative coherence and logical continuity; it is
           suggested to add a conclusive sentence indicating how the findings address the
           aim/research questions.
        9. The Conclusions lack a single, high-level “take-home message” that condenses the main
           contribution and its significance for the literature; it would be worthwhile to add a final,
           high-level statement.
