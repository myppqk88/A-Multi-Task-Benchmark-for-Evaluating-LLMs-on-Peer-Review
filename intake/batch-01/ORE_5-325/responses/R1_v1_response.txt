           2. Scope and positioning: We acknowledge entrepreneurship theory as a relevant

              distributive effects. Rather than building typologies of entrepreneurial motivation or
              adopting a business-school framework as the primary lens, we treat
              “entrepreneurship” ethnographically—as a morally charged and socially tested form
              of personhood and legitimacy under constraint. That said, we now speak more
              directly to entrepreneurship and inequality scholarship in Cuba (and to the
              constraints shaping enterprise) and clarify how transnational resources and scripts
              are mobilised as claims to competence and recognition.
