1. Overall Evaluation
This paper presents an ambitious attempt to integrate augmented reality (AR) and haptic feedback into a mathematics game designed to enhance conceptual understanding and reduce mathematics anxiety among primary school students. While the study demonstrates innovative thinking and solid intentions, several weaknesses in methodological rigour
,
theoretical grounding, and reporting clarity undermine its contribution. The work is potentially publishable but requires substantial revision to meet the standards expected of peer-reviewed educational technology research.
2. Significance and Originality
Strengths:
The combination of AR and haptic feedback for primary mathematics learning is novel and relevant.
The study’s dual focus on cognitive (conceptual understanding) and affective (anxiety) outcomes aligns with current research priorities in technology-enhanced learning.
The development of a fully implemented prototype,
Kidar Playtime
, demonstrates commendable applied effort.
Limitations:
The manuscript’s claim to originality is overstated. Recent studies (e.g., Hatira & Sarac, 2024; Mutis & Oberemok, 2025; Moreno et al., 2023) have already examined multimodal AR or haptic interventions in early STEM contexts.
The conceptual contribution to embodied learning theory remains superficial. The authors invoke “embodied design” and “multisensory learning” without a clear theoretical articulation or framework for how these constructs inform the intervention’s pedagogical model.
There is little discussion of cultural or contextual factors (e.g., curriculum) that could situate the study more meaningfully within its educational environment.
Recommendation:
Reframe the originality claim as a
proof of concept
rather than a theoretical innovation. Deepen engagement with embodied cognition literature beyond surface citation.
3. Methodology
Strengths:
The use of a quasi-experimental design with control and experimental groups is appropriate for intervention-based research.
Ethical procedures and institutional permissions are clearly documented.
Mixed-method triangulation (tests, surveys, interviews, observation) strengthens internal validity.
Weaknesses and concerns:
Randomization and equivalence:
The “stratified random sampling” lacks procedural detail. It is unclear how the groups were balanced on prior performance or anxiety levels. Without random assignment, causality remains tentative.
Intervention fidelity:
Although teachers received two weeks of training, the paper does not describe monitoring of fidelity during implementation—an important issue in educational technology trials.
Measurement instruments:
The
Conceptual Understanding Test
(CUT) and
MARS-E
are reported with Cronbach’s alphas, but no evidence of construct validity or item examples are provided. The local adaptation of MARS-E lacks psychometric justification.
Statistical reporting:
Tables contain inconsistencies (e.g., missing standard deviations, co-efficient of variation, “nan” values in Table 2).
ANCOVA and MANOVA results are mentioned but not systematically presented with F, df, and p-values for all variables.
Effect sizes (Cohen’s
d
= 2.35) are unusually large and warrant scrutiny; such a large value suggests possible measurement artefacts or overfitting.
Qualitative data:
Thematic analysis is mentioned but minimally reported. Only a few student quotes are presented, with no analytic depth or link to the quantitative trends.
Recommendation:
Provide transparent statistical tables with full reporting standards (APA/CONSORT). Clarify sampling and validity procedures. Deepen qualitative interpretation beyond anecdotal quotations.
4. Results and Interpretation
Strengths:
The results clearly indicate improvement in both achievement and reduced anxiety in the experimental group.
The integration of quantitative and qualitative results shows a positive direction for mixed-methods synthesis.
Weaknesses:
The discussion section conflates results and interpretation, leading to redundancy and descriptive narrative rather than analytical insight.
Causal language (“the intervention caused significant improvement”) is too strong given the quasi-experimental design.
The statistical claims (e.g.,
p
= 0.001,
d
= 2.35) are not contextualized within practical classroom effect sizes.
The paper fails to critically engage with possible confounding variables such as novelty effect, teacher enthusiasm, or increased attention from researchers all of which may have contributed to gains.
The integration of AR and haptics is not analytically disentangled; we cannot tell which component (visual AR vs tactile feedback) accounted for the observed effects.
Recommendation:
Reorganize this section to separate descriptive results from interpretative claims. Include limitations of inference and consider mediation analysis or post-hoc exploration of differential effects.
5. Theoretical Framing and Discussion
Strengths:
References to embodied cognition, constructivism, and affective engagement are timely and relevant.
The study’s aim to bridge cognitive and affective dimensions aligns with emerging frameworks in affect-aware learning technologies.
Weaknesses:
The theoretical integration is weak. “Embodied learning” and “affect-aware technologies” are invoked rhetorically rather than analytically operationalized.
The discussion lacks mechanistic explanation, that is, how precisely does haptic interaction reduce anxiety? What cognitive or neuropsychological pathways are proposed?
The manuscript cites an overwhelming number of secondary sources but does not critically synthesize them. The literature review reads as an annotated catalogue rather than a conceptual argument.
The discussion does not connect findings to teacher practice, curriculum design, or pedagogical affordances of AR-haptic learning in the real world.
Recommendation:
Reframe the discussion to engage critically with embodied cognition models (e.g., Abrahamson et al., 2020) and cognitive-affective coupling frameworks (e.g., Pekrun, 2021). Avoid excessive citation without synthesis.
6. Presentation and Style
Strengths:
The manuscript is well-organized into clear subsections.
Ethical transparency and data sharing (Zenodo link) enhance reproducibility.
Weaknesses:
Numerous language and grammar issues (e.g., “math is gets associated,” “vertigo directly affects your numeracy”) compromise readability.
Figures and tables are not well-integrated into the narrative—many are “Supplementary,” which obscures key evidence.
Some references are inconsistent or incorrectly formatted, e.g., “Hatirassa & Saracss, 2024,” “Kaźmierczak et al., Skowroński, Kaźmierczak, & Kowalczyk-Page 8-572.”
The writing style occasionally drifts into advocacy rather than maintaining analytical objectivity.
Recommendation:
Substantially edit for clarity, concision, and grammatical precision. Reorganize tables and figures into the main text for readability. Apply a consistent referencing style.
7. Limitations and Future Directions
The authors acknowledge sample size and duration as limitations, but do not address technological limitations (e.g., hardware calibration, haptic glove precision) or pedagogical transferability (teacher training, classroom management). Future research should explore longitudinal retention effects, cost-benefit analyses, and comparative studies isolating AR versus haptic influences.
8. Recommendation
Decision:
Major Revision
The manuscript has
potential
due to its innovative combination of AR and haptic technologies, but it currently reads as an overextended proof-of-concept study lacking theoretical depth, statistical transparency, and linguistic refinement. With rigorous methodological clarification, tighter theoretical articulation, and improved writing, this paper could make a meaningful contribution to research on embodied and affect-aware learning technologies in mathematics education.
