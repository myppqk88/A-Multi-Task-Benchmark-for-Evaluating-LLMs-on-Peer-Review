    Tomasz Komornicki
    Polish Academy of Sciences, Warsaw, Poland

    The article has a very broad focus of the research. It is not entirely clear what was the main
    purpose of the interviews conducted. This affects their correct evaluation. The theses posed go
    beyond the capabilities of the methodology used. At the outset we learn that this goal was to
    uncover the way European integration explains the unequal treatment of Polish migrants in the
    ‘old’ Member States. Later in the text, more far-reaching theses and references to concepts
    appear. Poles are qualified as the most common group subject to various forms of modern
    slavery. The article does not provide evidence for such a thesis. On the contrary, some statements
    from interviews, as well as quotes from the literature, further indicate that the situation of Polish
    migrants is better than those from the global south. None of the quoted statements indicate that
    Poles as a group were treated particularly badly. Rather, they point to generally poor working
    conditions in certain sectors in the UK. Therefore, some of the theses seem over-interpreted.

    The article also fails to mention some important contexts related to emigration from Poland to the
    UK. These include non-economic factors that determine the decision to stay in the UK for longer
    than the initially planned few months. Often mentioned here is the quality of public services,
    higher than in Poland. Another important element is the fact that not all migrants registered in
    2004 were actual migrants at the time. Some had already been in the UK before, and Poland's
    accession to the EU only enabled them to legalize their stay and work.
    In the article, emigration from Poland to the UK is treated in isolation from general migration
    flows from Poland. Against this backdrop, the UK gained (thanks to the immediate opening of the
    labor market in 2004) relatively better-educated migrants from Poland (relative to, for example,
    Germany, where migration took place illegally and included groups that were poorly qualified and
    more likely to be mistreated by employers). The cited data regarding sectors employing Poles
    (after Okólski) does not justify the selection of individuals for the interviews conducted. These are
    general sectors with both bad and better-paying jobs.

    The exposition of the interview referring to the political situation in Poland is unclear. First of all,
    this is the situation before 2023 (right-wing rule), which should be highlighted somehow.
    Secondly, the singular example should be contrasted with the thousands of returns to Poland
    from the UK that followed Brexit.

    Explaining the intensity of migration from Poland to the UK by the development of low-cost
    airlines seems wrong. Rather, airlines have responded to the demand from these migrations. Bus
    transportation between Poland and the UK also played a key role in their first phase.
