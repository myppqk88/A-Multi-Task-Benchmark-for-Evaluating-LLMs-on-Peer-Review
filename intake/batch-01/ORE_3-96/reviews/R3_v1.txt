    demand for a sample of bonds issued under the EFSF/ESM scheme between 2014 and 2020. The
    study uses a proprietary data set of bond issue, investor demand and individual investor orders to
    examine these key research questions. The analysis is generally descriptive and largely focused on
    a univariate type setting where the researchers examine a single factor as predictors of the main
    research questions examined in the study. The main findings are interesting and largely
    consistent with prior literature but nonetheless the unique data set allows the authors to address
    some novel aspects in these research questions that are otherwise difficult to explore.

    My main concerns are around the explanation of relevant theory, the largely univariate nature of
    the analysis, and the ability to explain and replicate the findings as described in the paper.
        1. Univariate nature of analysis – much of the empirical analysis is descriptive and focuses on
           the role of a single explanatory variable. For example, when analysing investor demand
           (oversubscription) the study focuses only on tenor of the bonds issued. There is a notional
           split in the data set between benchmark bonds and taps, but otherwise there are a range of
           additional firm level factors (issuing firm size, bond issue size, current leverage, etc) that
           might also predict demand and be correlated with the tenor of the bond. The authors may
           argue that some of these metrics are picked up in the oversubscription variable of investor
           demand divided by bond issue size, but even here it would still be relevant to control for
           whether these variables have a residual effect on demand. Similar comments apply for
           analysis of bond pricing/new issue premium, and volatility of investor demand. Many of the
           academic studies cited in this paper can give directions in relevant controls in a multivariate
           setting.

        2. Theoretical motivation – the descriptive nature of the analysis is appropriate and common
           in practitioner focused work, but I would also expect to see some further discussion in the
           introduction to the paper and through the motivation for tests on what a priori expectations
           are in the study. There is some work in this area around investor demand, but I found this
           weak in the remainder of the paper and also in the motivation for empirical tests. As an
           example, when tenor and benchmark/taps status is used to segment oversubscription I
           don’t think the paper does enough to explain what the correct tenor buckets should be (two
           different splits are used in the paper) and why we should expect differences in the two main
           issue types.

        3. Data description – while appreciating that the data is described as proprietary, I would still
           expect to see a lot more work around sample construction, data filtering, treatment of
           different tranches of the same bond (if applicable), and general description of the data
           available and its source to aid replication. I found this aspect of the paper unnecessarily
           weak. The authors should look at published work that uses proprietary from government or
           investment bank data sets on bond issuance for good models in explaining data without
           giving away unpermitted information on the proprietary nature of the data set.
