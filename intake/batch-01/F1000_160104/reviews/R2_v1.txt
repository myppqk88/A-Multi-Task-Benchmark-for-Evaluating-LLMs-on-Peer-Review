1. Summary of the Study
The manuscript investigates how universities and sustainable business values influence sustainable entrepreneurial intentions among Indian higher education students. Using the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) as a theoretical foundation, the study applies Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to analyze survey responses from 234 students. The findings suggest that entrepreneurial attitudes, subjective norms, and sustainable business values positively impact sustainable entrepreneurial intentions, while perceived behavioral control and the university's role do not significantly contribute.
2. Strengths of the Study
Relevance and Contribution:
The study addresses a critical gap in sustainable entrepreneurship research within developing economies, particularly in India.
Theoretical Foundation:
The integration of TPB with sustainable business values extends existing models and contributes to entrepreneurship literature.
Methodological Rigor:
The use of PLS-SEM is appropriate for analyzing complex relationships, and the study demonstrates clear justification for its methodological choices.
Practical Implications:
The research offers valuable insights for universities, policymakers, and educators on fostering sustainable entrepreneurial mindsets.
3. Areas for Improvement
Conceptual and Theoretical Considerations
The rationale for integrating "University Role" within the TPB framework needs further justification and stronger theoretical grounding.
The study lacks a thorough discussion of the theoretical positioning of sustainable business values within TPB.
Explicit hypothesis statements should be presented earlier in the manuscript for better clarity.
Methodological Refinements
Sampling and Generalizability:
The study focuses on students from Karnataka, India. Including participants from other regions would enhance external validity.
Measurement of Constructs:
The operationalization of key constructs, particularly sustainable business values and university roles, requires further refinement.
Common Method Bias:
While Harman’s single-factor test was conducted, additional tests (e.g., marker variable technique) would further validate the results.
Control Variables:
Including demographic factors like gender or family business background could add robustness to the analysis.
Statistical Analysis and Reporting
Structural Model Results:
Path coefficients,
t-values, and p-values
should be explicitly stated in the results section, which does not exist and authors should add it;
model fit indices
(e.g., SRMR, NFI, RMSEA, TLI, GFI …) are not currently presented and they should be in the manuscript.
Effect Sizes and Predictive Power:
Reporting f² effect sizes and R² values for each path would provide a clearer picture of practical significance.
Mediation and Moderation Analysis:
Investigating potential mediating or moderating effects (e.g., university role as a mediator) could strengthen findings.
Be clearer and more concise:
“The removed latent variables are PBC2=0.691,SV3=0.604,UR1=0.657,SN5=0.094,SN6=0.002, SN7=0.311,SN8=0.244,SN9=0.058” (page 10). These are
observable variables (items), not latent variables
. Additionally, removing
five out of nine items
from the subjective norms scale raises concerns—are you still effectively measuring subjective norms?
4. Interpretation and Discussion
The unexpected negative correlations (e.g., university role and perceived behavioral control) need deeper exploration, possibly considering unmeasured contextual factors.
The discussion section should critically evaluate limitations beyond acknowledging them, including cultural influences and potential biases.
Findings should be explicitly linked to sustainable development goals (SDGs) for broader theoretical and practical relevance.
5. Presentation and Writing Style
Grammar and Clarity:
While generally well-written, the manuscript contains some complex sentences that could be simplified for better readability.
Consistency in Terminology:
Terms like "sustainable entrepreneurship" and "entrepreneurial intention" should be clearly defined and used consistently.
Figures and Tables:
Some tables, particularly measurement model assessments, require better formatting and additional explanation.
6. Ethical Considerations
The manuscript states ethical approval was obtained, but clarification on whether informed consent was explicitly obtained from participants is needed.
7. Conclusion and Recommendation
This study makes an important contribution to sustainable entrepreneurship research in developing economies. However, improvements in theoretical justification, methodological rigor, and statistical reporting are necessary for publication.
Recommendation: Major Revisions Required
Strengthen theoretical justifications and conceptual clarity.
Enhance methodological robustness by refining construct measurements and reporting additional model fit indices.
Clearly report structural model results, including path coefficients and t-values, and include a dedicated results section for this analysis.
Expand the discussion of non-significant results and their implications.
Improve clarity in writing, consistency in terminology, and formatting of figures/tables.
By addressing these issues, the manuscript can significantly contribute to the field of sustainable entrepreneurship and higher education research.
