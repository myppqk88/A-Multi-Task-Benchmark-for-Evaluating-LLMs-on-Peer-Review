The article by Monsalve-Silva, Bermudez-Aponte, and Vera-Monroy presents a study on the implementation of didactic strategies aimed at students at risk of academic failure in general chemistry at Universidad de La Sabana. Two interventions were tested: a reinforcement course and a gamified kit for unit conversions. The mixed-methods quasi-experimental design, using 411 control students (2020–2023) and 60 experimental students (2023–2024), revealed improvements in academic performance, especially among students with low Saber 11 scores, as well as positive student perceptions. The topic is relevant, the interventions are innovative, and the paper provides valuable empirical evidence for assurance of learning processes in STEM higher education.
Regarding the study design, it is overall appropriate and technically sound, but only partly satisfactory. The quasi-experimental mixed-methods approach is suitable for the problem, and the instruments used are valid. However, the lack of precise participant numbers in each intervention group (reinforcement course, gamified kit, both) weakens the technical rigor and complicates interpretation of results. The description of methods and analyses is also only partly sufficient to allow full replication. While the authors describe the instruments (VAK, CHAEA, MAI) and statistical tests (Mann-Whitney, Spearman, Kolmogorov-Smirnov), replication would require additional detail. In particular, the gamified kit is shown only through illustrations, not full materials; the number of participants per subgroup is not reported; and the interpretation of p-values is not explained in sufficient depth.
The statistical analysis and its interpretation are likewise only partly appropriate. The choice of non-parametric tests is correct given the distribution of the data, but interpretation is limited. Effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d, r) are not reported, and the significance of p-values is not clearly explained. In Table 9, none of the p-values reach conventional significance, yet the text suggests positive effects. The combined effect of both strategies is under-discussed, although the data indicate potentially interesting interactions.
In contrast, all underlying source data have been made openly available on Zenodo under a CC-BY license, ensuring reproducibility. This is a strong aspect of the study and fully meets the requirements for data accessibility. As for the conclusions, they are supported by the results but only with some reservations. The claim that the reinforcement course significantly improves outcomes is well substantiated, and the positive effect of the gamified kit is reasonably supported. However, broader generalizations—such as the assertion that these strategies can be scaled across all subjects—go beyond the presented data and should be phrased more cautiously.
Additional issues should also be addressed. The grading scale is not explained; international readers may be confused unless it is stated clearly that in Colombia grades range from 0–5, with 5 as the highest and 3.0 as the minimum passing threshold. Tables would benefit from indicating the number of participants (N) per subgroup, and figure captions could provide more informative notes. More international references on remediation and gamification in higher education would help situate the study in a global context. Finally, the perspectives of instructors are missing; only student perceptions are reported, yet teacher views on feasibility and workload are critical for evaluating implementation.
In summary, the study is scientifically valid, well-motivated, and presents meaningful findings. Nevertheless, to improve transparency, replicability, and international readability, the authors should provide subgroup sample sizes, clarify the grading scale, report effect sizes, and interpret p-values with more rigor. For these reasons, my recommendation is: Approved with reservations.
