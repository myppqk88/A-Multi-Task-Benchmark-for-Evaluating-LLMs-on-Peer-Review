       This research paper endeavors to present an all-encompassing examination of the plight of

    "displaced Syrians" within the borders of Lebanon. This undertaking is accomplished through an
    extensive fieldwork initiative, conducted in both the Bekaa Valley and the city of Saida. The
    fieldwork is an amalgamation of various qualitative and quantitative research methodologies,
    including in-depth interviews, surveys, and ethnographic observations. The authors aptly argue
    that an array of stressors, notably the relentless surge in inflation due to an economic crisis, the
    omnipresent specter of the Covid-19 pandemic, and the devastating impact of the Beirut port
    disasters on Lebanese society, have grievously exacerbated the already precarious living
    conditions of the Syrian population. This confluence of challenges has not only magnified the
    Syrians' predicaments but also convoluted the issues they confront.

    In the absence of a formal domestic framework that could potentially offer protection and
    ameliorate the vulnerability of displaced Syrians, the reliance on informal community support and
    existing formal assistance mechanisms proves insufficient to shield them from perilous
    circumstances, abject poverty, and marginalization. Regrettably, these hardships have driven a
    substantial majority of Syrians to contemplate leaving the host country, albeit with limited
    prospects of success.

    In light of these pressing issues, the authors judiciously proffer a set of policy recommendations.
    These include advocating for the postponement of forced repatriation, enhancing the capacity of
    international humanitarian institutions to facilitate displaced individuals' access to vital
    information, adjusting the scope and magnitude of financial aid packages in accordance with the
    precipitous currency devaluation, and focusing concerted efforts on the improvement of health
    and education programs.
    This research paper is undeniably a well-crafted, logically structured, and cohesive exploration
    that vividly portrays the current predicament of Syrian refugees amid the multiple crises afflicting
    Lebanese society. It also proffers a concrete set of policy prescriptions. However, the wealth of
    research material upon which this paper is founded could have been more effectively leveraged to
    delve into the following areas:
         1. While acknowledging the existence of prior research on displaced Syrians in Lebanon, such
            as the works of Fakhoury, Janmyr, and Romola, the authors could have further elaborated
            on how their current findings either corroborate or challenge the conclusions of these
            scholars. This comparative analysis would have enriched the discourse.

        2. The authors might have delved deeper into the data derived from their quantitative and
           qualitative interviews to expound upon the stark disparities in aspirations and priorities
           between displaced Syrians in Bekaa and Saida. By shedding light on the underlying
           determinants of these differences, the paper could have offered insights into how the socio-
           economic circumstances and living conditions of refugees collectively shape their hopes,
           expectations, and, consequently, their decisions regarding relocation.

        3. While acknowledging that the influx of Syrians has altered the ethnic and sectarian
           composition of Lebanese cities and towns, exacerbating social tensions and fostering racial
           prejudices, the research paper, in its current form, falls short in adequately addressing how
           displaced Syrians experience these tensions. Additionally, there is room for a more
           comprehensive exploration of how the degree and nature of negative attitudes within the
           local population have evolved in tandem with Lebanon's multifaceted crises and how these
           experiences influence the dire conditions faced by the displaced population. Given that the
           authors mention their interviews aimed to gauge the strength of Syrians' ties to local

          communities, readers would benefit from an analysis of how intercommunal relations
          between the local Lebanese community and displaced Syrians evolve over time.

        4. Numerous studies conducted in Turkey on the conditions of Syrian asylum seekers
           document similar challenges to those experienced by Syrians in Lebanon. Incorporating
           insights from this extensive body of research in Turkey, juxtaposed with the Lebanese
           context, could provide valuable comparative analysis, highlighting the ways in which
           Lebanon's unique political, legal, and social conditions exert a distinct influence on the
           Syrian population within its borders.

        5. In light of the prevailing structural constraints, notably the absence of a robust domestic
           legal framework designed to safeguard the rights and well-being of Syrians residing in
           Lebanon, it becomes increasingly questionable whether the ostensibly modest policy
           recommendations put forth by the researchers can yield any discernible and positive impact
           on the dire circumstances faced by the Syrian population. An astute examination of the
           limitations inherent in these short-term and relatively small-scale policy proposals could
           have imparted a greater degree of nuance and sophistication to the authors' discussion. By
           addressing this crucial concern, the paper could have embraced a more comprehensive
           perspective, acknowledging the formidable hurdles posed by the overarching legal and
           institutional deficiencies within Lebanon. In doing so, it would have offered readers a more
           well-rounded understanding of the complexities inherent in effecting change in the lives of
           displaced Syrians within such a challenging and constrained environment.
