       Reviewer Report
       Summary of the Article
       This study presents a timely and well-structured stakeholder analysis of key EU industrial and
       environmental policies, including the Green Deal Industrial Plan (GDIP), the Net Zero Industry Act
       (NZIA), the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), and the 2023 Battery Regulation. By capturing
       stakeholder perceptions through interactive sessions, the paper provides an informative snapshot
       of how various actors interpret, align with, or challenge the ambitions of the EU’s clean tech
       transition. In particular, the analysis around the European Battery Passport and its connection to
       broader regulatory frameworks is both insightful and relevant. The work offers a valuable
       contribution by highlighting the sentiment landscape among key stakeholders and assessing how
       this influences the implementation and perceived coherence of EU policies.
       Evaluation of Research Design and Scientific Rigor
       The study design is appropriate for capturing qualitative and semi-quantitative perceptions across
       sectors. The interactive approach and use of structured questions is a good method for
       exploratory policy research. However, some aspects of methodology and data treatment require
       further clarification to ensure reproducibility and robustness:
           ○  Data Cleaning: The authors note that inconsistencies (e.g., empty fields) were removed, but
              it is unclear how biases were addressed during this cleaning process. A more explicit
              discussion is needed on how the authors ensured that responses were not filtered in a way
              that skewed the final dataset. What measures were taken to preserve the objectivity and
              diversity of opinions while resolving inconsistencies?
           ○  Statistical Analysis: While the work relies on qualitative and descriptive statistics,
              incorporating even basic inferential statistics or comparative analysis with previous datasets
              (if available) would strengthen the credibility of the results and offer greater predictive
              value for future policymaking.
           ○  Participant Insights: Although anonymity was maintained, including a selection of

    Engagement with the Literature
    The article engages reasonably well with current policy documentation and European Commission
    sources, but several areas could benefit from deeper literature integration:
        ○   The claim that road transport accounts for approximately 70% of transport emissions
            should be supported by a verifiable and up-to-date source, as numbers vary across datasets
            and are sensitive to temporal and geographic boundaries.
        ○   While the paper explores EU-level policy coherence, it does not sufficiently discuss the
            global geopolitical context. For instance, the dominant role of China in critical battery
            materials (e.g., lithium, cobalt, rare earths) is a central factor affecting Europe's strategic
            autonomy. The absence of this topic is a notable gap. Adding references on China’s market
            control and the implications for EU resilience would add critical nuance.
    Discussion of Results
    The discussion effectively connects survey findings with EU policy objectives, but there are several
    areas where deeper reflection or clarification is warranted:
        ○   Figure 4 - Skills and Workforce: Workforce training is certainly a recognized priority, but
            the analysis would benefit from emphasizing what type of training is most urgent. For
            instance, Europe could benefit significantly from developing more exchange and internship
            programs with non-EU countries that already have advanced mining and refining
            capabilities. Such partnerships would provide real-world exposure to processing
            technologies that are currently underrepresented in the EU.
        ○   Figure 5 - Research and Innovation: The role of R&D is well emphasized, but the authors
            should comment on the unequal R&D spending landscape across the EU. In countries
            where public R&D spending is below 1-2% of GDP, innovation stagnates, which directly
            impacts national contributions to the broader EU strategy. The bipolarity in funding is a
            barrier to EU-wide innovation, particularly in clean tech.
        ○   Figure 8 - Achievability of Targets: The difficulty of meeting targets is well acknowledged,
            but this is not necessarily a flaw. High-level targets should reflect ambition, especially for a
            region aspiring to lead in sustainable technologies. What matters is the presence of clear,
            measurable KPIs and robust implementation strategies under conditions of uncertainty.
        ○   Figure 9 - Evolving Skills Needs: The point about workforce skills is critical, but the static
            framing may be misleading. In reality, the clean tech workforce will need to continuously
            adapt, particularly as AI, automation, and new digital tools become integral to the battery
            and energy industries. This is not a fixed challenge but an evolving one.
        ○   Figure 12 - Recycling and Circularity: The emphasis on recycling as a solution needs
            nuance. While essential, important reminder is that recycling should serve as a complement
            to primary sourcing, not a replacement. Design for longevity and repairability should take
            precedence. Furthermore, efficient upstream sorting (e.g., for direct recycling pathways)
            and decreasing reliance on high-temperature pyrometallurgy in favor of selective
            hydrometallurgical routes are emerging game changers that deserve mention.
    European Battery Regulation Commentary
    The inclusion of the 2023 Battery Regulation is highly relevant. One key strength of this regulation
    is its potential to standardize safety and traceability across diverse battery chemistries and
    formats. This not only enhances consumer safety, especially for inherently volatile chemistries like
    lithium cobalt oxide (LCO), but also facilitates better design choices and sourcing transparency.
    The regulation also supports innovation in battery design by encouraging diverse chemistries and
    manufacturers, which can help reduce dependence on a few dominant players. Furthermore, the

    Minor Suggestions
        ○   Throughout the manuscript, be consistent in chemical nomenclature. For instance, avoid
            using both “Li” and “lithium” interchangeably in the same context. “Li” is a chemical symbol,
            not an acronym.
        ○   Consider enhancing the visual analysis of figures by integrating brief cross-comparisons
            with previous public consultations or EU strategy roadmaps where relevant.
        ○   Slight restructuring of the introduction could help by more clearly stating the research
            question upfront.
    Conclusion
    This paper offers a strong foundation and covers a highly relevant topic with commendable
    structure and depth. Before indexing, the authors should address key methodological gaps
    (particularly around data treatment and statistical interpretation), add global context regarding
    critical raw material geopolitics, and provide deeper analytical insights in selected result sections.
    Enhancing these areas will not only make the paper more scientifically sound but also improve its
    utility for policymakers, industry professionals, and researchers navigating Europe’s green
    industrial transition.
