       The article frames the implementation of CBAM as a trilemma involving three competing
       objectives:
           1. Level Playing Field
           2. Global Climate Justice
           3. De Facto Carbon Pricing Club
       The article reviews three primary design approaches to navigate this trilemma: a "dual-track"
       approach (using foreign aid to offset CBAM impacts), exemptions (differentiated pricing for
       vulnerable countries), and revenue recycling (returning CBAM proceeds to trading partners).

       The article is well-written and researched, as well as scientifically sound and policy-relevant.
       However, several points should be addressed.

       1. Objectives of the EU vs. Objectives of CBAM
       The article attributes the "three conflicting policy objectives" to the CBAM itself. Technically, the
       CBAM itself has a singular objective: addressing the risk of carbon leakage to maintain a level
       playing field. Objectives such as "climate justice" or "establishing a climate club" are broader EU
       policy goals, not inherent goals of the CBAM.

       To address this, the title and abstract should be revised to refer to the three conflicting policy
       goals of the EU in its implementation of CBAM, rather than the goals of the CBAM.

    2. Credit-Based Approaches to Solving the Trilemma
    The review of approaches should address a fourth option suggested in the literature whereby
    exporters to the EU from developing countries can retire carbon credits (e.g., Article 6 credits) up
    to the value of their CBAM bills. This would involve the EU allowing such retirements to qualify as a
    "carbon price effectively paid". See Sandler & Schrag (2025) - this approach should be added to the
    comparative analysis in Section 4 to provide a comprehensive review of the current policy
    landscape.

    3. Broadening "Climate Justice"
    While the article's addressing of "climate justice" is clear, it may be framed too narrowly, as the
    EU's broader goal is the political acceptability and trade continuity for developing countries. The
    primary concern for many global south partners is not just abstract justice, but the practical ability
    to continue trading with the EU without facing prohibitive barriers that could derail their economic
    development. To address this, the author could rephrase the "justice" objective as a "trade and
    political acceptability" objective to make the trilemma more robust for policy analysis.
