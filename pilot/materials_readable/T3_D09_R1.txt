Validity of the findings
I had a few concerns about the paper.
First, the definition of isolation and quarantine is ambiguous. This has a specific meaning in disease management contexts. Isolation usually refers to situations where people who are sick are kept apart from other people, whereas quarantine usually refers to people who are kept apart from others because they may have been exposed, i.e., they may eventually show signs of the disease. The definitions here appear to mix up people who: (a) may be fearful and who are isolating because they may feel that they are vulnerable to COVID; (b) have just had a test and are told to stay home or have been put into self-isolation, but not necessarily in quarantine. These people might be getting police checks, but they are not really in quarantine; (c) People in actual quarantine, e.g., hotels, in hospital or other places. Lockdown could be Stage 4 or Stage 2. Big difference. For these reasons, I have concerns about the grouping that forms the basis of the analyses.
I also wondered- if you were in hotel quarantine – would you get to do as much gaming as when you were at home?
Second, the PGSI is described as classifying people as moderate risk based on scores of 5-7, but it’s actually 3-7? Low risk = scores of 1-2. Is the scoring in the study in accordance with the regular scoring?
Third, I found the hypotheses a bit odd. I was surprised that the main hypothesis wasn’t about increases in gaming or the relationship between gaming and loot box use. Problem gambling is most likely related to loot box use because people who enjoy gambling tend to gravitate towards gambling content in games. However, the main thrust of this paper is that the stress of COVID drives people towards greater gaming (as per the WHO recommendation- yes, ironic given the IGD classification, but sadly it doesn’t surprise me after their performance earlier this year!).  In other words, wouldn’t one expect to see people’s gaming and loot box spending increase when they are really locked down and on mandatory stay-at-home orders? But it’s not so obvious that the cross over from gambling to gaming would occur.
Fourth, the study is all about moderation, but they haven’t really tested for moderation. It’s based on correlation difference tests. That’s OK, but it’s unusual. Maybe avoid referring to moderation and have a hypothesis that specifies that the simple first order effects will be different for the two groups.
Fifth, I didn’t feel that the paper provides enough detail about the demographic composition, gaming habits of the two groups; country etc..

==================== 审稿报告 R2 ====================

Basic reporting
I found the manuscript to be basically well written and well referenced.
As a comment on the introduction, I felt the authors tried to contextualize their study with regard to the controversy around the Internet Gaming Disorder diagnosis. E.g. line 58 “despite having codified gaming disorder (in the ICD-11)”, line 61 “significant disagreement”, and in particular, lines 64-67 that they are examining the effects of the pandemic on excessive gameplay “given the scientific debate about the potential for excessive video gameplay to have maladaptive consequences”. This needs to be handled quite carefully. Prima facie, it’s not clear to me why the effects of the pandemic on video gaming says anything about the IGD controversy (unless they are relying heavily on COVID-related contamination fears causing an increase in gaming, see comment in Validity). Second, the authors should pay careful attention to exact wording here, e.g. on line 65, for many academics, video gaming is only considered excessive if it has maladaptive consequences, so this line as worded is a tautology.
The study is pre-registered and the pre-reg hypotheses are presented on lines 94-123. I found it confusing that the results does not adhere to the same order of hypotheses. Either section could be re-organized but it would help the reader if they were aligned or at least cross-referenced.
I appreciate the dataset is open, but there is little reporting of descriptive stats other than in Figure 1. E.g. for interpreting the K-10 difference as ‘negligible’ (line 200 and 221) it would be great to see the means / SDs.
The authors present Fig 1 for the effects of self-isolation by PGSI group status. This analysis does not show a significant interaction. They do not show the corresponding figure for the PGSI continuous analysis that yields the significant interaction effect. One issue here is about the basic reporting, that is makes sense to graph the more interesting effect (or show both). There are related issues here that are perhaps more relevant to design and validity. First, in the pre-reg hypotheses, H3 is really the same hypothesis as H1, just tested with a categorical rather than continuous model. Likewise, H4 is the categorical version of H2. I'm not sure what to recommend here because these were pre-registered; but in both cases it is the same hypothesis tested in two ways. Second, the authors see an interaction (difference in correlation coefficients) in the continuous model (H2) but not the categorical model (H4). There is a speculative interpretation on line 243 about differences in the number of problem gamblers rather than how much they are spending, but the basic issue is that these 'discrepant' analyses are being conducted on the same data. It is more likely that this reflects either the thresholding of the PGSI (e.g. Currie et al thresholds vs the original Ferris & Wynne thresholds) or the relative statistical power of the two models. Inherently, a continuous model makes better use of the full data than any model that relies on binning scores together.
For PeerJ, the consent form should be uploaded with the Supp; the authors have confirmed this but I did not see it.
The raw data are available via an OSF link but there is no readme file on the variable annotations. With extensive reference to their pre-reg materials, I was able to find the geographical variable (see my comments on Design), but I would encourage the authors to prepare a document on the variable definitions.
Experimental design
This manuscript reports an online survey on Prolific of gaming behaviour during the early stage of the COVID pandemic. The focus on loot boxes (both expenditure and the Risky Loot Box index) and excessive video gaming provides important data for dissemination, and the design includes problem gambling (PGSI), distress (K-10) and contamination fears (Padua) as further covariates. The sample size is fairly large (n=1144) from US, New Zealand and Australia. A key finding is that the relationship between loot box spending and problem gambling is stronger in the group who describe themselves as self-isolating. This has implications for the public health discussion around the pandemic response, as well as wider implications for effect sizes in post-COVID gaming /gambling research.
I did have some concerns about the independent variable that forms the basis for the ‘natural experiment’ here. Participants were categorised as self-isolating or quarantining, versus not. A parallel model of lockdown (yes/no) was abandoned as too few participants endorsed being not in lockdown. Some basic points: 1) could the authors clearly state the date(s) on which the survey was run, perhaps in relation to important pandemic dates (e.g. declared state of emergency) in those jurisdictions, 2) can the authors add descriptives split by the 3 jurisdictions? 3) in the supp, can the authors state the number of participants who were in lockdown?
Currently, the analysis plan simply collapses by region. If for example NZ had a more restrictive ‘lockdown’ than the US, is it possible that their independent variable (self-isolation Yes/No) simply reflects a cross-jurisdictional effect? Second, the analysis plan considers the i.v. as ‘veridical’. Early in the pandemic there seemed to be public confusion about the terms ‘lockdown’ and ‘self-isolating’ (vs social distancing) (and again, the level of confusion may have varied by jurisdiction). The pre-reg materials do not indicate that definitions were provided to participants, which would be a limitation. Moreover, as 95% of participants were in lockdown, to what extent is ‘self-isolation’ within lockdown a subjective or objective status? (again I'm aware there are now clear jurisdictional definitions about these terms, but my point is whether the participants were thinking in these terms or not). This is quite important because subjective self-isolation (or conversely a subjective rejection of the local public health guidance) could be related to contamination fears in particular.
Other
Prolific survey: 54 participants did not play video games. How were the survey advertised on Prolific. As a prevalence rate, this seems high but it may reflect the general characteristics of Prolific participants. Please also add Age data to the Methods.
Line 170: why was the loot box outlier cut-off of 3.29 SD used?  The authors state that this was a pre-registered threshold but it seems an unusual number.
Validity of the findings
The authors included measures of distress and contamination fears. The authors allude in several places to the debate about whether gaming causes ‘psychopathology’ or is used to copy with negative states, and their distress and contamination measures appear to be interpreted as speaking to this e.g. lines 42 and 44 in abstract. This interpretation seems to warrant some discussion as to whether these constructs are state or trait related. The K-10 is past month, so presumably state, but the Padua contamination scale could be viewed as a stable trait. The questionnaire did not appear to ask whether the items had changed at all in relation the pandemic to the pandemic. Similarly in discussion line 223 “isolation requirements have resulted in increased excessive gameplay or expenditure” – the verb “increased” here implies some longitudinal component or at least a retrospective judgment as to whether one’s activities have changed (i.e. under COVID). Their design does not speak to this.
Line 207 “small but important contributor” – how do Ferguson’s criteria establish that a small effect size is important?
Additional comments
no further comments

This study was funded by a grant investigating loot boxes and in-game monetisation, and the research team are particularly interested in loot box reward systems. Thus, our primary hypothesis focused on this issue. However, we also predicted that we would observe an overall increase in excessive gaming, an increase in loot box spending, an increase in contamination concern and an increase in psychological distress. p. 4 I struggled with the logic that set up the study. If lots of people start increasing their gambling why would you expect relationships to get stronger? Couldn’t you also get floor effects and less variability as the majority of gamers spend more on gaming?

We thank the reviewer for this comment and for the opportunity to elaborate and clarify our argument. We did not expect lots of people to increase their gambling or loot box purchasing – we expected that more people would be playing games during the pandemic, exposing them to opportunities to purchase loot boxes. Given the observed relationship where video gamers with higher problem gambling symptoms more often spend money on loot boxes (Garea et al., 2020), we therefore expected the increase in video game play and increased exposure to loot boxes would lead to increased purchasing behaviour by problem gamblers. We now clarify this on pages 5-6 of the manuscript.

“The decrease in alternative social activities, and the increased need for online social activities may have the potential to exacerbate this relationship. Spending on loot boxes therefore appears to be more common among video game players with problem gambling symptoms (Garea et al., 2020).  The apparent increase in the number of people engaging with video games during the pandemic may result in increased exposure to, and hence opportunities for the purchasing of, loot boxes. This has prompted concerns about increased spending on loot boxes for certain user groups (Department for Digital, Cultural, Media & Sport., 2020; Harris, 2020). One study suggests that reducing exposure to loot boxes by removing them from a game appears to reduce spending only among those with higher problem gambling symptoms (Zendle, 2019). Thus it appears that spending by problem gamblers may be specifically impacted by the presence of loot boxes in video games while playing. This led us to predict that the social isolation of the pandemic might result in an increase in either overall spending on loot boxes, or, a specific increase in spending on loot boxes for problem gamblers. Thus, here we examined whether the established association between problem gambling symptomology was stronger for isolated gamers, compared to their non-isolated peers. As we thought that people who were more concerned about contamination would be more anxious and or more socially isolated during the pandemic than people who had lower contamination concern, we expected that people with higher contamination concern may have higher spending on loot boxes. Thus, we also examined whether contamination concerns were significantly associated with spending on loot boxes.”

“We pre-registered comparisons between those who reported that they were self-isolating (eliminating contact with other people) or quarantined (under mandated self-isolation) and those who were not. We acknowledge that this grouping may overlook important differences in contexts between different forms of isolation (e.g., for participants anxiously awaiting test results), however two important points bear mentioning. First, the study was conducted early in the pandemic outbreaks in these countries, and terminology, especially among the general public, was relatively new, with exact restriction limitations still under development (Cameron, 2020) and some ambiguity in the  Federal response in Australia and the US (Morrison, 2020a; Schuchat, 2020). We expected this would add noise to participants’ self-reported isolation status, resulting in our decision to group all isolated participants together. Second, the self-isolation questions allowed participants to report whether they were in self-isolation or quarantine to their best understanding of these terms. This may result in some measurement error in reporting. However, we expected that variations in the accessibility of participants’ usual social activities would be the primary driver of increased digital media exposure. Thus, we believe our approach offers a valid way to investigate the effects of increased perceived isolation. A more fine-grained study investigating whether effects are similar across different kinds of isolation using objective measures of such isolation would no-doubt be worthwhile. Nonetheless, our results provide early evidence that participants’ perceived isolation status was associated with changes in some gaming-gambling relationships.
We also pre-registered comparisons between those who were under lockdown orders (specified to participants as “including mandated social distancing, business closures, curfews, stay home orders or other movement restrictions”) in cities where movement and gathering restrictions were in place) and those who were not. However, very few participants were not in lockdown (n = 69, ~6% of our total data). Thus, estimates for non-lockdown participants are underpowered and, therefore, unreliable. Rather than report these unreliable estimates in the manuscript, we have included the results for these pre-registered comparisons between participants in lockdown and participants not in lockdown in the online supplementary materials with appropriate caveats. At the time of data collection, Aoteroa New Zealand had implemented a Nationwide lockdown which had been in effect for approximately 14 days. Federal lockdown responses had not been introduced in Australia, or the US, creating divergent state-based responses (Morrison, 2020a; Schuchat, 2020). We acknowledge that an examination of different stringencies of lockdown (e.g., Stage 2 versus Stage 4 lockdowns) would constitute a worthwhile follow-up study.” (pages 10-11).
To the reviewer’s point about whether someone in hotel quarantine would have access to video games, two points bear mentioning. First, during our data collection, hotel quarantine was not in widespread usage in Australia, NZ, or the US (e.g., Ministry of Health – Manatū Hauora, 2020; New South Wales Government, 2020; Oliver, 2020), with many participants being quarantined at home. Second, access to video games is very widespread, including on laptops, phones, mobile tablets, transportable game machines like the Nintendo Switch etc. Thus, there is little reason to believe that many participants did not have access to some form of gaming in quarantine whether in hotel or not. Our data do not seem to reflect that participants had difficulty accessing video games during the data collection period.

money (in their country’s currency) they had spent on loot boxes in the past month. We converted all 
167
values into US dollars using the listed currency conversion rates of the day using Google’s currency 
168
conversion. We converted all currencies into US dollars on April 21, 2020 using the following exchange 
169
rates: $USD = 0.63*AUD; $USD = 0.60*$NZD. In accordance with our pre-registration we excluded 9 
170
participants (0.8% of total data) who spent more than 3.29 SDs greater than the mean ($6.08, SD = 
171
$42.50) as outliers from analyses of loot box spending. This resulted in the exclusion of any participant 
172
who spent more than $133.58 USD on loot boxes in the past month. Including these participants did not 
173
qualitatively alter the results. 
174
Contamination Concern. We measured participants’ contamination concern with the Revised 
175
Padua-Inventory 10 item contamination subscale (Burns et al. 1996). The Padua-Inventory 10 is a 
176
psychiatric scale assessing obsessive compulsive symptomology. The contamination subscale assesses 
177
the degree of disturbance a range of common activities which might result in contamination causes 
178
participants (0, not at all – 4, very much). Example items include “I find it difficult to touch an object 
179
when I know it has been touched by strangers or by certain people” and “I feel my hands are dirty when 
180
I touch money.”
181
Procedure
182
Participants completed the Kessler-10 Psychological Distress Scale (K-10; Slade et al. 2011), 
183
Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI; Holtgraves 2009; Currie et al. 2013), Internet Gaming Disorder 
184
Checklist (IGDC; Przybylski et al. 2017), a measure of risky engagement with loot boxes (Risky Loot Box 
185
Index; RLI; Brooks & Clark 2019), and reported money spent on loot boxes in the past month. Measures 
186
and sources are detailed in the online supplementary materials. Participants reported contamination 
187
concern on the Revised Padua Inventory-10 (Burns et al. 1996), and their isolation status: self-isolation 
188
(n = 250), quarantine (n = 197), or neither (n = 697). This created a natural-groups experiment to 
189
examine whether self-isolation or quarantine affected outcomes. 
190
We pre-registered comparisons between two separate dichotomised groups – those who were 
191
self-isolating (eliminating contact with other people) or quarantined (under mandated self-isolation) and 
192
those who were not, and those who were under lockdown orders (in cities where movement and 
193
gathering restrictions were in place) and those who were not. However, very few participants were not 
194
in lockdown (n = 69, ~6% of our total data). Thus, estimates for non-lockdown participants are 
195
underpowered and, therefore, likely unreliable. Rather than report these potentially unreliable 
196
estimates here, we have included the results for these pre-registered comparisons between participants 
197
in lockdown and participants not in lockdown in the online supplementary materials.
198
Results
199
Psychological distress was higher among people in self-isolation or quarantine than those not, 
200
t(1,142) = 2.565, p = .010, d = 0.160 (a negligible difference). There was no evidence of an association 
201
between self-isolation or quarantine and excessive gaming (p = .330), loot box spending (p = .678), or 
202
risky loot box use (p = .984). 
203
Irrespective of isolation status, contamination concern was moderately associated with distress, 
204
r = .319, p < .001, and weakly associated with loot box spending, IGDC, and RLI (see Table 1). Though 
205
weak, relationships between contamination concern and IGDC and RLI both exceeded criteria for the 
206
smallest effects likely to bear clinical relevance (Ferguson 2009), suggesting contamination concern may 
207
be a small but important contributor to excessive engagement with both gaming and monetized in-
208
game reward mechanisms. We compared correlation strengths using Fischer’s Z-tests (Weaver & 
209
Wuensch 2013). Relationships were generally similar for people in self-isolation or quarantine though 
210
the association between RLI and contamination concern was significantly stronger for self-isolated or 
211
quarantined than not (Table 1). 
212
Problem gambling symptomology was associated with higher loot box spending, rs = .279, p < 
213
.001; with a stronger association for participants who were self-isolated or quarantined, rs = .361, p < 
214
.001, than those who were not, rs = .221, p < .001, Z = 2.523, p = .011. When classified into risk groups, 
215
problem gamblers spent more than lower risk gamblers (Figure 1), however, self-isolation or quarantine 
216
did not moderate this relationship. Finally, loot box spending was weakly associated with psychological 
217
distress, rs = .103, p < .001.
218
Discussion
219
We investigated whether self-isolation or quarantine, or contamination concerns were 
220
associated with increased excessive gaming, loot box spending or psychological distress. Compared to 
221
those not self-isolated or quarantined, participants in self-isolation or quarantine showed negligible 
222
increases in psychological distress, but not loot box spending, loot box engagement, or excessive 
223
gaming. It does not appear that isolation requirements have resulted in increased excessive gameplay or 
224
expenditure on loot boxes generally. 
225
However, higher contamination concern was associated with small-to-moderately higher 
226
excessive gaming and risky loot box engagement scores, and small increases in loot box spending. These 
227
effects were generally similar for those self-isolating or in quarantine and those not, with the exception 
228
that the relationship between risky loot box use and contamination concern was stronger for people 
229
self-isolating or in quarantine than those not. People may be engaging with video games to manage 
230
their contamination concerns about the Covid-19 pandemic.
231
The established relationship between problem gambling symptomology and loot box spending 
232
[9] was stronger for those in self-isolation or quarantine than those not, demonstrating closer 
233
agreement between problem gambling symptom scores and loot box spending. Indeed the relationship 
234
between problem gambling symptomology and loot box spending increased from a small to a moderate 
235
effect for isolated gamers.
236
However, when PGSI scores were used to categorise gamblers into discrete risk categories (e.g., 
237
low-risk, medium-risk, problem gamblers), average spending within categories was similar for self-
238
isolated or quarantined participants and those not in isolation or quarantine. Since average spending did 
239
not increase for problem-gamblers in self-isolated or quarantined (vs. non self-isolated or non-
240
quarantined), it appears that although the correlation between raw PGSI scores and loot box spending 
241
become more closely aligned for isolated gamers, how much they individually spend per month does not 
242
substantially increase. This may suggest an increase in the number of problem-gamblers purchasing loot 
243
boxes in self-isolation or quarantine. Given that the potential gambling activities were substantially 
244
limited by Covid-19 restrictions which limited in-person gambling activities, this may suggest more 
245
gamblers turning to gambling analogues such as loot box purchasing in video games.
246
Whether these effects remain when self-isolation and quarantine periods end is unknown. 
247
These increases may plausibly represent temporary coping mechanisms which will abate when the 
248
threat of Covid-19 ends. Alternatively, given the prolonged pandemic activity worldwide, they may 
249
persist as the acquisition of new gambling-like behaviours. Re-examination as the pandemic subsides 
250
may be required to determine whether any effects are temporary or long lasting. If there are prolonged 
251
mental health effects of the pandemic, then this may plausibly result in similar long-term changes in the 
252
ways people interact with video games.
253
More generally, our results suggest that isolation due to the Covid-19 pandemic may act as a 
254
moderator for relationships in gaming and addictions research, inflating the size of some previously 
255
observed relationships (Garea et al., 2020). We therefore urge researchers to exercise caution in 
256
ensuring that the potential consequences of the pandemic and associated isolation are considered when 
257
interpreting the size of effects from data gathered during the pandemic in these areas of research. 
258
Further, we suggest that the moderating impact of Covid-19 may not be limited to these fields, and 
259
suggest that researchers consider the impact of the pandemic on their data carefully.  
260
Conclusion
261
This natural groups experiment examined eight hypotheses relating to the effects of the Covid-
262
19 pandemic on excessive gaming, psychological distress, problem gambling and loot box spending. Our 
263
data suggest that contamination concern is associated with higher excessive gaming and risky loot box 
264
engagement, and to a lesser extent, loot box spending. Problem gambling symptomology is associated 
265
with increased loot box spending, and this effect is appreciably stronger among people who are 
266
currently self-isolating or in quarantine. Future work should examine whether the increase in the 
267
strength of the relationship between loot box spending and problem gambling symptomology remains 
268
after the pandemic subsides.

Abstract
25
COVID-19 has prompted widespread self-isolation and citywide/countrywide lockdowns. The World 
26
Health Organisation (WHO) has encouraged increased digital social activities such as video game play to 
27
counteract social isolation during the pandemic. However, there is active debate about the potential for 
28
video game overuse, and some video games contain randomised purchases (loot boxes) that may 
29
psychologically approximate gambling. In this pre-registered study, we examined the effects of self-
30
isolation and quarantine on excessive gaming and loot box spending. We recruited 1,144 (619 Male, 499 
31
Female, 26 Other) Australian, Aotearoa New Zealand, and US residents who were quarantined or self-
32
isolating (n = 447) or not (n = 619) during the COVID-19 pandemic to a cross-sectional natural 
33
experiment. We compared the associations between problem gambling symptomology, excessive 
34
gaming and loot box spending for isolated and non-isolated participants. Participants completed the 
35
Kessler-10 Psychological Distress Scale, Problem Gambling Severity Index, Internet Gaming Disorder 
36
Checklist, a measure of risky engagement with loot boxes, concern about contamination, and reported 
37
money spent on loot boxes in the past month, as well as whether they were quarantined or under self-
38
isolation during the pandemic. Although, in our data, excessive gaming and loot box spending were not 
39
higher for isolated (self-isolated/ quarantined) compared to non-isolated gamers, the established 
40
association between problem gambling symptomology and loot box spending was stronger among 
41
isolated gamers than those not isolated.  Concerns about being contaminated by germs was also 
42
significantly associated with greater excessive gaming and, to a lesser extent, loot box spending 
43
irrespective of isolation status. Gamers might be managing concerns about the pandemic with greater 
44
video game use, and more problem gamblers may be purchasing loot boxes during the pandemic. These 
45
relationships may represent temporary coping mechanisms which abate when COVID-19 ends. Re-
46
examination as the pandemic subsides may be required. More generally, the results suggest that social 
47
isolation during the pandemic may inflate some pathological gaming effects. We urge caution not to 
48
generalise psychological findings from research conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic to be 
49
necessarily representative of the magnitude of relationships when not in a pandemic.

Introduction
53
COVID-19 has prompted widespread non-pharmaceutical public health interventions including 
54
self-isolation and citywide lockdowns (Lewnard & Lo, 2020). Although these measures have many 
55
advantages, including a substantial flattening of the disease curve, they may have unintended 
56
maladaptive consequences. For instance, self-isolation and quarantine has substantially reduced access 
57
to safe and legal social activities. Social support is an essential aspect of mental health, and social 
58
isolation can increase psychological distress (Teo & Valenstein, 2013). Multiplayer video games offer one 
59
avenue for online social engagement. In fact, to encourage stay-at-home social activities, the World 
60
Health Organisation (WHO) recently partnered with 58 major video game companies to launch the 
61
#PlayApartTogether campaign, encouraging people to stay at home and play video games during the 
62
pandemic (Takashi, 2020); despite having codified gaming disorder into their international classification 
63
of disease codes in 2018 (World Health Organisation, 2018). 
64
Scholarly debate persists about the potential for video games to foster maladaptive or excessive 
65
use (Aarseth et al., 2017; van Rooj et al., 2018). In particular, there is significant disagreement about 
66
whether excessive gaming produces, or is a coping mechanism for, psychopathological symptoms such 
67
as depression and anxiety ( van Rooj et al., 2018). Irrespective of whether excessive gaming is a 
68
symptom or cause of psychopathology, there is clear theoretical rationale for examining the extent to 
69
which social isolation associated with pandemic control measures contributes to excessive gameplay. 
70
Moreover, people who are more concerned about being contaminated by the virus might also increase 
71
gameplay to cope with the anxiety or social isolation due to the pandemic. Here, we examined whether 
72
(a) socially isolated individuals exhibited higher rates of excessive gameplay than less isolated peers, and 
73
(b) whether contamination concerns (worry about, and avoidance behaviours of, disease and germs; 
74
Burns et al., 1996) were significantly associated with excessive gameplay.
75
A distinct but related issue is that the inclusion of certain monetisation mechanics within video 
76
games might contribute to excessive gameplay or spending (on game-related purchases). Some video 
77
games contain randomised purchasable rewards (loot boxes) that some scholars have observed may 
78
psychologically approximate conventional gambling activities (Drummond & Sauer, 2018; Griffiths, 
79
2019). Some scholars have also observed that these mechanisms might meet the legal criteria in many 
80
jurisdictions, and could potentially be considered a form of bona fide gambling (Drummond et al., 2020; 
81
McCaffery, 2020). This may explain why studies have repeatedly found a small significant association 
82
between problem gambling symptomology and spending on loot boxes (Brooks & Clark, 2019; 
83
Drummond et al., 2019; 2020; Li et al., 2019; Macey & Hamari, 2019; Zendle & Cairns, 2018; 2019; 
84
Zendle et al., 2020), a result confirmed by meta-analysis (Garea et al., 2020). The decrease in alternative 
85
social activities, and the increased need for online social activities may have the potential to exacerbate 
86
this relationship. Spending on loot boxes therefore appears to be more common among video game 
87
players with problem gambling symptoms (Garea et al., 2020). The apparent increase in the number of 
88
people engaging with video games during the pandemic may result in increased exposure to, and hence 
89
opportunities for the purchasing of, loot boxes. This has prompted concerns about increased spending 
90
on loot boxes for certain user groups (Department for Digital, Cultural, Media & Sport., 2020; Harris, 
91
2020). One study suggests that reducing exposure to loot boxes by removing them from a game appears 
92
to reduce spending only among those with higher problem gambling symptoms (Zendle, 2019). Thus it 
93
appears that spending by problem gamblers may be specifically impacted by the presence of loot boxes 
94
in video games while playing. This led us to predict that the social isolation of the pandemic might result 
95
in an increase in either overall spending on loot boxes, or, a specific increase in spending on loot boxes 
96
for problem gamblers. Thus, here we examined whether the established association between problem 
97
gambling symptomology was stronger for isolated gamers, compared to their non-isolated peers. As we 
98
thought that people who were more concerned about contamination would be more anxious and or 
99
more socially isolated during the pandemic than people who had lower contamination concern, we 
100
expected that people with higher contamination concern may have higher spending on loot boxes. Thus, 
101
we also examined whether contamination concerns were significantly associated with spending on loot 
102
boxes.
103
Pre-registered hypotheses.
104
In this pre-registered study 
105
(https://osf.io/f4cvj/?view_only=4643a14cafc34453b05e0041f4156531) we examined participants’ 
106
excessive gaming scores, loot box spending, psychological distress, and whether they were self-isolating 
107
(limiting contact with others) or under quarantine (in mandatory self-isolation). We predicted that1: 
108
1.
Participants who report being in self-isolation or quarantine will have higher 
109
psychological distress (as measured by the K-10), higher spending on loot boxes, higher 
110
risky loot box use scores (as measured by the Risky Loot box Index; RLI), higher excessive 
111
gaming (as measured by the IGD scale), and higher contamination concern than 
112
participants not in self-isolation or quarantine.
113
2.
There will be a significant positive correlation between contamination concern (as 
114
measured by the contamination subscale of the Revised Padua Inventory 10) and 
115
psychological distress as measured by the K-10.
116
3.
There will be a significant positive correlation between contamination concern and 
117
spending on loot boxes, risky loot box use, and excessive gaming scores (as measured by 
118
the IGD scale). This effect will be stronger for participants currently in self-isolation or 
119

4.
There will be a significant positive correlation between the amount of money 
121

122

123
(PGSI). 
124
5.
The relationship will be stronger for participants in self-isolation or quarantine than for 
125
those not in self-isolation or quarantine.
126
6.
Participants who are categorized as problem gamblers by the PGSI will report spending 
127
more money in the past month on loot boxes than participants who are categorized as

Methods
139
Participants
140
Using Prolific Academic, we employed a natural experimental design to compare those people 
141
who were isolated due to the pandemic to those people who had not been isolated by the pandemic. 
142
There was no theoretical reason to believe that the pandemic had selectively isolated gamers or 
143
problem gamblers at a higher rate than other people, so this created a natural-groups experiment to 
144
assess the effects of isolation due to the pandemic on the playing and spending habits of isolated (vs 
145
non-isolated) gamers. We therefore surveyed 1,200 participants from Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand 
146
and the US on their video game play and gambling. Excluding those who did not play video games (n = 
147
54), and participants who failed attention checks (n = 2), our final sample was 1,144 participants (619 
148
Male, 499 Female, 26 Other). Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of the sample by country. 
149
The majority of participants were from the US (n = 930), with 173 participants coming from 
150
Australia, and 41 coming from Aotearoa New Zealand. Age ranged from 19 to 80 years old (M = 31 .4 
151
years, SD = 10.5 years). Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study by proceeding to 
152
the questionnaire after reading the information sheet which advised them to cease participation if they 
153
did not consent to participate in the study. Ethics approval was obtained for human data collection for 
154
this study from Massey University’s Human Ethics Southern B Committee, Approval number SOB 19/11.
155
Measures
156
Our study followed the same design as an earlier study protocol with the inclusion of some 
157
additional measures (Drummond et al., 2020). We employed a variety of standardised psychiatric 
158
measurement tools to assess participants’ wellbeing, problem gambling symptomology, excessive 
159
gaming, risky loot box use and concern about contamination. These measures are detailed below.
160
Psychological distress. The Kessler (K-10) Psychological Distress Scale assessed psychological 
161
distress of participants (Slade et al., 2011) and was employed as previously described in Drummond et 
162
al. (2020). The K-10 includes 10 items assessing how often (0, none of the time– 5, all of the time) over 
163
the past 30 days participants experienced various non-specific aspects of psychological distress (e.g., 
164
“During the last 30 days, about how often did you feel hopeless?”). 
165
Spending on loot boxes in past month. Data on loot box spending was collected as previously 
166
described in Drummond et al. (2020). We asked participants to report approximately how much money 
167
(in their country’s currency) they had spent on loot boxes in the past month. We converted all values 
168
into US dollars using the listed currency conversion rates of the day using Google’s currency conversion. 
169
We converted all currencies into US dollars on April 21, 2020 using the following exchange rates: $USD = 
170
0.63*AUD; $USD = 0.60*$NZD. In accordance with our pre-registration we excluded 9 participants (0.8% 
171
of total data) who spent more than 3.29 SDs (equivalent to a Z-score differing from the mean at p < .001; 
172
Tabachnick, Fidell, & Ullman, 2007)  greater than the mean ($6.08 USD, SD = $42.50 USD) as outliers 
173
from analyses of loot box spending. This resulted in the exclusion of any participant who spent more 
174
than $133.58 USD on loot boxes in the past month. Including these participants did not qualitatively 
175
alter the results. 
176
Risky Loot Box Index. The Risky Loot Box Index (RLI), is a five item scale designed to examine 
177
risky engagement with loot box mechanics (Brooks & Clark, 2019). This scale was employed in the same 
178
manner as described in Drummond et al. (2020). The scale asks participants to rate their agreement on a 
179
standard 7 point likert scale with items assessing their cognitions associated with opening loot boxes 
180
(e.g., “Once I open a loot box, I often feel compelled to open another”). 
181
Excessive gaming. Data were collected as previously described in Drummond et al., (2020). We 
182
adapted the Internet Gaming Disorder Checklist to assess excessive gaming symptoms (Przybylski et al., 
183
2017). This checklist was based on the proposed diagnostic criteria for Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD). 
184
Participants indicate how true (1, not at all true– 4, very true) statements were about how gaming had 
185
interfered with aspects of their lives (e.g., “I have lost interest in other hobbies or entertainment in 
186
order to play games”) or emotions (e.g., “I feel irritable, anxious or sad when I am unable to game”). 
187
Contamination Concern. We measured participants’ contamination concern with the Revised 
188
Padua-Inventory 10 item contamination subscale (Burns et al., 1996). The Padua-Inventory 10 is a 
189
psychiatric scale assessing obsessive compulsive symptomology. The contamination subscale assesses 
190
the degree of disturbance a range of common activities which might result in contamination causes 
191
participants (0, not at all – 4, very much). Example items include “I find it difficult to touch an object 
192
when I know it has been touched by strangers or by certain people” and “I feel my hands are dirty when 
193
I touch money.”
194
Problem gambling symptoms. Data on problem gambling symptoms was collected as previously 
195
described in Drummond et al. (2020). The Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) assessed Problem 
196
Gambling Symptoms. This scale asks participants to indicate how frequently (0, never– 3, almost always) 
197
during the past 12 months, they experienced problems caused by their gambling behaviors (e.g., 
198
“borrowed money or sold anything to get money to gamble”). The scale contains 9 items, with higher 
199
scores indicating greater symptoms of problem gambling. The PGSI has good validity in a non-clinical 
200
population (Holtgraves, 2009). The scale can also be used to categorize participants into discrete groups 
201
of varying risk for gambling problems. In accordance with Currie et al.,’s (2013) revised criteria for the 
202
PGSI which display better concurrent validity to the original scoring criteria, participants who score 0 on 
203
the scale are considered non-problem gamblers; low-risk gamblers score 1–4 on the PGSI; moderate-risk 
204
gamblers = 5–7 on the PGSI and problem gamblers score 8 or higher on the PGSI. 
205
Procedure
206
Participants completed the Kessler-10 Psychological Distress Scale (K-10; Slade et al., 2011), 
207
Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI; Currie et al., 2013; Holtgraves, 2009), Internet Gaming Disorder 
208
Checklist (IGDC; Przybylski et al., 2017), a measure of risky engagement with loot boxes (Risky Loot Box 
209
Index; RLI; Brooks & Clark, 2019), and reported money spent on loot boxes in the past month. 
210
Participants reported contamination concern on the Revised Padua Inventory-10 (Burns et al., 1996), 
211
and their isolation status: self-isolation (n = 250), quarantine (n = 197), or neither (n = 697). This created 
212
a natural-groups experiment to examine whether self-isolation or quarantine affected outcomes. We 
213
advertised our study on Prolific Academic as a study investigating video games and gambling. Data were 
214
collected between the 7th and the 9th of April 2020 (inclusive). At this time, Aotearoa New Zealand had 
215
been in stringent lock-downs (Level 4 under their COVID-19 Alert system) since the 25th of March 2020 
216
(Ardern, 2020). Australia and the US had not enacted a federal lockdown response (though individual 
217
States had enacted different lockdown restrictions; Morrison, 2020a; Schuchat, 2020). For context, 
218
Australia implemented a national boarder closure (with exemptions for returning citizens, permanent 
219
residents, and immediate family members) on 20th March 2020 (Morrison, 2020b), and non-U.S. citizens 
220
travelling from China, Iran, The European Schengen Area, The United Kingdom and Ireland were banned 
221
from entering the US by Presidential Proclamations taking effect between 2nd February 2020 and 16th 
222
March 2020 (Liu, 2020; NAFSA, 2020). 
223
We pre-registered comparisons between those who reported that they were self-isolating 
224
(eliminating contact with other people) or quarantined (under mandated self-isolation) and those who 
225
were not. We acknowledge that this grouping may overlook important differences in contexts between 
226
different forms of isolation (e.g., for participants anxiously awaiting test results), however two 
227
important points bear mentioning. First, the study was conducted early in the pandemic outbreaks in 
228
these countries, and terminology, especially among the general public, was relatively new,  with exact 
229
restriction limitations still under development (Cameron, 2020) and some ambiguity in the Federal 
230
response in Australia and the US (Morrison, 2020a; Schuchat, 2020). We expecteded this would add 
231
noise to participants’ self-reported isolation status, resulting in our decision to group all isolated 
232
participants together. Second, the self-isolation questions allowed participants to report whether they 
233
were in self-isolation or quarantine to their best understanding of these terms. This may result in some 
234
measurement error in reporting. However, we expected that variations in the accessibility of 
235
participants’ usual social activities would be the primary driver of increased digital media exposure. 
236
Thus, we believe our approach offers a valid way to investigate the effects of increased perceived 
237
isolation. A more fine-grained study investigating whether effects are similar across different kinds of 
238
isolation using objective measures of such isolation would no-doubt be worthwhile. Nonetheless, our 
239
results provide early evidence that participants’ perceived isolation status was associated with changes 
240
in some gaming-gambling relationships.
241
We also pre-registered comparisons between those who were under lockdown orders (specified 
242
to participants as “including mandated social distancing, business closures, curfews, stay home orders or 
243
other movement restrictions”) in cities where movement and gathering restrictions were in place) and 
244
those who were not. However, very few participants were not in lockdown (n = 69, ~6% of our total 
245
data). Thus, estimates for non-lockdown participants are underpowered and, therefore, unreliable. 
246
Rather than report these unreliable estimates in the manuscript, we have included the results for these 
247
pre-registered comparisons between participants in lockdown and participants not in lockdown in the 
248
online supplementary materials with appropriate caveats. At the time of data collection, Aoteroa New 
249
Zealand had implemented a Nationwide lockdown which had been in effect for approximately 14 days. 
250
Federal lockdown responses had not been introduced in Australia, or the US, creating divergent state-
251
based responses (Morrison, 2020a; Schuchat, 2020). We acknowledge that an examination of different 
252
stringencies of lockdown (e.g., Stage 2 versus Stage 4 lockdowns) would constitute a worthwhile follow-
253
up study. 
254
Results
255
Participants played video games, on average, most days (M = 2.31, SD = 0.83). This was likely 
256
due to the fact that the study was advertised as a study on video game use and gambling, and therefore 
257
attracted participants who played video games. On average, participants reported spending $29.63 USD 
258
(SD = $56.27 USD; Range = $0 - $500 USD) on video games and $2.98 USD (SD = $11.48 USD; Range =$0 - 
259
$107.10 USD) on loot boxes in the previous month. Although these averages appear small, it is worth 
260
noting that this was due to a large number of participants not purchasing video games (n = 578) or loot 
261
boxes (n = 989) in the past month, together with the wide ranges for these variables.
262
Contrary to predictions, psychological distress was actually higher among people not in self-
263
isolation or quarantine (M = 24.88, SD = 8.65) than those in self-isolation or quarantine (M = 23.53, SD = 
264
8.68), t(1,142) = 2.57, p = .010, d = 0.16, though this was a negligible difference (Ferguson, 2009; Sauer & 
265
Drummond, 2020). We found no evidence that IGDC scores differed between participants in self-
266
isolation/quarantine (M = 7.94, SD = 5.59) and participants not in self-isolation/quarantine (M = 8.28, SD 
267
= 5.55), t(1,142) = 0.97, p = .330, d = 0.16. We also found no evidence of differences in spending on loot 
268
boxes between participants in self-isolation/quarantine (M = $3.15 USD, SD = $11.38 USD) and those not 
269
(M = $2.86 USD, SD = $11.56 USD), t(1,133) = 0.42, p = .678, d = 0.02. There was no evidence of a 
270
difference in risky loot box use scores between participants who were isolating (M = 13.53, SD = 8.16) 
271
and those not (M = 13.52, SD = 8.00), t(1,142) = 0.02, p = .984, d = 0.01. Similarly, contamination concern 
272
scores were not statistically different between those in self-isolation/quarantine (M = 12.80, SD = 9.55) 
273
to those not (M = 13.77, SD = 9.72), t(1,142) = 1.66, p = .098, d = 0.10.
274
Collapsed across isolation status, contamination concern was moderately associated with 
275
distress, r = .319, p < .001, and weakly associated with loot box spending, IGDC, and RLI (see Table 2). 
276
Though weak, relationships between contamination concern and IGDC and RLI both exceeded criteria 
277
for the smallest effects likely to bear clinical relevance (Ferguson, 2009), suggesting contamination 
278
concern may be a clinically significant contributor to excessive engagement with both gaming and 
279
monetized in-game reward mechanisms. We compared correlation strengths using Fischer’s Z-tests 
280
(Weaver & Wuensch, 2013). Relationships were generally similar for people in self-isolation or 
281
quarantine though the association between RLI and contamination concern was significantly stronger 
282
for participants who were self-isolated or quarantined than those who were not (Table 2). 
283
Problem gambling symptomology was associated with higher loot box spending, rs = .279, p < 
284
.001; with a stronger association for participants who were self-isolated or quarantined, rs = .366, p < 
285
.001, than those who were not, rs = .209, p < .001, Z = 2.825, p = .005. Figure 1 shows the association 
286
between loot box spending and PGSI scores, split by isolation status.
287
When classified into risk groups, a 2-way ANOVA showed a main effect of problem gambler 
288
status, such that problem gamblers spent more than lower risk gamblers on loot boxes (Figure 2). 
289
However, there was no evidence that self-isolation or quarantine altered this relationship. Finally, loot 
290
box spending was weakly associated with psychological distress, rs = .103, p < .001.  
291
Discussion
292
We investigated whether self-isolation or quarantine, or contamination concerns were 
293
associated with greater excessive gaming, loot box spending or psychological distress. Contrary to 
294
predictions, compared to those not self-isolated or quarantined, participants in self-isolation or 
295
quarantine showed negligiblly lower psychological distress, but no differences in loot box spending, loot 
296
box engagement, or excessive gaming. We found no evidence that self-isolation resulted in greater 
297
excessive gameplay or expenditure on loot boxes generally. It is unclear why lower psychological distress 
298
scores were observed for those in self-isolation or quarantine. Perhaps those in self-isolation or 
299
quarantine may perceive their health risks to be lower due to their isolation, and this offset any distress 
300
associated with isolation. Nonetheless, the effect size was negligible in magnitude, and unlikely to be of 
301
clinical importance (Ferguson, 2009).
302
However, higher contamination concern was associated with small-to-moderately higher 
303
excessive gaming and risky loot box engagement scores, and small increases in loot box spending. These 
304
effects were generally similar for participants who were self-isolating or in quarantine and those who 
305
were not, with the exception that the relationship between risky loot box use and contamination 
306
concern was stronger for people self-isolating or in quarantine than those not. People may be engaging 
307
with video games to manage their contamination concerns about the COVID-19 pandemic. Of course, 
308
individuals may engage in a range of other activities (e.g., conventional gambling, online shopping, 
309
increased time streaming movies/ T.V. shows, etc.) as a means of managing contamination concern and 
310
social isolation due to the pandemic. 
311
The established relationship between problem gambling symptomology and loot box spending 
312
(Garea et al., 2020) was stronger for those in self-isolation or quarantine than those not, demonstrating 
313
closer agreement between problem gambling symptom scores and loot box spending. Indeed the 
314
relationship between problem gambling symptomology and loot box spending was moderate for 
315
isolated gamers compared to small for non-isolated gamers.
316
However, when PGSI scores were used to categorise gamblers into discrete risk categories (e.g., 
317
low-risk, medium-risk, problem gamblers), average spending within categories was similar for self-
318
isolated or quarantined participants and those not in isolation or quarantine. Since average spending did 
319
not increase for problem-gamblers in self-isolated or quarantined (vs. non self-isolated or non-
320
quarantined), it appears that although the correlation between raw PGSI scores and loot box spending 
321
become more closely aligned for isolated gamers, how much they individually spend when devolved into 
322
risk categories does not substantially increase. Orford et al. (2010) suggest that gambling disorder is 
323
more appropriately viewed as a continuum rather than as discrete risk-groups, and the lack of 
324
differences between risk categories juxtaposed against a significant increase in the strength of the 
325
association between raw PGSI scores and spending may reflect a loss of variability in PGSI scores when 
326
this continuous measure is devolved into a categorical variable. 
327
Whether these effects persist when self-isolation and quarantine periods end is unknown. These 
328
increases may plausibly represent temporary coping mechanisms which will abate when the threat of 
329
COVID-19 ends. Alternatively, given the prolonged pandemic activity worldwide, they may persist as the 
330
acquisition of new gambling-like behaviours. Re-examination as the pandemic subsides may be required 
331
to determine whether any effects are temporary or long lasting. If there are prolonged mental health 
332
effects of the pandemic, then this may plausibly result in similar long-term changes in the ways people 
333
interact with video games.
334
More generally, our results suggest that isolation due to the COVID-19 pandemic may act as a 
335
moderator for other relationships in pathological gaming research, inflating the size of some previously 
336
observed relationships (Garea et al., 2020). We therefore urge researchers to exercise caution in 
337
ensuring that the potential consequences of the pandemic and associated isolation are considered when 
338
interpreting the size of effects from data gathered during the pandemic in these areas of research. 
339
Further, we suggest that a moderating effect of COVID-19 may not be limited to these fields, and 
340
suggest that researchers in general carefully consider the potential impact of the pandemic on their 
341
data. 
342
One limitation of the present study is that it is unknown whether participants were responding 
343
to the Padua Contamination Concern index as a state or trait based measure. It is thus unclear whether 
344
the associations between the contamination concern scale, loot box purchasing,  excessive gaming and 
345
risky loot box use measures imply that changes in concerns about contamination are causing changes in 
346
these outcomes, or if, more generally, people high in trait contamination concern tend to have higher 
347
scores on these measures. Our interpretation (and we admit that it is speculative) is that both may be 
348
true. Participants who are concerned about contamination may be particularly likely to engage in 
349
distracting behaviours (including spending behaviours) during a global pandemic. Future work should 
350
examine this possibility and its alternatives more deeply.
351
Conclusion
352
This natural groups experiment examined eight hypotheses relating to the effects of the COVID-
353
19 pandemic on excessive gaming, psychological distress, problem gambling and loot box spending. Our 
354
data suggest that contamination concern is associated with higher excessive gaming and risky loot box 
355
engagement, and to a lesser extent, loot box spending. Problem gambling symptomology is associated 
356
with greater loot box spending, and this effect is appreciably stronger among people who are currently 
357
self-isolating or in quarantine. Future work should examine whether the differences in the strength of 
358
the relationship between loot box spending and problem gambling symptomology for gamers who were 
359
isolated during the pandemic remains after the pandemic subsides.
