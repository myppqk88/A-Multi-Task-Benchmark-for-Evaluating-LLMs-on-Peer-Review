==================== 审稿报告 R1 ====================

The paper offers a perspective on regional allocation of human capital and skill mismatch by
discussing different strands of the literature (human capital theory, economic geography,
education policy, institutions, etc.) and showing complementary empirical evidence using variation
in the location quotient index. The index is based on education shares across three broad
occupational groups in NUTS2 regions from a selected group of countries. The authors document
considerable regional variation in skill mismatch.
Overall, I found the paper thought-provoking and I appreciated that it drew upon various
literatures. However, I am unsure that it is possible to adequately address all questions raised by
the paper within the analysis framework chosen by the authors. More specific comments follow.
i) The paper does not separate normative from positive statements in a clear way, while lacking
notion of an optimal allocation of talent without which such discussion cannot be done. In the
empirical part, the authors focus on vertical mismatch, by which they implicitly mean high skilled
workers employed in non-managerial and non-professional roles (ISCO groups below 3). However,
such allocation could arise due to a particular industry structure which favours middle-skilled
occupations and therefore attracts a disproportionate amount of workers across all skill levels.
The paper could be improved by separating the issues of spatial allocation from mismatch, and by
corroborating the results on mismatch by other indicators of skill shortages and oversupply, such
as rates of return to education, local unemployment rates and job turnover.
ii) The authors' construction of the quotient index assumes that all countries should have similar
relative shares of human capital allocation, but this ignores country-level differences in education
systems organisation and differences in economic development (as more developed economies
tend to have higher investment rates in education in general). For example, vocational education
plays a relatively larger role in Germany and provides educational tracks to occupations which
would otherwise require a college degree in other countries. Since such country-level differences
make the results difficult to interpret, the authors should focus on within-country variation.

iii) While the paper introduces five different factors of regional specialisation, the empirical
approach doesn't in fact leave the realm of the neoclassical human capital theory, as it is largely
informed by spatial allocation of workers within the standard labour demand framework with
regional specialisation. While the authors mention other determinants such as global value chains
and local institutions, there is no systematic characterisation of their variation shown with data.
The findings on mismatch in particular lack an interpretation supported by documented evidence
on country and regional differences along the dimensions considered in the paper. Given
limitations of regional data, it is doubtful that it is feasible to discuss all these mechanisms in
detail and isolate their effects. However, the paper can still be informative by developing
comprehensive descriptive evidence in a systematic way.
iv) The paper omits critical details about the sample construction: it is not clear whether the
authors imposed age restrictions, used only full-time or part-time workers, included both genders
or only males, included or excluded migrant workers (which can have strong implications for the
results). It's also not clear whether nation averages of the shares are weighted. How did the
authors handle missing data?
v) I am concerned about specific years chosen for the analysis (2009, 2021) which could affect the
results due to strong negative economic shocks which were unevenly distributed. Since the
analysis doesn't rely on time variation, the authors should use different years and average the
results across 5 or 10 year intervals to reduce noise.
vi) The paper only shows the results for high skilled workers. What about other groups?

This article approaches a very interesting and important topic: the geography of skill mismatches
in Europe. The article uses quantitative data to map skill mismatches in selected regions, and
discusses reasons as to why these geographical variations arise.
In fact, it would benefit the article if it could focus even more on this phenomenon and its
explanations. The introduction is interesting because it juxtaposes more traditional human capital
theory with arguments derived from geographical political economy. However, while one could
debate the outcomes of this intellectual tension, this is not really the core of the article (human
capital theory is though dismissed as simplistic and positivist – a language I would myself avoid
given the convincing literature on the overall benefits of education and higher skills). The focus of
the article is not to estimate the benefits from education. Instead, I would wish for a stronger
focus on the geography of skill mismatches in the introduction. This also would open to the real
empirical geographical question in the article – what does the geography of skill mismatches look
like, and why do differences between regions arise across Europe?
I would also recommend a revision of the literature review in the same fashion (section: Human
capital and its uneven spatial distribution). Right now, this section focuses on human capital
theory and some sources of mismatches. The discussion makes a valid point around the
importance of institutions (in fact, one could probably argue that labour market and education
institutions are among the most variable factors across the countries within the single market).
Moreover, the geographical discussion treats the issue of regional divergence and uneven
regional development. On this topic, there is already a sizeable literature, and this should also not
be the core discussion of this paper. While I would actually like to challenge some of the
arguments in a within-European context (for example, that growth is a trade-off between regions,
the peculiar connection made between vocational training and precarious labour and the new
rounds of “Fordist-influenced spatial organization” – within Europe the Fordist era was in fact
characterized by decreasing regional economic disparities), this is besides the point.
Instead, I would like to recommend a stronger focus on the geographies of mismatches and why
they arise. The paper would make a stronger contribution if it could remain with this focus. In
particular, I would welcome a discussion about the dimensions that the authors later return to as
explaining the geography of mismatches: dynamism of national economies, regional
specializations, local institutions and demographic structure. In such a context, for example, the
discussion about the global production chains and their spatialities is interesting, but it does not
quite explain the geography of mismatches.

In the empirical part, the use of the different LQs is ingenious. However, it would be great if the
authors could comment on the reliability of the mismatch indicator: is there a risk that we simply
misjudged the level of skills needed in jobs, or that they changed? And where in the economy do
the most important mismatches arise? I believe that this could be easily described using the
available data, as an introduction to the geographical analysis. It would also be good if the authors
explained whether the patterns could be taken to be general – or are the results a feature of the
regions/countries that selected themselves into the university initiative?
The authors caution a warning for fig. 1.1a and b. It is hard what to make of this – is this part of
the analysis reliable or not?
In the discussion section, the authors are looking for different ways to explain the geographical
variation in the mismatches. It would be good to know how the authors ended up thinking about
these factors (linking to the theoretical section above). Secondly, it is a bit hard for the reader to
evaluate the relevance of the factors. Here, I would recommend the authors to provide empirical
evidence. If not in terms of regressions, so at least displaying the co-variation between the factors
and mismatches in regions. For example, one could display a scatterplot between the size of the
mismatch in regions, and the demographic structure. I believe that relevant indicators could be
calculated for all the dimensions. Such an exercise would make the arguments much more
convincing and interesting.
To complement this, we should always keep in mind that the LQs illustrate the spatial variation of
a phenomenon (relative), and not how important the phenomenon itself is (absolute). In fact, it
would be good to know the absolute level too – how sizeable are the mismatches around
European regions? This would be a way to illustrate the absolute importance of the phenomenon.
Also, but this is maybe too much of an extension, it would be very interesting to know if the
mismatches actually have consequences for regional growth (or, are the mismatches rather a
result of growth because growing regions attract labour that maybe cannot be accommodated
immediately?). Using the lag structure of the data, such an experiment could be made.
In the policy recommendations, the authors argue for “strong interventions”. However, the
interventions that they mention seem to be only vaguely related to the findings of this paper
(except maybe for the high-quality career counselling). Instead, I would encourage the authors to
develop policy recommendations that are immediately associated with the findings of this paper.
In sum, I find the topic and empirical approach very promising. However, revisions according to
the above would give the paper a much stronger basis to argue for particular and precise
conclusions concerning the geography of skill mismatches in the European Union. I do hope that
the authors find these comments constructive for further work.

Abstract
Investment in human capital in the form of education and its returns
for workers in terms of better-quality jobs remains a contested issue
in the literature. While some postulate that such an investment leads
to marginal productivity increments, others refute this hypothesis
arguing that it overlooks crucial political economic factors. To
contribute to ongoing debates, this paper provides a comparative
analysis at the regional level, covering the period between 2009 and
2021 and 5 EU countries (Greece, Bulgaria, France, Germany, and
Denmark), putting particular emphasis on the regions that are
associated with the countries of the European Reform University
Alliance (ERUA). Specifically, the analysis calculates highly educated
individuals employed in low-, medium-, and high-skill occupations,
and then identifies the relative regional concentrations of each
(mis)match using the Location Quotient Index. Findings highlight the
uneven geography of skill mismatches, as robust and innovative
economies tend to allocate their labour force across occupations
more efficiently than peripheral regions. Discussing our key findings
through the lens of Geographical Political Economy, we point to five
interconnected determinants of human capital allocation: national
economies’ dynamism, regional industrial specialisations, degree of
urbanisation, local institutional capacity, and demographics.
Concluding, five sets of policy recommendations are proposed
aligning with the EU New Skills Agenda and the European Universities
Initiative.

Introduction

With the 1970s crisis of Fordism leading to long processes
of industrial restructuring and deindustrialisation, workforce
skills have come to the forefront (Thrift, 2008). More
specifically, amid an unstable and often recessive conjuncture
which has been exacerbated by the successive crises of the
last decade (the 2008/09 Global Crisis, the COVID-19
pandemic, the ongoing energy crisis), employees and
regulatory bodies keep stressing the importance of new
hard skills—often associated with the digital and green
transitions—as well as soft skills related problem-solving
and adaptability to changing conditions (McGuinness et al.,
2018). Indicatively, the European Commission (EC) has
increased its focus on measures that boost workforce
skills, as reflected in the “Agenda for Skills” adopted
in 2016 (EC, 2016), the new “Agenda for Skills for
Sustainable Competitiveness, Social Justice and Resilience”
(EC, 2020), and the proclamation of 2023 as the ‘European

Year of Skills’. The EC has set ambitious targets for upskilling
and reskilling the workforce with an implementation horizon
of 2025, and a closer look reveals that its policy toolkits
strongly resonate with the basic tenets of Human Capital
Theory (HCT from now on; Becker, 1964; Mincer, 1958;
Mincer, 1974; Schultz, 1961; Schultz, 1970). In this context,
investment in education is not only presented as a way for
people to improve their skills and competencies so as to
position themselves better within the labour market, but
also as a means for upgrading human capital overall and
produce economic growth (EC, 2016; EC, 2020).
In its fundamental narrative, HCT postulates that education
increases the marginal productivity of labour (generating
growth in collective terms) and eventually leads to higher
income (for individuals). However, such simplistic and
positivist accounts have come under question especially
since the crisis of Fordism in the 1970s and the subsequent
complex disruptions in Global Value Chains (GVCs from
now on). Specifically, critique coming from class-focused
views highlights how education reproduces unequal societal
relations (Galiakberova, 2019), while Geographical Political
Economy accounts point that decisions of local, non-business
actors in relation to education are not always rational, and are
influenced decisively by globalisation trajectories, producing
in this way spatial unevenness (Teixeira, 2022). Overall, a
good part of mainstream human capital-related theorisations
is not grounded on empirical research (see Marginson, 2019;
Nafukho et al., 2004; Tan, 2014; Teixeira, 2022), while
research on skill supply and demand has produced quite
contradictory findings (see Brunello & Wruuck, 2021;
McGuinness et al., 2018; Sala, 2011).
In an effort to offer a fresh eye on the literature’s
contradictory results and fill its gaps, the paper at hand
follows highly educated workers and examines where they
tend to be employed in high-, medium, and low-skill
occupations. To do so, it builds upon a conceptual framework
that combines Geographical Political Economy and human
capital-related theorisations, and then conducts a comparative
empirical analysis that utilises the Location Quotient
Index (LQ from now on) for the period 2009–2021 and
on 5 countries (Greece, Bulgaria, France, Germany, and
Denmark). The selected study countries relate directly to
the European Reform University Alliance (ERUA), which
currently implements the ReERUA Horizon Project.
Specifically, the Alliance comprises universities located
at North and South Aegean (in Greece), Île de France
(in France), Yugozapaden (in Bulgaria), Freiburg (in
Germany), and Sjælland (in Denmark); throughout the
analysis, particular emphasis is put upon these regions.
Our study identifies five parameters influencing workforce
skills’ geographical and functional allocation—economic
dynamism at the national level, regional industrial
compositions, level of urbanisation, demographics, and
institutional characteristics—concluding that the mechanisms
of human capital development tend to reproduce uneven
development rather than ameliorating spatial inequalities.

The above outline our paper’s multifarious contribution.
On the one hand, it offers valuable insights into the negative
externalities associated with skill mismatches in the regions
of five countries representing the different socio-economic
models within the EU (Nordic, Continental European,
Mediterranean and post-socialist; Farkas, 2011), going
beyond the basic theses of prevailing theories such as HCT;
in this way, it takes a distinct position within the ongoing
debates. At the same time, it strengthens ERUA through its
study of the skill gaps between the consortium’s associated
regions that can eventually help bridge them, ultimately
making in this way a crucial contribution to EU initiatives
such as that of the European Universities.
The paper is structured as follows: the next section provides
a theoretical framework on education and skills in the labour
market and develops a conceptual framework based on
approaches from Geographical Political Economy and
elements from HCT, the Job Competition Model, and
Assignment Models. Then it lays out the methodology and
presents the results of the empirical analysis. Finally, the key
findings are discussed within the context of the relevant
literature, while the paper closes with brief conclusions that
include a set of policy recommendations.

Human Capital and its uneven spatial distribution:
Theoretical considerations and a tentative
conceptual framework

Albeit HCT is a theory which appeared in the literature early
after WWII, it still has a firm grasp on the public discourse
and policy documents. Essentially, HCT was established as
a theorisation of wage income as a function of education
and work experience (Mincer, 1958). According to it, educational
attainment is positively correlated with income because
education increases skills, which in turn increase productivity,
and higher productivity is rewarded with a higher income
(Becker, 1964; Strober, 1990). Implicitly, the idea of
self-investment in one’s own capital in the form of education
makes graduates responsible for individual success or failure,
and the state is supposed to intervene when inequalities
increase (Marginson, 2019). That is because education is
an investment that is crucial not only for workers themselves,
but also for entire economies (Tan, 2014). Indeed, when
the average income of educated people increases, this
in turn increases the national average income and eventually
creates a multiplier effect (Pritchett, 2001). At the same time,
extensive horizontal mismatches constitute a distortion in
the ideal geographical and functional allocation of human
capital, reducing the productivity of firms and economic
systems in general, and more importantly, profoundly
diminishing an economy’s capacity to adapt to gradual
restructuring processes or sudden shocks (Brunello & Wruuck,
2021).
The skills, competencies, and knowledge required are
determined by the tasks each worker must undertake for
their position and the industry in which they work (Giouli
et al., 2021). There are two broad categories of skills. For

one, hard skills, which are the skills required to perform
a certain job; these are mostly acquired through formal
education, (re)training, and on-the-job experience, thus they
are measurable through academic degrees and various types
of certificates. Examples of such skills include language
proficiency, expertise on certain software, technical skills,
etc. (McGuinness et al., 2018). On the other hand, soft skills
(also known as non-cognitive skills or interpersonal and
personal skills) complement hard skills and are basic
competencies needed in the workplace, even if they are
not tied to a specific job. Communication, teamwork, and
organisational skills are among the soft skills most often
required by employers regardless of specific occupations
(McGuinness et al., 2018). Employers consider soft skills as
an indicator of job performance, as they determine a person’s
ability to lead and/or work in teams (Hewitt, 2006), and thus,
largely determine career success (Watts et al., 2008).
Overall, the development of both hard and soft skills is
closely linked to employment and income opportunities,
both waged and self-employment (Vinayan et al., 2020).
Despite the undeniable importance of skills, however, there
are often mismatches between their supply and demand,
as labour markets are being imperfect in their nature
(Teixeira, 2022). Specifically, there are two broad types of
mismatches. Horizontal mismatches refer to cases where
workers are engaged in activities outside their field of
education and/or skills. For their part, vertical mismatches
refer to cases where workers engage in activities that do
not match their skill level. Although mismatches refer to
both overqualification and underqualification, the paper at
hand we will focus on the former. Overskilled workers tend
to earn less than those who are in a position matching their
skills, tend to have lower satisfaction levels, and are overall
more inclined to quit their job. Importantly, they are less keen
on acquiring new competences, albeit their high education
does not protect them from skill obsolescence (Somers
et al., 2019). Relatedly, overskilling worsens employment
prospects in the long run, as it undermines incentives for
young people to continue investing in human capital
(Cedefop, 2015). Many studies have shown that vulnerable
groups such as migrants, women and young people are more
often trapped in such situations (Cedefop, 2014). For
firms, overskilling indicates an untapped potential that is
underutilised or not used at all, lowers productivity as their
staff is dissatisfied, and limits their adaptation strategies as
overskilled workers are reluctant to retrain (Brunello & Wruuck,
2021). On a wider note, for national economies, firms’ lower
productivity erodes competitiveness and investments in
education are not returned into the economy (McGuinness
et al., 2018).
In this context, HCT cannot provide a comprehensive
analytical framework neither in terms of the geographical (dis)allocation of human capital (Teixeira, 2022), nor in
terms of individuals’ educational and career choices, which
are not always based on rational processes (Marginson,
2019). More specifically, HCT assumes that the human capital

is allocated exclusively through labour market mechanisms,
not taking political economic factors into account (Teixeira,
2022). Namely, workforce skills sooner or later align with
demand through labour force development systems, thus
skill mismatches cannot exist in the long run (McGuinness &
Pouliakas, 2016). In this process, wider needs are translated
into smaller scales through the operation of local labour
regimes (Jonas, 1996). As implied, HCT approaches the
equilibrium between the supply and demand of skills
within an “institutional vacuum” context, which is detached
from processes outside the market and without broader
power relations in place (Psilos & Gereffi, 2011). In other
words, HCT ignores the impact of space and the political
economy, when in reality skills and labour are constantly
influenced by broader processes including demographic
and socio-economic changes, institutional settings, and
the field of reproduction which includes family and peer
pressure, cultural practices, etc. (Peck & Smucker, 1998). The
above attempt to outline how the development of skills is an
inherently social process, brought about and maintained
by hierarchical power relations within and outside labour
markets (Galiakberova, 2019). The influence of powerful
non-entrepreneurial actors is key in shaping human capital,
as fittingly described by theorisations around Local Labour
Regimes (Werner, 2019). Similarly, labour force development
is not an equal opportunity field, but rather depends on
characteristics such as class, race, gender, ethnicity and migrant
status; in fact, labour force development plays a key role
in perpetuating—not only social inequalities but also—labour
market segmentation (Teixeira, 2022). Lastly, crises and
recessions have a decisive impact on the development of
skills. Indicatively, the 2008/09 Global Crisis led to a
concerted slashing of public spending on education, which
profoundly altered human capital in the following years
(Gourzis & Gialis, 2019). All the above mark clear that the
return of investments in education, whether at the scale of
an individual or of a whole state, can be quite unpredictable.
Moreover, the above imply that many reproducing mechanisms
related to human capital fuel spatial inequalities by favouring
economic stability and growth for certain regions at the
expense of others (Teixeira, 2022). Specifically, with
capital-and technology-intensive activities tending to concentrate in already advanced regions, new rounds of uneven
development unfold (Vinuela-Jimenez et al., 2010). In this
process, large metropolitan areas with a diverse economic
base concentrate human capital, cutting-edge technology,
and finance-related activities, while economically backward
and declining regions become even more disconnected from
knowledge and innovation (Feldman et al., 2021). Against
this narrative of uneven development, many theories have
argued that developed regions may tend to attract activities,
workforce, and capital as they further industrialise, but
they also do spill over positive effects to their surrounding
areas (Boudeville, 1968; Perroux, 1955). In fact, theoretical
models such as the “poles of growth” have been
well-established and dominant in policy and scientific
debates since the first post-war decades (Christofakis &
Papadaskalopoulos, 2011). Countless strategic plans have

been devised based on such theorisations, aiming at
transforming agglomeration tendencies into a competitive
advantage, with examples coming from many different
geographical contexts including Germany, France, and Greece
(Hadjimichalis, 1992; Leontidou, 1990). After the end of
Fordism, however, Growth Poles Models started losing their
grip as they proved to be largely unsuccessful: conditions
did not improve for most lagging regions, while large urban
areas also suffered a number of negative externalities due
to unchecked population growth and piling of activities
(Christofakis & Papadaskalopoulos, 2011). Nevertheless, new
approaches in economic geography continue to emphasise
the formation of dynamic urban centres due to their key role
in the global economy and their spillover qualities (Florida
et al., 2008; Fujita & Mori, 1996; Fujita et al., 1999); as
becomes clear, this fuels new rounds of Fordist-influenced
spatial organisation (Krugman & Venables, 1996).
In our view, such approaches and the policy responses they
inspire reinforce the polarisation of economic growth
right from the outset. As a result, it is not surprising that
educational and employment opportunities still vary greatly
across regions (Fratesi & Rodríguez-Pose, 2016; Iammarino
et al., 2019). Certain regions that are locked into a positive
path dependency, namely a process which reinforces virtuous
cycles of regional growth path, while others are locked
in their rigidities including outdated technology and
institutional weaknesses, which reinforce vicious cycles
of regional vulnerability. Moreover, the latter often find
themselves deeper into negative path dependencies after
recessive periods, as they have no capacity to absorb
shocks and adapt to a new reality (Rodríguez-Pose et al.,
2023). Having said the above, there are cases of regions
reinventing themselves and carving new development
pathways, revitalised and repositioned in emerging global
production networks and chains of value added (Boschma,
2015). Still, the norm for most already vulnerable regions
is that they prove unable to create knowledge-intensive and
high-tech jobs for high-skilled workers. These regions are
typically peripheral, rural and less developed (MacKinnon
et al., 2022). Lastly, it must be stressed that even within
regions that have developed self-sustaining processes of
growth stark inequalities emerge, as different fractions of
capital seek for both cheap and skilled workers (Teixeira,
2022). Importantly, such labour market segmentation is
sustained by dual human capital development mechanisms;
namely, next to a robust education system, a training system
that focuses more on vocational training and apprenticeship
initiatives is formed, serving as a channel for precarious
labour (Teixeira, 2019). The deepening inequalities within
affluent regions is an emerging topic in the literature, with
many attributing the rise of populism to this phenomenon
(Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023).

Methodological remarks

The aim of this paper is to study the spatialities of the
matching of highly educated workers in different skill-level
occupations. Specifically, we study the regional (NUTS-2
level) concentrations of workers with high levels of education

in low, medium, and high skill occupations. The paper
focuses on high education levels to examine the contested
issue of overskilling, which refers to instances where a
worker is not using their whole skillset in their job
(Brunello & Wruuck, 2021). The severe short- and long-term
consequences of overskilling, as indicated in the previous
section, underline the high scientific importance and policy
relevance of our study’s objective.
The above research objective is scrutinised for five countries
(Greece, Bulgaria, France, Germany, and Denmark), which
were chosen on the basis that they stand for the four different
socio-economic models that have been identified to currently
co-exist within Europe: the Nordic, Continental European,
Mediterranean, and post-socialist ones (Farkas, 2011).
Moreover, while both France and Germany fall under the
Continental model, they are both analysed due to their stark
differences in terms of their growth models, and particularly
regarding their wage structures (which relate to this paper’s
objectives; Hein & Vogel, 2008). This way, the analysis
provides a view that can be representative of the EU as a
whole. Moreover, throughout the analysis we focus on
six regions which are specifically related to the ReERUA
Horizon Project, in the frame of which the analysis at hand
is conducted; these are the North and South Aegean

regions in Greece, Île de France in France, Yugozapaden
in Bulgaria, Freiburg in Germany, and Sjælland in Denmark
(see Figure 1.A). These regions place particular emphasis
on the knowledge that comes from their educational
institutions; at the same time, they are developing strong
knowledge networks within the ReERUA consortium, despite
having very different socio-economic structures. As such,
they constitute interesting case studies, especially when seen
within their wider context.
The data were taken from the Eurostat Labour Force Survey
upon request in July 2022.
The aforementioned analysis is conducted for two years
(2009 and 2021), which essentially allow for a comparison
between the onset of a period characterised by successive
challenges for regional and national economies across the
EU and the most recent available data. Specifically, this
choice of years covers the Great Recession that followed
the 2008/09 Global Crisis (lasting from 2009 until 2013–14
for most European economies), a period of partial recovery,
and the two pandemic years (2020 and 2021; Herod et al.,
2021). As such, the temporal scope of this study constitutes
an interesting case and reveals much about how regions
received and adapted to recessive pressures.

Highly educated workers in low skill level occupations

LQ(i) shows the relative regional concentration of highly
educated employed persons in low-skill occupations. In 2009,
overconcentration of this phenomenon was mostly observed
in Île de France (LQ=2.62) and in the southern regions
of France, namely the agricultural regions of Midi-Pyrenees
(LQ=3.42) and Auvergne (LQ=3.83), in the affluent region
of Rhône-Alpes (LQ=2.36), and in the touristic region of

Provence-Alpes-Côte d’Azur (LQ=2.52). Similar trends
can also be observed in Thuringia (LQ=2.21), a region in
Germany known for its university, the Greek capital region
of Attiki (LQ=1.97), and the capital region of Bulgaria
Yugozapaden (LQ=1.81). For 2021, we can see that in all
French regions for which data are available LQ values
are above 2.15. Other regions with particularly high LQ
values are Hovedstaden (LQ=3.30) and Midtjyllan (LQ=3.25)
in Denmark. Underconcentrations or close to the transnational
aggregate one were not observed in the regions with available
data (see Figure 1.1a and Figure 1.1b).

In 2021, the overall number of regions exhibiting
overconcentration increased. However, Attica stands out
(LQ=2.06), as well as the other Greek regions show similar
trends with the exception of the rural North Aegean (LQ=1.06).
The German regions have low or close to the aggregate
average shares, including Freiburg. The same applies for
Denmark; indicatively, the highest value in the country can
be found in Hovedstaden (LQ=0.82). In France, most
regions exhibit an over-concentration; only seven regions are
exceptions to this, having shares close to the aggregate
average, including Île de France (Figure 1.2b).

Discussing the determinants of human capital
allocation
Our analysis above has highlighted the uneven geography of
skill and education levels’ matching across the regions
under study. Here, drawing upon our findings, we will discuss
the deeper causes of these socio-spatial inequalities,
highlighting five distinct, yet interrelated parameters that
directly shape the distribution of human capital: (a) the
economic dynamism of national economies, (b) the
particularities of regional industrial structures, (c) urbanisation
levels, (d) local institutional structures and practices, and
(e) demographics.
First, there are multiple indications that dynamism of national
economies is a prime determining factor for the spatially
uneven distribution of human capital and skills’ matching.
In our study, robust national economies, both in terms of
total and per capita GDP (Germany, Denmark, France), were
found to overall exhibit more notable concentrations of
highly educated workers in high skill positions than less
dynamic ones (Bulgaria, Greece); Denmark had the highest

GDP per capita in 2021 among the countries under study,
being above the EU average by 33%, while Germany and
France followed (with 20% and 5% respectively).
Additionally, a closer look reveals that the former countries
have oriented their production towards dynamic sectors
comprising high added value manufacturing, financial
services, and other specialised B2B services (Herod et al.,
2021). Beyond exhibiting more efficient skill matching, countries
with dynamic sectoral specialisations concentrate much
higher shares of a high-skilled workforce in the first place
(Lee & Rodriguez-Pose, 2013). The reason that some
countries can develop a workforce with a higher skill set
whereas others struggle to follow, is their positioning within
global value chains (GVCs). In one way or another, being
embedded to some extent is common for almost all national
economies (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011), albeit whether
this embeddedness benefits them (Humphrey, 2004) or
exposes them to external pressures (Psycharis et al., 2014)
remains an open question. In general, it is commonly accepted
that already advanced economies achieve a better positioning
which brings upon them a series of positive outcomes,
including being able to attract high-skilled labour, as they
have the capacity to utilise it properly (Fernandez-Stark
et al., 2011).
Beyond the national scale, positioning within global
production networks (GPNs) translates in profoundly different

ways at the regional level (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011).
Characteristically, stark inequalities among regions regarding
skill and education matching were found in Germany
and France despite being strong national economies, while
instances of a great matching were identified within lagging
national economies, as in the case of Bulgaria’s and Greece’s
capital regions (Yugozapaden & Attica respectively). For
one, this observation underlines how robust regions within
a poorer context overconcentrate capital and activities at the
expense of their surrounding regions; interestingly, the same
applies even in Germany, France, and Denmark, although to
a lesser extent. The above help us to (at least partially) refute
theoretical and empirical postulations around the spillover
effect of growth poles (see Florida et al., 2008).
More importantly, the above highlight the importance of
regional structures within a wider context of value chains,
which brings us to the second factor of spatial unevenness
in
human
capital
distribution,
regional
industrial
specialisations. The very constitution of “sequential structures”
such as GVCs implies uneven development: some places
get to host the “strong links” of these chains—i.e., the
activities of higher value added which demand skilled
labour—whereas the rest are left with the weaker ones
(Smichowski et al., 2016). Within a value chain, a higher
value added is typically attributed to the initial-stage and
the later-stage activities, whereas those in the middle are
lower value added (forming a “smiley face” curve;
Mohiuddin & Su, 2014). Specifically, activities which
come first in production chains relate to the standardisation,
innovation, and design of a product, activities coming
afterwards include the manufacturing and assembly of
a product, and activities that come last relate to logistics,
marketing, and branding (Fróes de Borja Reis & Barroso
de Souza, 2018). Thus, regions which for example host
innovation research centres or constitute strong logistic
centres are positioned in better terms within the global
geography of skills, whereas those which constitute
manufacturing/assembly centres are positioned in worse
terms, except for the case of particularly high added value
products (Mohiuddin & Su, 2014). In sectoral terms,
constructions, manufacturing, and the primary sector typically
comprise lower skill level occupations and as a result
attract workers with a lower education level (Cedefop,
2018); in contrast, industries characterised by a high
R&D expenditure/value added rate (e.g., biotech), as well
as service and business operations stemming from scientific
knowledge are linked with high-skill labour. In our analysis,
regions standing out in terms of matching highly educated
workers with high skill occupations included Île de France,
Berlin in Germany, and Hovedstaden in Denmark. All of
the above constitute innovative regions with a high GDP
per capita – for its part, Île de France is an innovation
leader at a pan-EU level (as indicated by the Regional
Innovation Scoreboard; European Commission, 2022).
Similarly, Yugozapaden, which also exhibited a notable high
skill/education matching has taken significant steps towards

economic modernisation and technological improvement in
recent years (European Commission, 2022).
As becomes evident, the first two factors discussed above
are deeply interconnected. For one, regions cannot alter
their industrial composition substantially without upgrading
within global value chains. Moreover, this upgrading occurs
usually within a wider context (i.e., the national one); it is
not common for regions to upgrade while being part of
obsolete domestic economic systems (Gereffi & Lee, 2016).
In the frame of our analysis, the overlapping of the first two
factors can be indicated by the concentrations of highly
skilled workers in middle skill level occupations, since
this reflects some sort of skill mismatching. Specifically,
all Greek regions displayed relevant overconcentrations, with
Attica standing out among all study regions. This reflects
not only Greece’s weaker embeddedness within global value
chains and lack of orientation towards productive activities
(e.g., the North Aegean specialises in small-scale agriculture
while the other insular regions overfocus on tourism;
Herod et al., 2022), but also its capital region being only
but a moderate innovator, lagging significantly in terms
of R&D spending (European Commission, 2022). Of course,
these industrial specialisations at the national and regional
level cannot be seen outside a wider historical context, as
contemporary global value chains are the product of long
structural transformations and abrupt adaptations to crises.
Specifically, in the aftermath of the crisis of Fordist
industrialisation during the 1970s, regions in Northern
and Central Europe took a turn toward technologically
advanced sectors; in contrast, being unable to maintain their
export capacity, peripheral regions across Southern Europe
saw their industrial systems dismantled and resorted to less
innovative, constructions-driven growth models (Herod et al.,
2021). Additionally, the 2008/09 Global Crisis put a heavy
strain on the peripheral parts of the EU and led to the
burst of the real estate bubbles they had developed,
decimating their construction activity (Gourzis & Gialis,
2019). However, having their industrial structures already
dismantled, these regions followed a course further into
deindustrialisation, gradually developing a heavy dependence
on tourism activities (Herod et al., 2022). As a result, their
economic base in recent years comprises a mix of less
innovative, technologically backward sectors such as
construction, retail, and low value-added market services
(Fana et al., 2016).
In further examining the determinants of skill (mis)matches,
closely related to industrial specialisations is the extent of
urbanisation. Regions with dynamic metropolitan areas
typically abound in occupations requiring higher skill levels,
thus, demand for skilled labour is high there (Psycharis
et al., 2014); on the other end, urban environments often
constitute more attractive environments for a high skilled and
creative labour force as they are characterised by more
amenities—cultural,
transportations-wise,
choices
for
education, etc.—and lower entry barriers for newcomers

(Florida et al., 2008). As a result, our analysis indicated
the highest levels of high education/skill matching in
metropolitan regions. This is rather expected, since the main
urban agglomerations within each national context act as
dominant poles, accumulating population and capital,
concentrating the better part of domestic infrastructure, and
attracting foreign investments (Perroux, 1955). In this frame,
they develop economies of scale which fuel processes of
mostly self-sustained growth (Polyzos, 2015). Interestingly,
our analysis found that instead of metropolitan regions in
general, it was mostly the capital regions within each
country that exhibited the aforementioned characteristics.
On a deeper level, being the administrative and business
centres in their national context, these regions represent
their countries’ development potential; extending this postulation, their performance often determines a whole country’s positioning within global value chains (Christofakis &
Papadaskalopoulos, 2011). Nevertheless, we must note here
that the above take place within a context of highly dualised
labour markets; namely, besides the core of well-qualified
people working in high skill occupations, there is a periphery
of precarious workers undertaking menial tasks (Teixeira,
2022). Lastly, we must also note that metropolitan regions’
potent forces of attraction lead to a skill mismatching-related
paradox: our analysis indicated that super-star cities within
robust national economies, as well as capital cities smaller
national economies, concentrate a larger highly educated
workforce than what their labour markets can sustain. As a
result, they were found to display overconcentrations of highly
educated people even in low-skill occupations, as in the case
of Île de France. This is a particularly important observation,
as it implies deep cutting inequalities and fierce competition
even within the most dynamic regional labour markets, which
exacerbate during recessive periods (Herod et al., 2021).
Nevertheless, such types of mismatches were found to be
more prominent in less dynamic areas; it is characteristic
that most of the regions at the periphery of the five study
countries we studied here displayed concentrations of highly
educated workers in low skill occupations. More importantly,
in these regions, skill mismatches concerning the high-skilled
labour do not solely emerge in times of economic turbulence,
but they become their inherent, diachronic traits (Iammarino
et al., 2019). A result of these widespread mismatching
phenomena across various regional contexts is that one out
of four university graduates in the EU work in jobs requiring
lesser skills than what they have acquired through their
education. This means that a good part of the workforce
is underused (Cedefop, 2018), despite approaches such
as HCT postulating that overskilling cannot persist in the
long run as it is automatically balanced by market mechanisms (McGuinness & Pouliakas, 2016). Apart from a less
advantageous position within domestic and international
networks, a good part of these persistent skill mismatches
should be attributed to weak local institutional structures and
practices, which are apparent especially in peripheral
regions (Herod et al., 2022). Specifically, weak institutions
fuel ‘information asymmetries’ among tertiary education

graduates leading them to poor career choices (McGuinness
et al., 2018). The above observations are of crucial importance,
as overskilling constitutes a serious threat, especially
when it persists, since it undermines the incentive for
young people to continue investing in their human capital
(Cedefop, 2015), diminishes productivity rates, and renders
whole economy vulnerable to sudden changes (Brunello &
Wruuck, 2021). In addition to their weak institutional
structures, peripheral regions also appear unable to attract
and keep a minimum amount of high skilled labour to cover
their needs (Florida et al., 2008), thus suffer from extensive
underskilling as well (Lee & Rodriguez-Pose, 2013). In this
context, these regions enter a vicious cycle of being unable
to fix skill mismatches, which in turn shapes the conditions
that render those regions even more susceptive to all types
of mismatches. This self-regenerating vulnerability comes
in stark antithesis with the virtuous cycle of self-sustained
growth that dynamic regions enter; combined, these cycles
mark clear that—in the absence of decisive institutional
interventions—spatial inequalities tend to deepen instead
of being ameliorated over time (Teixeira, 2022).
One last decisive difference between urbanised and rural
areas that has to be taken under consideration is their
demographic structure. In Europe and elsewhere, urban
centres retain a younger population as compared to rural
areas (Gregory & Patuelli, 2015). This shapes the composition
of skills directly: older workers have lost their incentive
to retrain, while the introduction of new technologies
render their skill sets obsolete (McGuinness et al., 2018).
At the same time, the weight of meeting skill demand falls
upon the shoulders of those entering the labour market,
who must develop the appropriate competencies (Cedefop,
2015). Moreover, conditions of intensified competition
further push younger people to invest more in their training
(Brunello & Wruuck, 2021), with education being their
main defence mechanism in retaining their place in the
job market (as postulated in the Job Competition Model;
Sala, 2011). Such conditions are particularly prevalent in
European labour markets post-2008 (Gourzis & Gialis, 2019).

Conclusions

This study examined the issue of human capital allocation
across five European countries at the regional level,
focusing on the regions where the university departments
of the ERUA consortium are located: North and South
Aegean in Greece, Île de France in France, Yugozapaden
in Bulgaria, Freiburg in Germany, and Sjælland in Denmark.
Our findings highlighted the uneven geography of skill
(mis)matches, as capital and metropolitan regions were
observed to be more efficient than less affluent and
peripheral ones in matching supply (in terms of education)
and demand (in terms of skill level occupations), especially
when it comes to upper-level skills. To further decipher
this remark, we discussed the role of five inter-related
factors: economic dynamism at the national level, regional
sectoral specialisations, urbanisation, institutional structures,
and demographic trends. Importantly, our analysis pinpointed

that human capital is shaped by multiple forces at the
same time, most of which relate with each other in a
dialectical way. Furthermore, we identified a series of
virtuous and vicious cycles which drive uneven development
as they refer to self-sustained, regenerating processes of
resilience and vulnerability. Ultimately, we found human
capital to be of the utmost importance in regional
development; although it can generate spatial growth, its
concentration in certain areas leaves lagging regions out of
growth pathways.
To break this pattern, strong interventions are required. Thus,
here we recommend a series of actions drawing upon the
EU’s New Skills Agenda, which are tailored for the regions
associated with the ERUA but can be applied to other
contexts as well. For one, in the regions with strong research
and academic activity (as the ones related to the consortium),
it is important that their universities work together with
stakeholders from industry to spill over knowledge and
cover the research and innovation needs of the labour
market. In this frame, learning events can be developed
to identify the skills needed in the labour market and
communicate them to the unemployed and precariously
employed. Moreover, these events must be combined
with high quality career counselling for the unemployed
and teaching of digital skills to the precariously employed,
with special attention to vulnerable groups such as the
youth, women, and migrants; this reskilling and upskilling
could be undertaken by university staff in the frame of
the European Competence Framework for Researchers.
Apart from the above, social partners should be more
encouraged to be involved in labour market forecasting
and early identification of skill shortages and trends through
joint actions; this way, a tighter network of social, scientific,
and policy-related stakeholders would be formed. Finally,
amid the current energy transition, developing green skills

will produce a generation of climate-conscious professionals
with pertinent competences that will ultimately benefit
both the environment and the workforce.

Abstract
This paper examines the uneven geography of skill mismatches by
studying the regional allocation of highly educated workers in high,
medium or low skill-level occupations. To this end, a conceptual
framework is developed combining Geographical Political Economy
with theorizations around the optimal allocation of human capital. A
comparative empirical analysis is then conducted using the Location
Quotient Index for the period 2009-2021 for five countries (Greece,
Bulgaria, France, Germany and Denmark), where the universities
affiliated to the European Reform University Alliance (ERUA) are
located. The results of this analysis are then contextualized through
correlations with various macroeconomic, population density, and
demographic metrics, which represent factors underlying the uneven
geography of skill mismatches. The analysis stresses five critical
factors behind the regional and occupational allocation of skills:
economic dynamism at the national level, regional industrial
specializations, urbanisation levels, demographic structures, and
institutional endowments. Moreover, it is argued, these factors behind
human capital development tend to reproduce geographical
unevenness rather than ameliorating spatial inequalities. Based on
these observations, a set of policy actions is recommended based on
the five factors mentioned above and the EU’s New Skills Agenda. With
the above, the paper offers valuable insight into the negative
externalities associated with skills mismatches, taking into account the
different socio-economic models and school-to-work transition
systems across the EU. Moreover, it attempts to make a positive
contribution to the European Universities Initiative, as examining the
skills gaps in the regions associated with the ERUA strengthens the

Op

Alliance itself and helps to bridge existing inequalities.

Adopting a comprehensive approach which incorporates institutional,

Introduction

During the past five decades, long processes of
de/re-industrialization brought the importance of workforce’s
skills to the forefront (Thrift, 2008). In the current unstable
conjuncture, which has been marked by successive crises,
including the 2008/09 Global Crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic,
and the subsequent energy crisis, governments and regulatory
bodies keep stressing the importance of new hard skills—often
associated with the digital and green transitions—as well as
soft skills related to problem-solving and adaptability to changing conditions (McGuinness et al., 2018). Indicatively, the
European Commission (EC) has published relevant agendas
(EC, 2016; EC, 2020), while it proclaimed 2023 as the
“European Year of Skills.” EC’s targets for up-/re-skilling
initiatives and its policy toolkits strongly resonate with the
basic tenets of Human Capital Theory (HCT from now on;
Becker, 1964; Mincer, 1958). In this context, investment in
education and VET (Vocational Education and Training) is
presented not only as a way for employees to improve their
skills and competencies but also as a means for upgrading
human capital overall, which can lead to economic growth
(EC, 2016; EC, 2020).
Despite the above, there are still significant skill mismatches in
many European regions, which the EU is trying to alleviate by
better linking education and employment, supporting training
programmes, and promoting labour mobility. Notably, a key
factor in the Europe 2020 Strategy for achieving dynamic labour

markets and efficient job matching is labour mobility (European
Commission, 2012); to this end, the EU has launched the EURES
(EURopean Employment Services) platform and EPALE
(Electronic Platform for Adult Learning in Europe). Yet, such
efforts have brought varying results, given regional differences
overall, including in educational structures. Such differences
can reproduce unequal social relations (Galiakberova, 2019),
and ultimately, have led to a highly uneven geography of
mismatches. In addition, from the perspective of Geographical
Political Economy, beyond differences in regulatory, educational, or industrial structures, the very decisions of local,
non-business actors regarding education are not always
rational and are crucially influenced by the trajectories of globalization, reproducing spatial inequalities all over again
(Teixeira, 2022). Notably, most dominant theories related to
skills and education are not clearly evidence-based (Tan, 2014;
Teixeira, 2022), while empirical research on skill supply and
demand has produced several contradictory findings (Brunello
& Wruuck, 2021; McGuinness et al., 2018; Sala, 2011).
In an effort to offer a fresh eye on the literature’s contradictory results and rectify its gaps, the paper at hand examines
the regional allocation of highly educated workers and examines where they tend to be employed in high-, medium-, and
low-skill occupations. This research subject is particularly
crucial, considering that overskilling indicates severe short- and
long-term consequences. To do so, the paper develops a
conceptual framework that combines Geographical Political
Economy and human capital-related theorizations. Then, it
conducts a comparative empirical analysis that utilises the
Location Quotient Index (LQ from now on) for the period
2009–2021 and on 5 countries (Greece, Bulgaria, France,
Germany, and Denmark), with the results of this analysis being
subsequently correlated with several macroeconomic, population, and demographic metrics that pertain to factors that
influence the spatial allocation of workforce skills. Given
that the study at hand is conducted for the European Reform
University Alliance (ERUA), it is geographically focused
on the countries where the Alliance’s universities are based.
Specifically, the Alliance comprises universities based on
North and South Aegean in Greece, Ιle de France, Yugozapaden
in Bulgaria, Freiburg in Germany, and Sjlland in Denmark;
throughout the analysis, particular emphasis is put upon these
regions.
Our study concludes that five factors are decisive for workforce skills’ geographical and functional allocation: economic
dynamism at the national level, regional industrial composition, level of urbanization, demographics, and institutional
characteristics. Moreover, it underlines that the mechanisms
of human capital development tend to reproduce uneven
development rather than ameliorating spatial inequalities.
The above outlines our paper’s multifarious contribution. On
the one hand, it offers valuable insight into the negative externalities associated with skill mismatches, taking into account
Europe’s different socio-economic models and school-to-work

regimes (Pohl & Walther, 2007), as these have been identified
in the literature (i.e., Nordic, Continental European, Mediterranean and post-socialist; Farkas, 2011). At the same time, studying skill gaps in ERUA’s associated regions strengthens the
Alliance itself and helps bridging existing inequalities; more
broadly, this is a crucial contribution to the EU’s European
Universities initiative.
The paper is structured as follows. The next section provides
a theoretical framework on education and skills in the labour
market and develops a conceptual framework based on
approaches from Geographical Political Economy and elements
from HCT, the concept of optimal allocation, the Job Competition Model, and the Assignment Models. Then it lays out
the methodology and presents the results of the empirical
analysis. Finally, the key findings are discussed within the
context of the relevant literature, while the paper closes with
brief conclusions that include a set of policy recommendations.

Human Capital and its uneven spatial distribution:
theoretical considerations and a tentative
conceptual framework

In general, the skills, competencies, and knowledge required
by the workforce are determined by the tasks each worker
must undertake for their position and the industry in which
they work (Giouli et al., 2021). There are two broad categories of skills. Hard skills are those required to perform a certain
job, including for instance language proficiency, expertise on
certain software, technical skills, etc. (McGuinness et al., 2018).
As these skills are acquired mostly through formal education,
(re)training, and on-the-job experience, they are measurable
through academic degrees and various types of certificates.
Given the latter, this is the type of skills this paper will mostly
focus on. Nevertheless, soft skills—also known as non-cognitive
skills or interpersonal and personal skills—complement
hard skills and are basic competencies needed in the workplace, even if they are not tied to a specific job. Under this
category, communication, teamwork, and organizational skills
are included, being actively sought after by employers regardless of the sector or occupation (McGuinness et al., ibid).
Overall, the development of both hard and soft skills is
closely linked to better career prospects, either in waged
positions or in self-employment (Vinayan et al., 2020).
To engage with the necessary skills required in the workplace, the relevant literature has developed numerous theories. Although having appeared early after WWII, HCT still
has a firm grasp on the public discourse and policy documents.
Postulating that wage income is a function of education and
work experience, HCT highlights the importance of individual
educational attainment (Mincer, 1958). Specifically, education
increases skills and thus productivity, which is then rewarded
with a higher income (Becker, 1964). In fact, the level of
education of a workforce remains crucial also on a broader
note, as it increases national income and ultimately generates
multiplier effects (Pritchett, 2001). Therefore, education is not
only crucial for the workers themselves, but also for economies (Tan, 2014). To maximize the effects of higher educational attainment, however, talent has to be allocated in an

optimal way, namely, a worker must employ their skills in
the most appropriate setting. It is then through the optimal allocation of talent that a higher educational attainment level
generates individual and social returns (Acemoglu, 1995).
Despite the undeniable importance of education for developing
the necessary skills, skill mismatches are common, pointing to
labour markets being rather imperfect in their nature (Teixeira,
2022). Two broad types of mismatches appear in literature.
On the one hand, horizontal mismatches refer to cases where
workers are engaged in activities outside their field of education and skills. Extensive horizontal mismatches constitute
a distortion in the ideal geographical and functional allocation of human capital, reducing the productivity of firms and
economic systems in general, and more importantly, profoundly diminishing an economy’s capacity to adapt to gradual
restructuring processes or sudden shocks (Brunello & Wruuck,
2021). On the other hand, vertical mismatches refer to cases
where workers engage in activities that do not match their
skill level, namely they are either over- or underqualified for
the tasks they perform. Although both instances bear significant consequences for the individual, the firm, and the economy,
the paper at hand focuses on overskilling. Otherwise coined
as surplus human capital (Zhu & Chen, 2016), it has a negative impact on an individual’s wages and job satisfaction
(McGuinness & Wooden, 2009). Specifically, overskilled
workers tend to earn less than those who are in a position
matching their skills, tend to have lower satisfaction levels,
and are overall more inclined to quit their job. Importantly,
they are less keen on acquiring new competences, despite their
high education not protecting them from skill obsolescence
(Somers et al., 2019). As such, overskilling diminishes employment prospects in the long run, as it undermines incentives for young people to continue investing in human capital
(Cedefop, 2015). Many studies have shown that vulnerable
groups such as migrants, women and young people are more
often trapped in such situations (Cedefop, 2014). For firms,
overskilling indicates an untapped potential that is underutilized or not used at all, lowers productivity as their staff is dissatisfied, and limits their adaptation strategies as overskilled
workers are reluctant to retrain (Brunello & Wruuck, 2021).
On a wider note, for national economies, firms’ lower productivity erodes competitiveness and investments in education
are not returned into the economy (McGuinness et al., 2018).
Various factors shape the widely documented uneven geography of skills and their mismatches (Alamá-Sabater et al.,
2024; Cedefop, 2010). Importantly, regional mismatches can
be greatly affected by the robustness (or lack thereof) of the
broader national economy. In underdeveloped countries, for
instance, talent is more starkly concentrated in spatial and
sectoral terms, leading to greater cross-sectoral asymmetries in
productivity (Porzio, 2017). In contrast, in developed countries,
which are characterized by more dynamic economies, talent
is more optimally allocated; however, for this to occur
institutional barriers have to be low (Allen et al., 2013). At the
regional level, the dynamism of an economy is equally crucial.
Capital- and technology-intensive activities tend to concentrate in already advanced regions, fueling new rounds of

uneven development (Viñuela-Jiménez et al., 2010). As such,
metropolitan areas with a diverse economic base concentrate
human capital, cutting-edge technology, and finance-related
activities, while economically backward and declining regions
become even more disconnected from knowledge and innovation (Feldman et al., 2021). Similarly, developed industrial
regions tend to attract new activities, workforce, and capital, as they further industrialise (Boudeville, 1968), although
in many cases they also spill over positive effects to their
surrounding areas (Perroux, 1955). However, in most cases,
while the latter regions enter virtuous cycles of regional growth,
others—mainly peripheral, rural and less developed regions
(MacKinnon et al., 2022)—are locked in vicious cycles due
to outdated technology, institutional weaknesses and other
factors (Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023). In the above, apart from
the dynamism of a regional industrial structure, the sectoral
specialization per se plays a key role. Characteristically, sectors like accommodation and catering, commerce and storage, and transportation, are reported to suffer greater skill
mismatches—both horizontal and vertical—than others
(Chłoń-Domińczak & Żurawski, 2017).
Having said the above, even within regions where significant
volumes of high-skilled workers are employed, labour markets
have become increasingly polarized, as different fractions of
capital seek for both cheap and skilled workers (CapsadaMunsech & Valiente, 2020; Teixeira, 2022). Such segmentation is crucially sustained by dual human capital development
mechanisms. Namely, next to a robust education system, a
training system that focuses more on vocational training and
apprenticeship initiatives is formed, serving as a channel for
precarious labour (Teixeira, 2019). Nevertheless, dual educational systems such as Germany’s have also been praised for
their positive effects in school-to-work transitions, albeit having been oversimplified in the relevant literature (Deissinger,
2015). Similarly praised is the universalistic regime in Nordic
countries like Denmark, which invest in human capital and
match high levels of educational attainment with demand for
such workers (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019). Beyond educational
systems, a number of policy and governance factors can shape
youth transition regimes. Indicatively, the role of institutions
and stakeholders in counselling and supporting graduates ameliorates or exacerbates spatial disparities in skill mismatches
(Ali, 2014); in statist regimes, for instance, employers are not
sufficiently involved in matching skills’ demand and supply
(Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020). As such, in the
sub-protective regime of the Mediterranean EU, in countries
like Greece, youth transitions to adulthood are long and problematic (Emmanouil et al., 2023). This leads to a large pool
of highly qualified people who either cannot find a job, have
a profession for which they are overqualified, or have a profession in a different sector from the one in which they were
trained, while at the same time many companies are struggling
to fill their vacancies (Biagi et al., 2020). Similar phenomena
of weak linkages between education and labor market, high
levels of temporary employment, extensive skill mismatches
and low labor demand characterize the post-socialist regime
in countries like Bulgaria (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019).

Beyond the national and regional factors, sudden shocks of
economic or other nature, as well as structural transitions,
directly contribute to increasing skill mismatches (Cedefop,
2015). Indeed, the 2008/2009 Global Crisis led to a concerted slashing of public spending on education, impacting on
human capital profoundly in the following years (Gourzis &
Gialis, 2019). Beyond cuts in public expenses, recession has
been found to severely diminish the capacity of policies that
are already in place to bring significant effect, especially in the
regions lagging behind (Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020;
Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023). On another note, digitalization
is predicted to lead to a significant increase in highly skilled
occupations, particularly in healthcare and digital technology (Lund et al., 2021), while the number of low and
medium-skilled occupations (including many administrative,
clerical and manual jobs) is expected to decline, leading to an
increasing polarization of the labour market (Chiacchio et al.,
2018). Similarly, the green transition will deeply affect jobs
in the fossil fuel sectors, affecting certain labour markets disproportionately (Bednorz & Sadauskaitė, 2022). This stresses
that the study of the uneven geography of skills and their
mismatches is crucially relevant than ever given the new and
challenging conditions facing the labour force. Indeed, the
Great Recession that began in 2008, the Covid pandemic and
the challenges of the twin transitions (the nexus of digitalization and green transformation) have already had and will
continue to have a significant impact on labour markets and
workplace rules, as they lead to structural changes that affect
countries and regions unevenly.
Lastly, it must be mentioned that all the above factors are
often shaped through diachronic processes and path dependencies, which can be region-specific or not. For instance, a
region’s industrial heritage may have shaped a workforce
of high level skills, granting it the capacity to adapt to new
demands for higher value added industrial activities. In contrast, regions that are less advanced, having diachronically
hosted less innovative, technologically backward sectors may
not have this capacity (Calcagnini et al., 2021).
Overall, approaching the equilibrium between the supply and
demand of skills within an “institutional vacuum” context—
namely, detached from processes outside the market and without considering the broader power relations in place (Psilos &
Gereffi, 2011)—is unrealistic. This means that skills and labour
are constantly influenced by broader processes including
demographic and socio-economic changes, institutional settings, and the field of reproduction, which includes family and
peer pressure, cultural practices, etc. (Peck & Smucker, 1998).
The above attempt to outline how the development of skills
is an inherently social process, brought about and maintained
by hierarchical power relations within and outside labour
markets (Galiakberova, 2019). The influence of powerful
non-entrepreneurial actors is key in shaping human capital,
as fittingly described by theorizations around Local Labour
Regimes (Werner, 2019). Similarly, labour force development
is not an equal opportunity field, but rather depends on characteristics such as class, race, gender, ethnicity and migrant

status. In fact, labour force development plays a key role in
perpetuating—not only social inequalities but also—labour
market segmentation (Teixeira, 2022). The above imply that
many reproducing mechanisms of the geography of skill
mismatches fuel spatial inequalities by favouring economic stability and growth for certain regions at the expense of others
(Teixeira, 2022).

Methodological remarks

The aim of this paper is to study and contextualize the uneven
geography of skill mismatches, focusing on highly educated
workers. To do so, it first calculates the concentrations of workers with high levels of education in low, medium, and high
skill-level occupations. The focus on the workers with high
education levels serves in examining the contested issue of
overskilling (Brunello & Wruuck, 2021). The severe short- and
long-term consequences of overskilling, as indicated in the literature, underline the high scientific importance and policy
relevance of this objective. Then, to contextualize the observed
uneven geography of skill mismatches, the paper employs
a simple correlation analysis, considering various economic,
demographic, and spatial metrics that represent crucial
underlying factors.
The analysis is conducted at the regional level (NUTS-2) and
its geographical scope covers five countries: Greece, Bulgaria,
France, Germany, and Denmark. These were chosen on the

basis that they represent the main educational/employment
regimes in Europe: the universalistic of the Nordic countries, the
employment-centered of Continental Europe, the sub-protective
of the Mediterranean countries, and the post-socialist of
Eastern Europe (Farkas, 2011; Pohl & Walther 2007). While
both France and Germany fall under the Continental model, they
are both analyzed due to their stark differences in terms of their
wage structures (Hein & Vogel, 2009) and educational models
(O’Reilly et al., 2019). Given the research’s geographical scope
covering all main European regimes pertaining to employment
and skills, its findings can be contextualized more broadly
and provide insight into the EU as a whole. At various points,
we focus on the six regions that are specifically related to
the ReERUA Horizon Project, in the frame of which the analysis at hand is conducted. These are the North and South
Aegean regions in Greece, Île-de-France in France, Yugozapaden
in Bulgaria, Freiburg in Germany, and Sjlland in Denmark
(see Figure 1). These regions place particular emphasis on
the knowledge that comes from their educational institutions;
at the same time, they are developing strong knowledge networks within the ReERUA consortium, despite having very
different socio-economic structures. As such, they constitute
interesting case studies, especially when seen within their
broader context.

essentially allows for a comparison between the onset of the

Our analysis above highlighted the uneven geography of skill
and education levels’ matching across the regions in five countries

To better contextualise our findings, we also conducted a
correlation analysis between the concentrations of all types of
skill (mis)matches and various metrics that the relevant literature has indicated as significant. Drawing upon our main findings, here we discuss the most crucial factors underlying the
uneven geography of skill mismatches. In this framework, we put
particular emphasis on the economic dynamism of national
economies, regional industrial structures, urbanization, local
institutional structures and practices, and demographic structures, drawing from the main findings of the relevant literature
(Alamá-Sabater et al., 2024; Cedefop, 2010).
First, there are multiple indications that the dynamism of
national economies is a prime determining factor for the spatially uneven distribution of human capital and skills’ matching. In our study, this dynamism’s role in human capital
allocation is reflected in the negative correlation between
national GDP and LQM. Indeed, robust national economies,
both in terms of total and per capita GDP (Germany, Denmark,

France), were found to overall exhibit more notable concentrations of highly educated workers in high skill positions than
less dynamic ones (Bulgaria, Greece). Additionally, a closer
look reveals that the former countries have oriented their production towards dynamic sectors comprising high added
value manufacturing, financial services, and other specialized
B2B services, in a more decisive way than the latter ones
(Herod et al., 2021). As such, beyond exhibiting more efficient skill matching, countries with dynamic sectoral specializations concentrate much higher shares of a high-skilled
workforce in the first place (Lee & Rodriguez-Pose, 2013).
The reason that some countries can attract or develop a workforce with a higher skill set whereas others struggle to follow
cannot be seen independently of a broader context pertaining
to their positioning within global value chains (henceforth
GVCs). The very constitution of “sequential structures” such as
GVCs implies uneven development: some countries get to host
the strong links of these chains—i.e., the activities of higher
value added which demand skilled labour—whereas the rest

are left with the weaker ones (Smichowski et al., 2016).
Within a value chain, a higher value added is typically attributed to the initial-stage and the later-stage activities, whereas
those in the middle are lower value added (forming a “smiley
face” curve; Mohiuddin & Su, 2014). Specifically, activities
which come first in production chains relate to the standardization, innovation, and design of a product, activities coming
afterwards include the manufacturing and assembly of a product, and activities that come last relate to logistics, marketing,
and branding (Fróes de Borja Reis & Barroso de Souza, 2018).
As such, it is commonly accepted that already advanced economies achieve a better positioning within these complex structures. This brings upon them a series of positive outcomes,
including being able to improve productivity through attracting
high-skilled labour (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011). Moreover,
this positioning is clearly reflected in country-level differences in education systems, which allows benefited countries
to better develop high-skilled labour in the first place. As mentioned above, the countries that we study follow different educational paths that have a direct impact on the labour market.
For example, the universalistic (e.g.Denmark) regime with high
investments in education and fast and stable school-to-work
transitions is completely different from countries of the
sub-protective regime (e.g. Greece), which display severe skills
mismatches and low labour demand in general (Hadjivassiliou
et al., 2019).
Beyond the national scale, positioning within GVCs translates in profoundly different ways at the regional level as
well (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011). Characteristically, stark
inequalities among regions regarding skill and education matching were found in Germany and France despite being strong
national economies overall, while instances of a great matching
were identified within lagging national economies, as in the
case of Bulgaria’s and Greece’s capital regions (Yugozapaden
& Attica respectively). For one, this observation underlines
how robust regions within a less robust national context
over-concentrate capital and activities at the expense of their
surrounding regions. Such a case is Yugozapaden in Bulgaria,
with an LQH score of over 1.31 in 2021. Interestingly, the same
applies even in Germany, France, and Denmark, although to
a lesser extent. Indicatively, LQH was found to be particularly high in Berlin (Germany) and Hovedstaden (Denmark),
while many regions in these countries did not perform as well.
The above helps us to—at least partially—refute theoretical
and empirical postulations around the spillover effect of
growth poles (see Florida et al., 2008).
Overall, the above highlights the importance of regional industrial specializations and innovation capacity, which are
deeply interconnected. Specifically, beyond the correlation
between LQH and regional GDP, our analysis documented
the statistical significance of the Innovation Index’s scores.
As shown, then, not only is the size of a regional economy or
the standard of living of its citizens important in the optimal
allocation of human capital, but also regional innovation

performance. Regions which for example host innovation
research centres or constitute strong logistic centres are positioned in better terms within the global geography of skills,
whereas those which constitute manufacturing/assembly centres are positioned in worse terms (Cedefop, 2018), except for
the case of particularly high added value products (e.g., biotech;
Mohiuddin & Su, 2014). In our analysis, regions standing out
in terms of matching highly educated workers with high skill
occupations included Île-de-France, Berlin in Germany, and
Hovedstaden in Denmark. All of the above constitute innovative
regions with a high GDP per capita; for instance, Île-de-France
is an innovation leader at a pan-EU level (European
Commission, 2022). Similarly, Yugozapaden, which also exhibited a notable high skill/education matching, has taken significant steps towards economic modernization and technological
improvement in recent years (European Commission, ibid).
Nevertheless, regions cannot upgrade within GVCs completely
detached from their broader national context, especially if
this refers to an obsolete domestic economic system (Gereffi
& Lee, 2016). This postulation is indicated by the concentrations of highly skilled workers in middle skill level
occupations, which reflects some extent of skill mismatching. Specifically, all Greek regions displayed relevant
over-concentrations, with Attica standing out among all study
regions. This reflects the weaker productive structures of
Greece as a whole as well as its regions. For instance, the North
Aegean specializes in small-scale agriculture, while the other
insular regions in the country overfocus on tourism (Herod
et al., 2022). Even the country’s capital region is only a
moderate innovator, lagging significantly in terms of R&D
spending (European Commission, 2022). Apart from tourism,
there are many manufacturing- and assembly-related activities
along GVCs that favor medium skill-level occupations and
therefore generate tendencies towards a disproportionate amount
of highly educated workers in such occupations. Such is the
case with most French regions, and especially the agri-food
heavy Franche-Comté.
Of course, beyond a current positioning within broader GVCs,
these industrial specializations at the national and regional
level also reflect a historical context, including the effect of
long structural transformations and abrupt crises. Specifically,
in the aftermath of the crisis of Fordist industrialization during the 1970s, regions in Northern and Central Europe took
a turn toward technologically advanced sectors; in contrast,
being unable to maintain their export capacity, peripheral
regions across Southern Europe saw their industrial systems dismantled and resorted to less innovative, constructions-driven
growth models (Herod et al., 2021). Moreover, the 2008/09
Global Crisis put a heavy strain on the peripheral parts of the
EU and led to the burst of the real estate bubbles they had
developed, decimating their construction activity (Gourzis &
Gialis, 2019). However, having their industrial structures already
dismantled, these regions followed a course further into deindustrialization, gradually developing a heavy dependence
on tourism activities (Herod et al., 2022). As a result, their

economic base in recent years comprises a mix of less innovative, technologically backward sectors such as construction,
retail, and low value-added market services (Fana et al., 2016).
In further examining the determinants of skill (mis)matches,
closely related to industrial specializations is the extent of
urbanization. As the analysis revealed, there is a positive
moderate correlation between the LQH and the population density, showing that urbanization is important in human capital
allocation. Regions with dynamic metropolitan areas typically
abound in occupations requiring higher skill levels, thus,
demand for skilled labour is high there (Psycharis et al., 2014);
on the other end, urban environments often constitute more
attractive environments for a high skilled and creative labour
force as they are characterized by more amenities—cultural,
transportations-wise, choices for education, etc.—and lower
entry barriers for newcomers (Florida et al., 2008). This is
rather expected, since large urban agglomerations act as dominant poles, accumulating population and capital, concentrating the better part of domestic infrastructure, and attracting
foreign investments (Perroux, 1955), eventually fueling processes of self-sustained growth (Polyzos, 2015). Nevertheless,
our analysis highlighted a mild positive correlation between
population density and perfect skill matching (LQH), as well
as no negative correlation between density and LQL and LQM.
This indicates that, instead of metropolitan regions in general, it was mostly the capital regions within each country that
exhibited a good performance in skill matching. In fact, many
metropolitan regions were found to simultaneously display
over-concentrations of highly educated people in low-skill occupations, as for instance Île-de-France. This is a particularly
important observation, as it implies deep cutting inequalities
and fierce competition even within the most dynamic regional
labour markets, which have in fact exacerbated during the
2010s recession (Herod et al., 2021). Indeed, the relevant literature has noted that urbanized regions exhibit highly polarized labour markets (Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020).
Namely, besides the core of well-qualified people working in
high skill occupations, there is a periphery of precarious workers undertaking menial tasks (Teixeira, 2022). Moreover, with
super-star cities within robust national economies and capital
cities within smaller national economies concentrating a larger
highly educated workforce than what their labour markets can
absorb, and thus channeling it to lower skill-level occupations,
the simultaneous high scores in LQH and LQL cannot be seen as a
paradox.
Nevertheless, it is not central metropolitan areas that were
found to exhibit the starkest overskilling (LQL) but those around
them. Aside from some robust regions then, overskilling is so
widespread that one out of four university graduates in the
EU works in jobs requiring lesser skills than what they have
acquired through their education (Cedefop, 2018). Moreover,
this phenomenon is persisting, as skill mismatches in most
peripheral regions do not solely emerge in times of economic
turbulence, but they constitute diachronic traits (Iammarino
et al., 2019); this is despite approaches such as HCT postulating that overskilling cannot persist in the long run and

that it is automatically balanced out by market mechanisms
(McGuinness & Pouliakas, 2016). Even worse, beyond being
unable to attract high skilled labour so as to cover their needs,
most peripheral regions also appear unable to keep the one
they have developed (Florida et al., 2008). In this context,
apart from extensive overskilling, they also suffer from extensive underskilling as well (Lee & Rodriguez-Pose, 2013). As
such, they enter a vicious cycle of deepening skill mismatches. This self-regenerating vulnerability comes in stark
antithesis with the virtuous cycle of self-sustained growth in
dynamic regions. Combined, these cycles make clear that—in
the absence of decisive institutional interventions—spatial
inequalities tend to deepen instead of being ameliorated over
time (Teixeira, 2022).
Therefore, the link between peripherality and skill mismatches
highlights the importance of local institutional structures and
practices, which are particularly weaker in peripheral regions
(Herod et al., 2022). Weak market and state institutions bring
a series of consequences. For one, they can influence entrepreneurial activities (Junaid et al., 2022). In fact, “institutional
sclerosis”, namely entrenched interest groups within weak institutions, has long been accused of impeding economic growth
by resisting changes that could disrupt their established benefits, and leading to economic stagnation and less innovation
(Olson, 1982). Moreover, weak institutions lead to “information
asymmetries” among graduates, leading to poor career choices
(McGuinness et al., 2018). In our analysis, although an indicator of effective governance was not included, proxies such
as GDP and the Innovation Index were found to be positively
correlated with LQH and negatively with LQM.
One last decisive difference between urbanized and rural areas
that has to be taken under consideration is their demographic
structure. Our analysis revealed a negative correlation between
the LQH and the Ageing Index and a mild positive correlation between the LQM and the Dependency Index. However,
the relationship between allocation of talent and demographics is more complex. What cannot be reflected in our
analysis—and most other secondary ones—is that older workers generally lose their incentive to retrain over time, with
the introduction of new technologies rendering their skill sets
obsolete (McGuinness et al., 2018). At the same time, the
weight of meeting skill demand falls upon the shoulders of
those entering the labour market, who must develop the appropriate competencies (Cedefop, 2015). Amid intensified competition, younger people are further pushed to invest in their
training (Brunello & Wruuck, 2021), with education being
their main defence mechanism in retaining their place in the
job market (Sala, 2011). As such, younger labour markets are
not necessarily functioning better in terms of talent allocation.

Conclusions

This study examined the issue of human capital allocation at
the regional level across five European countries, which represent key employment models within the EU. Our findings
highlighted the uneven geography of skill mismatches, as capital and metropolitan regions were observed to be more efficient

than less affluent and peripheral ones in matching supply
(in terms of education attainment level) and demand (in terms
of demanded skill-level in occupations), especially when it
comes to upper-level skills. To further decipher this remark,
we discussed the role of five interrelated factors: economic
dynamism at the national level, regional sectoral specializations, urbanization, institutional structures, and demographic
trends. In this framework, we briefly discussed the role of
regions’ positioning within broader structures such as GVCs.
Importantly, our analysis pinpointed that human capital is
shaped by multiple forces at the same time, most of which
relate with each other in a dialectical way. Furthermore, we
identified a series of self-sustained, regenerating processes of
resilience and vulnerability, i.e., virtuous and vicious cycles.
Ultimately, we found human capital to be of the utmost importance in regional development. Although it can generate spatial growth, its concentration in certain areas leaves lagging
regions out of growth pathways.
To break this pattern of uneven development, policy interventions are required. Thus, here we recommend a series of actions
drawing upon the five factors discussed above, while taking into account the EU’s “New Skills Agenda”. Our recommendations have been tailored for the regions associated with
the ERUA but can be applied to other contexts as well, given
that these regions represent Europe’s key employment regimes.
Looking at the local institutional structures and practices, in
the regions with strong research and academic activity like the
ones related to the consortium, it is important that their universities work together with stakeholders from industry to spill
over knowledge and cover the research and innovation
needs of the labour market. In this frame, learning events for
reskilling and upskilling in emerging sectors—e.g., green
skills—are necessary. In addition, high quality career counselling is key, especially for vulnerable groups such as the youth,
women, and migrants, for not only making sensible early career
decisions but also in overcoming career challenges throughout their lives (Papakota, 2016). In fact, as noted above, such
career counseling is more than necessary in peripheral regions,
where career choices are less informed due to “information
asymmetries” (McGuinness et al., 2018). In this context,
educational structures such as schools, universities or vocational training centres should respond to the needs of students
at different stages of career development by identifying skills,
experiences, values and personality traits of graduates, technological trends and employment opportunities, employers’
expectations and career planning (Litoiu & Oproiu, 2012).
Lastly, tighter networks of social, scientific, and policy-related
stakeholders should be encouraged, so that through joint
actions they engage in labour market forecasting and early
identification of skill shortages.
Regarding the spatially uneven distribution of human capital due
to national economies’ dynamism (or lack thereof) or regional
industrial specializations, it can be addressed through targeted investment incentives and guided industrial reorientation.
Governments can rechannel entrepreneurial activity into
underdeveloped regions through financial incentives like tax

breaks, grants and subsidies. More crucially, countries and
regions with economies entirely oriented towards medium and
low-skill sectors (e.g., tourism) that generate mainly low-quality
jobs, should pursue a re-specialization in more dynamic
high value-added and high-skill sectors (Emmanouil et al.,
2024); this should reduce spatial and talent inequalities. Furthermore, our analysis showed that high skills increase
inversely with population ageing and are positively associated
with the degree of urbanization; as such, investment in education, training, and continuous learning, or policies promoting economic resilience and adaptability, may not be sufficient
in rural and underdeveloped regions. This highlights the need
for a move away from a horizontal implementation of policies, which is the case with many EU initiatives such as the
Youth Guarantee (Emmanouil et al., ibid). Instead, policies
should be spatially sensitive so as to keep young people with
high levels of education in non-urban and/or peripheral regions,
where they will work in high-skill occupations and put their
human capital in good use. Taking into account the specific
needs of regions, promoting innovation, incentivizing investment, and sharing best practices, can therefore promote
balanced economic growth and reduce inequalities in the
geographical distribution of skills.
