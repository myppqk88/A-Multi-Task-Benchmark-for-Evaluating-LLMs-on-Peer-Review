Abstract
25
Introduction: Hope (hoping) is most commonly assessed as a dispositional trait and associated 
26
with quality of life, self-care agency and non-attempts of suicide. However, little research has 
27
been conducted on hoping for specific events. 
28
Materials and Methods: We distributed a survey consisting of Integrative Hope Scale (IHS) 
29
and visual analogue scales on which respondents could declare their levels (intensity) of hope for 
30
specific events, to all first year health students enrolled at the University Department of Health 
31
Studies, Split, Croatia in 2011/12, as well as to working health professionals attending a nursing 
32
conference in April 2012. 
33
Results: A total of 161 (89.4%) students and 88 (89.8%) working health professionals returned 
34
the completed questionnaires. We found high generalized hope scores of students and working 
35
health professionals (Md=111, 95% CI 109-113 vs. Md=115, 95% CI 112-119; U=5353, 
36
P=0.065), and weak to moderate correlations of trait and specific hopes (r=0.18 to 0.48, 
37
Spearman's rank correlation coefficient). Students and workers reported 31 different things they 
38
hoped for most in life, of which the most prevalent were being healthy and happy. There was 
39
very little agreement between participants’ perceived influence of the four factors compromising 
40
the trait hope (self-confidence, ambition, optimism, and social support) on the levels of their 
41
specific hopes. 
42
Conclusions: Our findings, while strengthening the validity of hope as a trait, indicate that 
43
specific hopes of individuals are moderated by factors not captured by the IHS trait scale. Further 
44
research should explore specific hoping in detail, as well as the effectiveness of interventions 
45
aimed at increasing specific or generalized hoping.

Introduction 
47
Hope (hoping) is regarded as the earliest and the most indispensable virtue inherent in the state 
48
of being alive (Erikson, 1964). It is the central tenet of religions, especially Christianity 
49
(Benedict XVI, 2006, Titus 1:2, 2011), and an indispensable companion of illness and healing. It 
50
accompanies researchers during their scientific discoveries and individuals during their 
51
tribulations. Hope has been a popular topic in literature and arts, ever since its entrapment in 
52
Pandora’s box (White, 1914); and recently, it has become the topic of growing research in the 
53
fields of positive psychology, philosophy, nursing and medicine (Cutcliffe and Herth, 2002, 
54
Schrank et al., 2008, Smith, 2012, Snyder et al., 1996). Hope has been positively correlated with 
55
quality of life (Evangelista et al., 2003), self-care agency (Alberto and Joyner, 2008), caregiver 
56
burden (Zink Jadaa, 2008), and non-attempts of suicide (Meadows et al., 2005). However, its 
57
measurement is still a topic of great debate (Lopez and Snyder, 2003, Schrank et al., 2008). In 
58
short, although hope is widely perceived as something that can be higher for one object or event 
59
than for another, thresholds and norms for specific hopes in populations have not been explored 
60
or measured. Researchers have instead focused on measuring dispositional (trait) hope, defined 
61
as hoping that applies across situations and times; or even more specifically on measuring state 
62
hope, a person’s current hoping disposition (Lopez and Snyder, 2003, Snyder et al., 1996). With 
63
more than 32 published instruments for the measurement of hope, Schrank et al., in 2011, have 
64
developed an Integrative Hope Scale (IHS) by combining properties of the most commonly used 
65
hope scales in published research (Miller Hope Scale, Herth Hope Index, Snyder Hope Scale) 
66
(Schrank et al., 2011). Using that scale, it was the goal of our research to determine the 
67
association of trait hope with specific hopes measured by visual analogue scales, as well as the 
68
perceived influence of the four factors compromising the IHS scale (confidence, positive future 
69
orientation, lack of perspective, social relations) on individuals’ specific hopes. 
70
Materials and Methods
71
Questionnaire
72
The English version of the IHS had been translated into Croatian by the authors and then back 
73
translated by an independent language expert to confirm its validity. Four items were 
74
reformulated in the process. Alongside demographical questions on age and sex, we also asked 
75
the participants to declare the level (intensity) of their specific hopes on the visual analog scale 
76
(VAS), graded from 0 to 100 (with every 10 intervals marked), for different events: finishing 
77
their studies in time and being healthy at the age of 60. We then asked the respondents to name 
78
(using an open ended question) what they hope for most in life, and to designate the level of 
79
hope for their stated goal. Following each of the VAS questions we also asked the respondents to 
80
list four factors: self-confidence, ambition, optimism, and social support; from most to least 
81
contributing to their previously stated level of hope (Appendix 1). We chose these four factors 
82
based on the IHS subscales: ‘trust and confidence’, ‘positive future orientation’, ‘lack of 
83
perspective’, ‘social relations and personal value’(Schrank et al., 2011). As we aimed for 
84
positive factors, we found that ‘optimism’ best captures the inverse of the ‘lack of perspective’ 
85
subscale.
86
Sampling
87
We used two-stage convenience sampling of two different age groups of health professionals. 
88
First, as a pilot study and with an assumption that the level of hope declared on the VAS or IHS 
89
could be influenced by the order by which examinees filled out these questionnaires, we 
90
randomized all first year students of health studies at the University of Split (who enrolled in 
91
their first year of studies in 2011/2012) into two groups: the 1st group was given the IHS 
92
questionnaire followed by the VAS, while the 2nd group was first given the VAS followed by the 
93
IHS. A simple random number generator was used for random allocation to the groups. As we 
94
found no evidence that the order of presenting questionnaires influenced either IHS or VAS 
95
scores (Supplementary Table 1), in further analysis we treated both groups as one. As our joint 
96
sample was age-homogeneous, in order to test the effect of age on IHS or VAS scores, we 
97
recruited working health professionals who attended the Education for lecturers of nursing 
98
courses in April 2012, Split. All of the working health professionals were given a questionnaire 
99
in which the IHS questionnaire was printed first. Cronbach’s alpha of the CIHS for both groups 
100
combined was 0.869 (95% CI=0.843-0.892) showing good internal consistency. 
101
Statistical analysis
102
Frequencies and percentages were used for the description of categorical variables, and median 
103
(Md) and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normal distributions. The Mann-Whitney U test was 
104
used to assess the difference in medians between the groups, while the chi-square test was used 
105
to compare frequency distributions of categorical variables. Correlations between the CIHS total 
106
and subscale scores with VAS scores were assessed by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. 
107
Concordance of the ranking order with which the participants graded factors which influenced 
108
their hope levels were determined using Kendall's coefficient. The level of significance for all 
109
statistical tests was 0.05. Data was analyzed with SPSS statistical package 19.0 (SPSS; Chicago, 
110
Illinois, USA).

112
The study was approved by the ethical review board of University of Split, Croatia (no. 003-

08/11-03/0005).

Results
115
A total of 161 (89.4%) students of first year health studies (132 women, 26 men, missing data for 
116
3 respondents) participated in the study, as well as 88 (89.8%) working health professionals 
117
attending a nursing conference (86 women, 2 men). The students were 18-47 years old, with a 
118
median age of 19 (IQR=19-21), and the workers were 22-70 years old, with a median age of 48 
119
(IQR=38-52).
120
There was no significant difference between the two groups in their IHS total score (Md=111, 
121
95% CI 109-113 vs. Md=115, 95% CI 112-119; U=5353, P=0.065). However, workers had 
122
higher scores on the IHS’ ‘trust and confidence’ and ‘social relations and personal value’ 
123
subscale scores, as well as higher levels (designated on VAS) on being healthy at the age of 60 
124
and the things they most hoped for in life (Table 1). 
125
Sex differences were observed for the student population, with males reporting higher hopes for 
126
being healthy at the age of 60 (U=1153.5, p=0.009). For both groups, trait hope (measured by the 
127
IHS) showed a significant strength of correlation (r=0.18 to 0.48) with specific hopes measured 
128
by the VAS (Table 2).
129
When answering an open ended question on what their most hoped-for thing in life was, students 
130
and workers listed 1 to 5 answers (concepts), with no differences between the groups on the 
131
number of concepts they listed (Md=1, 95% CI 1-2 vs. Md=2, 95% CI 1-2, U=5373, P=0.169). 
132
Cumulatively, 31 most hoped-for concepts emerged, with health and happiness being the most 
133
prevalent in both groups. However, the frequency distribution of individual concepts showed 
134
several significant differences, with students hoping more for health, work and family, while 
135
workers for life contentment (Table 3). 
136
After designating levels of hope on the VAS scales participants rated 4 factors (self-confidence, 
137
ambition, optimism, and social support) according to how much they contributed to their levels 
138
of hope. The same order of the factors was listed by 23 (14.3%) students, and 0 (0%) workers. 
139
The ranking order of the factors between different participants showed very little agreement, 
140
even when participants with highest or lower trait hope scores were analyzed separately 
141
(Kendall's W from 0.024 to 0.117; Supplementary Table 2). 
142
Of the four factors, optimism was most commonly chosen by the participants of both groups as 
143
the factor which gave the most strength (intensity) to their level of hope for being healthy at the 
144
age of 60, as well as for the their most hoped-for thing in life (χ2=2.632, P=0.004 and χ2=6.438, 
145
P=0.09, respectively). On the other hand, no single factor was chosen by the students as that 
146
which contributes most to their hope of finishing studies in time, but rather all 4 factors (self-
147
confidence, ambition, optimism, and social support) were represented in equal measure 
148
(χ2=6.903, P=0.075,  Supplementary Table 3). 
149
In order to see if the factor which individuals chose as most influential to their specific hopes 
150
was identical to the highest scored factor on their trait (IHS) hope, we ranked the IHS subscales 
151
scores of each individual from highest to the lowest. This resulted in ambition (positive future 
152
orientation) being expressed as the strongest factor of the four for both groups of participants 
153
(Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).
154
Discussion
155
Our study showed that there were no differences between total scores of trait hope between 
156
training and working health professionals; and that trait hope is weakly to moderately correlated 
157
with health professionals’ specific personal hopes. Together these findings strengthen the 
158
validity of hope as a human trait, and imply its stability in adult population, as demonstrated by 
159
Schrank et al. on the general population of Austria (Schrank et al., 2011). The IHS scores in our 
160
sample were however higher than those found in Austria suggesting either cultural or quality of 
161
life differences, or even the specifics of the caring profession which our sample was based on. 
162
Averill et al. have shown that religion, specifically Judeo-Christian influences on the Western 
163
nations, compared to Confucianism influences on the Eastern nations, left a profound influence 
164
on both the conceptual grasping and importance of hope (Averill et al., 1990). Although there is 
165
a higher percentage of declared Catholic population (86.28%) in Croatia than in Austria 
166
(73.66%) (Croatian bureau of statistics, 2013, Statistics Austria, 2001), neither the Schrank et al. 
167
study (Schrank et al., 2011) nor our study, checked for religious orientation, requiring that these 
168
differences be explored in further studies.      
169
Higher levels of the subscales ‘trust and confidence’ and ’social relations and personal value’ of 
170
working health professionals in our study compared to those of the student population, most 
171
likely result from age specific developmental characteristics and family status. Similarly, the 
172
differences observed in the most hoped-for things in life for these two populations could 
173
originate from the higher number of individuals within the working population who have already 
174
achieved their hopes and goals for work and family, and are therefore more oriented toward life 
175
contentment and spiritual fulfillment. Workers’ higher levels of hope for being healthy at the age 
176
of sixty could result from the facts that our sample consisted only of an active working 
177
population and that the workers were also closer to the 60 year-mark, meaning that they could, 
178
based on their age and health so far, better evaluate their future health. Our findings of male 
179
students having higher hopes for being healthy at the age of 60 could originate from observed 
180
gender differences in the perception of health (Suris et al., 1997), yet, as our sample included 
181
only a small number of male students (n=26) this difference needs to be confirmed in further 
182
studies. 
183
Our study also adds further support for hope being an emotion that can be expressed and 
184
recollected (Smith, 2012), as the most hoped for things in life our participants listed are almost 
185
identical to those in the Averill’s study one of analysis of hope (Averill et al., 1990),  in which, 
186
wanting to “eliminate” abstract hopes, Averill et al. asked participants to name events in the 
187
previous year when they specifically hoped for something (after they had also been asked to 
188
explain and provide examples of differences between wanting or desiring something, and hoping 
189
for something).
190
We acknowledge that our sample was not random; however, it was not the goal of this 
191
study to determine hope norms for the Croatian population, nor have such studies on hope been 
192
conducted anywhere in the world. Likewise, the most hoped-for things in life listed by the 
193
students and workers of health professions should not be taken as representative, outside perhaps 
194
health professions, as hopes and life goals depend on a multitude of factors, including those 
195
intrinsic, generational, social and cultural (Grouzet et al., 2005, Twenge et al., 2012). 
196
The positive association we found between a person’s trait hope and their levels of hope 
197
for different specific events, coupled with the weak to moderate strength of those correlations 
198
(r=0.18 to 0.48) and the differences in which individuals ranked 4 factors compromising trait 
199
hope (self-confidence, ambition, optimism, and social support) according to how much they 
200
contributed to their levels (intensity) of hope for specific events (Kendall’s W from 0.002 to 
201
0.15), indicates that specific hopes of individuals are moderated and perhaps even more strongly 
202
influenced by factors not captured by the IHS trait instrument. As determination and increased 
203
goal oriented actions are invoked by the changes in the intensity of hope(ing) for that goal 
204
(Averill et al., 1990), further research should focus on determining the most influential factors 
205
for specific hopes and the effectiveness of interventions that aim to increase specific or 
206
generalized hoping.
