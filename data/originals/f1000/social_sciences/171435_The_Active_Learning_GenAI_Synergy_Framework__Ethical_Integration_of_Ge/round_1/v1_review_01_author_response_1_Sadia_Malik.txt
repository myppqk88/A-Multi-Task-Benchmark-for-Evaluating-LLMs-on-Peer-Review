Author Response to Review 1 (v1)
Author: Sadia Malik
Date: 2026-03-03
============================================================

1.
Reviewer Comment:
The ethical and governance discussion does not engage with major, current GenAI frameworks, particularly the EU AI Act, UNESCO guidance for generative AI in education and research, and the Safe AI in Education Manifesto.
Response:
This limitation is acknowledged, and Section 2.5 has been strengthened by incorporating discussion of the EU AI Act (2024), UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education and research (2023), and Safe AI frameworks, demonstrating how the independently developed ethical protocol converges with these international standards while providing practical implementation strategies for resource-constrained contexts where such frameworks offer limited operational guidance. (See p. 6, Section 2.5; p. 9, Section 3.5; p. 19, Section 5.5)
2.
Reviewer Comment:
Although the mixed-methods, quasi-experimental pre-/post-test design is appropriate for classroom research, the core comparison is confounded because experimental and control groups are intact classes from different degree programmes, threatening internal validity and requiring stronger controls or reframing of claims as exploratory.
Response:
This is a valid methodological concern regarding the quasi-experimental design. The reviewer correctly identifies that using intact classes from different degree programs (Digital Marketing vs. BBA) introduces potential confounding variables that could threaten internal validity. The claims are reframed as exploratory. (See p. 8, Section 3.3)
3.
Reviewer Comment:
Baseline equivalence between groups is insufficiently reported, including pre-test distributions, prior English proficiency, and possible teacher effects.
Response:
Baseline equivalence reporting has been strengthened. Pre-test analysis confirmed no significant differences in writing performance (U = 2,561, p = .532), prior English proficiency (t = 0.58, p = .564), gender distribution (χ² = 1.89, p = .169), or age (t = 0.73, p = .468). Both groups had equivalent admission requirements (IELTS 5.5) and qualified instructors. Section 3.3 now includes comprehensive baseline data with score distributions and demographic characteristics.  (See pp. 8-9, Section 3.3)
4.
Reviewer Comment:
Key replication details are missing or under-specified, including exact AI tools and versions, access conditions, prompt templates, permitted and forbidden uses, ethical reflection workflows, and the weekly active-learning sequence.
Response:
Replication transparency was enhanced in Sections 3.3 and 3.5 through documentation of participant characteristics, instructional conditions, ethical integration protocols, and the AI tools used (ChatGPT, Claude AI, Meta AI, Canva). Although full prompt templates are provided through the Zenodo repository and in the appendices, guided instructional procedures and ethical usage principles are clearly described, supporting pedagogical transferability in comparable ESL contexts. (See p. 8, Section 3.3; p. 9, Section 3.4; p. 10, Section 3.5)
5.
Reviewer Comment:
Writing assessment procedures lack sufficient detail, including rubric criteria and levels, scoring procedures, rater training, inter-rater reliability, and handling of missing data; additionally, “ethical awareness” is not clearly operationalised or measured beyond reflection prompts.
Response:
Section 3.6 now details assessment procedures: 100-point analytic rubric (coherence, organization, grammar, critical thinking—25% each), two trained raters, inter-rater reliability κ = 0.84, third-rater adjudication for discrepancies. Ethical awareness operationalized through coded reflection quality (4-point scale), AI disclosure accuracy, and interview analysis, showing progression from M=1.8 to M=3.4 across 15 weeks. (See p. 11, Section 3.6)
6.
Reviewer Comment:
The qualitative methodology requires clearer reporting of interview sampling, interview protocol, transcription process, coding procedures, and coding reliability.
Response:
Qualitative methodology is detailed in Sections 3.6 and 3.8. Purposive sampling selected eight participants (diverse performance levels, gender balanced). Semi-structured interviews (30-45 min) explored five domains; audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and accuracy-checked. Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase thematic analysis was applied; two researchers independently coded 25% (κ = 0.82). Interview protocol added to Appendix 7. (See p. 12, Section 3.6; p. 12, Section 3.8)
7.
Reviewer Comment:
The use of non-parametric tests is reasonable; however, statistical reporting is incomplete, as effect sizes are described qualitatively without specifying metrics, and repeated intermediate testing raises concerns about multiple comparisons without clear justification or control.
Response:
Effect sizes are now reported using rank-biserial correlation (r): experimental r = 0.68 (large), control r = 0.23 (small). Intermediate tests served as developmental tracking; Bonferroni correction (α = .008) was applied retrospectively—only Tests 5-6 retained significance (r = 0.72, r = 0.58), confirming that the findings are robust and not inflated by multiple comparisons. All tables updated with effect size metrics.  (See Tables 6-9, pp. 13-14 Sections 4.1.1-4.1.3)
8.
Reviewer Comment:
The discussion sometimes implies causality and broad generalisability despite design confounds; alternative explanations, contextual limitations, and stronger triangulation between quantitative results and qualitative findings are needed.
Response:
Discussion revised to eliminate causal language ("suggests," "associated with" replacing "demonstrates," "proves"). Alternative explanations related to programme-level confounds and intact class design have been explicitly acknowledged in the manuscript. Contextual limitations explicitly stated (Pakistani context, specific tools, 15-week timeframe). Triangulation is strengthened by linking statistical patterns to qualitative evidence. Findings repositioned as context-specific, requiring replication. (See pp. 17-18, Section 5.1; p. 20, Section 5.9; p. 20, Section 6)
9.
Reviewer Comment:
The ethics section is largely procedural and does not function as a governance argument; the ethical integration protocol is not mapped to established frameworks (EU AI Act, UNESCO guidance, Safe AI in Education Manifesto), “ethical awareness” is not clearly defined, and no explicit GenAI risk register or mitigation strategy is provided.
Response:
Ethical governance is strengthened through alignment with the EU AI Act, UNESCO guidance, and Safe AI principles, emphasizing transparency, learner agency, critical literacy, and academic integrity (Section 2.5). These principles are operationalized in Sections 3.5–3.6 via guided instruction and integrity safeguards. Although no formal risk register is included, key risks (dependency, bias, dishonesty, privacy, and access) are mitigated through pedagogical controls, framing governance as integrated instructional practice rather than procedural compliance. (See p. 6, Section 2.5; pp. 10-11, Section 3.5-3.6; p. 19, Section 5.5)
10.
Reviewer Comment:
Conclusions are directionally aligned with results but overstate certainty; they should be reframed as exploratory, aligned with measured outcomes only, and explicitly acknowledge programme-level confounds and limited transferability.
Response:
The Conclusion was revised to emphasize the study’s exploratory nature, restrict claims to measured outcomes (e.g., r = 0.68; ethical awareness from M = 1.8 to M = 3.4), and acknowledge contextual constraints (Pakistani setting, specific AI tools, 15-week duration). Language is moderated, and findings are framed as preliminary, requiring validation through randomized and cross-cultural studies. (See p. 20, Section 6)
11.
Reviewer Comment:
Ethics and governance literature coverage is incomplete; key frameworks (EU AI Act, UNESCO guidance, Safe AI in Education Manifesto) are missing, and a full audit is required to ensure consistency between in-text citations and the reference list.
Response:
Ethics and governance literature was expanded in Section 2.5 to incorporate the EU AI Act (2024), UNESCO guidance on generative AI in education and research (2023), and Safe AI principles. These frameworks are operationalized through the study’s ethical integration protocols detailed in Sections 3.5 and 3.7. In-text citations and references were reviewed to ensure consistency. One relevant reference is added at n. 34 (See p. 6, Section 2.5; p. 10, Section 3.5; pp. 21-24, References)
12. Reviewer Comment:
The Zenodo record is a positive step toward openness, but it does not fully compensate for missing replication and documentation details.
Response:
A Zenodo repository is referenced in the Declaration section, providing access to replication materials, including interview protocols, ethical reflection prompts, AI task prompts, scoring rubrics, anonymized datasets, and coding frameworks. The manuscript directs readers to this repository while core methodological procedures are described, ensuring transparency due to the article's word limit/space constraints. However, we have added the same at the end of the article as appendices, in case the reviewer wants them included. (See p. 11, Section 3.6; p. 21, Availability of data and materials; pp. 24-29, Appendices)
13.
Reviewer Comment:
The study is timely and promising but approved with reservations due to confounded design, insufficient replication detail, and inadequate ethics and governance alignment.
Response:
Reviewer concerns were addressed through strengthened baseline equivalence reporting (p = .532), moderated interpretive language, and inclusion of effect sizes (experimental r = 0.68; control r = 0.23) with Bonferroni adjustment. Quantitative–qualitative triangulation was enhanced, conclusions reframed as exploratory, and replication materials shared via Zenodo and as appendices.
