Article: Inclusive Education Implementation in Oman and Indonesia: A Comparative Analysis Study

============================================================

This article makes a significant contribution to the study of inclusive education through a comparative approach between Indonesia and Oman, two non-Western countries rarely studied side-by-side. The article's primary strength lies in its successful linking of socio-cultural contexts with educational governance structures to explain variations in the implementation of inclusive education. The author's use of qualitative policy document analysis of 33 official documents allows the authors to present a robust systemic picture of the practices, challenges, and direction of inclusion policies in both countries.
The results demonstrate a sharp and balanced analysis. Oman's centralized education system ensures policy consistency but lacks adaptation to local needs and community participation. Conversely, decentralization in Indonesia encourages local innovation and community involvement but creates disparities in the quality of implementation across regions. These findings enrich the global discourse by emphasizing that no single governance model is entirely ideal for inclusive education; rather, a contextually tailored, hybrid approach is needed.
The article also consistently identifies cross-contextual issues, such as limited teacher preparedness, weak curriculum adaptation, and strong cultural stigma against disabilities. This discussion is relevant and aligned with recent literature, particularly in relation to Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and the Individualized Education Program (IEP). However, the article could be strengthened by incorporating an implementation perspective at the school level or the voices of practitioners (teachers and principals) to complement the document-based analysis.
Overall, this article is academically sound, policy-relevant, and offers applicable recommendations for developing countries with similar social and cultural characteristics. This study is a worthy reference in developing inclusive education policies, particularly for policymakers, comparative education researchers, and practitioners seeking to bridge the gap between regulations and inclusive practices on the ground.
