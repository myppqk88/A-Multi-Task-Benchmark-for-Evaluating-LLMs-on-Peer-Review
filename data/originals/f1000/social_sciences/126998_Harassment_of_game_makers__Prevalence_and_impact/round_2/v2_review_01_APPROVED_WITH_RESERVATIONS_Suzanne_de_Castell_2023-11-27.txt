Article: Harassment of game makers: Prevalence and impact
DOI: 10.12688/f1000research.126998.2
Version: 2
Reviewer: Suzanne de Castell
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2023-11-27
Report ID: 166316
============================================================

Kowert and Crevoshay’s paper contributes an important exploratory study of the types, frequency and mental health impacts of online harassment upon gamesworkers, carried out by means of an online survey of 282 videogame professionals. As their results make very clear, people working in games suffer and witness extremely high levels (92% reported) of online harassment, and experience profound and even grave suffering because of it. The work is of great interest, and this review is far, far too long because of that.
The authors aim to “...
discuss the prevalence rates, nature of harassment, and the ways in which a lack of diversity has contributed to this phenomenon.”
It would be useful to know from the outset to what extent this paper reporting and analyzing survey data is also setting out explanations, given that the authors explicitly say they don’t want to be doing this. If it's not what they are doing, then it would help make that clearer by perhaps reframing things as suggestions, indications, hypotheses even?
Instead, on page 2, right at the beginning of the paper, some seemingly explanatory conclusions are already set out “…
the lack of clear boundaries between professional and personal social media accounts belonging to game makers, toxic gaming cultures within the gaming industry, and a lack of diversity within gaming content and cultures
.” Encountering this right at the beginning of the paper does give the impression that the analysis has already taken place and the conclusions have been arrived at prior to any of the data being presented, analyzed or interrogated.
Similarly, it appears pre-determined from the outset that a, perhaps even
the
, primary problem underlying the phenomenon is lack of diversity. Without questions, evidence, or argument sufficient to answer the fundamental question (however much we might agree with this, and indeed I do) HOW lack of diversity explains toxicity in the games industry: it is from the start simply declared that it does so. Interestingly, the concluding discussion states “
…the harassment of game makers
seems
directly tied to a lack of diversity within the industry, as evidenced by the ubiquity of identity-based harassment.”
The language of "seeming’’ communicates that this is an impression, not a now-established fact. However, as early as the second sentence of the abstract, and again (p4, para 3), it's stated that
“A lack of diversity has
unquestionably
contributed to the high levels of such incidences
.” It would help readers to follow the argument more logically if it were to begin with ‘impressions’, then take up evidence and argument, reach (or not, perhaps) the conclusion that lack of diversity has an “unquestionable” relationship to these toxic incidents. As to the difficult question of HOW lack of diversity, questionably or not, does this, the reason provided here is that over half of the respondents stated they were targeted because of
“some aspect of their identity”.
The problem is that ‘identity’ is underspecified: obviously straight white males have an ‘identity’, but to what extent is that identity targeted for online harassment? (And if they were to be harassed because of their SWM identity, how would that harassment indicate there’s a problem of a lack of diversity? It just doesn’t quite follow. And this is where closer attention to intersectionality is missing. (This need for conceptual nuance around 'identity' is paralleled by a need, as well, for greater nuance around who counts as a “gamesworker” - something the authors recognize in their discussion of limitations.) This study included anyone “working in games” - yet the problems of toxicity and harassment are actually not problems experienced first hand by “anyone working in games”; we know from other studies that both identity and work role make a huge difference to who gets harassed, with what levels and kinds of harassment - and to what effect personally and professionally. As the authors do point out:
“Future work should aim to sample more professionals in different areas of the gaming industry to better evaluate the frequency and impact of these behaviors across job functions.”
It's as important, especially given the discussion and recommendations in this paper, to enable data disaggregation in terms that speak to identity and intersectionalities. And, again, these researchers obviously are just as aware of that, as they note, “
This experience tends to be magnified for victims of marginalized gender and ethnic backgrounds
”. But those differences are otherwise methodologically obscured in this study, and not investigated so that we might learn more about who - specifically people having what identities, and what job roles - experience what levels and kinds of harassment. The problem here is that knowing about type and frequency, of harassment, without also knowing about its
distribution
, can guide principles, not policy, nor therefore justify the conclusion advanced that
“Without large-scale action, this problem [online harassment] will continue to reinforce the lack of diversity inside game studios, pushing out marginalized employees...”
As a general point, this is surely true, but without conceptual nuance and empirical specificity, we can have no practical idea of what that “large-scale action” should or could look like.
With respect to ‘toxic gamer culture’, a similar problem arises, in that, while we know from this work that gamesworkers experience more online harassment than members of the games community, this study’s focus is on gamers harassing gamesworkers: “
there has been a growing trend for hate and harassment
in gaming cultures
to expand beyond other players towards gamemakers.
” Without diminishing the importance of that observation, it remains the case that much of the literature reports on harassment (typically of women, as we all know very well) not by gamers but by other gamesworkers, yet this subject is not raised here, which of course means that this paper’s suggested solution that employers build studio policies that address
“the lack of clear boundaries between professional and personal social media accounts belonging to game makers”
will leave a significant dimension - and maybe the MOST significant dimension - of the problem uninterrogated. It would be useful to find out what percentage of the online hate and harassment came from ‘gamers’ - and how much from games industry ‘colleagues’.
Perhaps most importantly, while employer inaction and toxic gamer culture are identified in this analysis of online harassment, it’s interesting that no consideration is given to the role unregulated social media technologies and policies play in a burgeoning culture of harassment and hatred that is pervasive far beyond gamer culture or the games industry. Indeed, the respondents to this survey do
“...believe that gaming companies should have stronger policies in place around hate and harassment (87.2%).”
But note that survey participants see social media companies as bearing greater responsibility for the problem: “...
respondents think social media companies should be doing more to support people being targeted online (93.7%)
"
- this suggests that more respondents situate responsibility with media companies than with game companies. The information gleaned from this survey, while, as is acknowledged, it may be from a limited and specific sample, is nevertheless extremely interesting, and it would have been terrific to delve more deeply into these findings, and how they speak to this paper’s analysis, and its recommendations around policy and best courses of action. As well we learn that
“Most respondents believe that the games industry contributes to the ease with which people engage in online hate and harassment (62.4%)..."
but many more see the problem as lying elsewhere: “
The way that social media works is also believed to contribute to the ease with which people engage in online hate and harassment (96.5%)..."
Yet nowhere is that participant response, suggesting this kind of toxicity might be a widespread phenomenon, probed into in this analysis - it’s as if gamesworkers experience significantly MORE online harassment and hatred than people in other fields - but do they? How do we know? What about K-pop stars, whose virulent online harassment has led to increasing rates of suicide and mental health breakdowns? Or pro-choice activists? Or prominent authors who voice unpopular views about sex and gender, for that matter? This matters enormously for where we do the work of reparation. But how do we know (and why would we assume) that gamesworkers as a group experience more extreme and more frequent harassment on Twitter (etc.) than other groups?
We simply don’t know this, and we have no reason to suppose it's true. This doesn’t in any way take away from the importance of understanding - and acting to address - what does happen to gamesworkers, but it does make quite a difference to the analysis and to any proposed laws, policies or procedures designed to contend with it. The paper’s conclusion that
“Games leadership need to create stronger policies around hate and harassment of their employees, delineate clearer social boundaries between game maker and game player, and provide greater mental health support should their employees become targets of online harassment.”
only makes sense IF the problem has been correctly diagnosed. But what if the problem isn’t one experienced any more often by gamesworkers than other professionals, and what if that problem is due primarily to the inbuilt toxicity of platforms like Twitter, which not only allow and enable but actively incite and invite hateful and assaultive behaviours, and what if it’s not gamesworkers at all, but women, trans folk, people of colour and other minorities working (and here mostly in lower level positions) in games who are experiencing the high percentages of workplace toxicity, as well as the online forms of harassment that are reported here as if it were a generic feature of games industry worker experience.
So with all respect for the work that this paper accomplishes, it's sometimes hard to grasp relationships between data, analysis, and recommendations. It would help enormously to have the authors state explicitly the connection between knowing the types and frequencies of online harassment for gamesworkers as a group, and courses of action to address that problem, besides providing mental health and other supports for victims, who appear to constitute a considerable percentage of the gamesworkers surveyed. Specifically, this would call for clarification of what 'identity' means, specification of WHOSE experiences (i.e. what ‘identities’ and roles) these findings refer to - because it's surely not everyone who works in games who experiences hate and vitriol -
some explanation of how this analysis is “intersectional”, consideration of the question of whether it is actually games culture or ‘twitterculture’ that is the root problem here, and whether its specific to gamesworkers or is a toxic element across many workplaces and professions - and what we leave out when we frame the problem as harassment of gamesworkers by gamers, leaving out entirely how much more devastating it just might be when that harassment, online as well as off, is up close and personal, at work, or carried out by bosses and co-workers.
I hope these comments are helpful, thank you for this paper, I enjoyed and learned from it.
