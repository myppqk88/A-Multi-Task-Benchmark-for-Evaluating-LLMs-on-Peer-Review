I. Overview
The paper offers a valuable literature review on Text and Data Mining (TDM), mapping the complex regulatory framework surrounding these processes and shedding light on what constitutes lawful TDM. However, several areas require improvement for the paper to reach its full potential. This report provides some comments and suggestions to strengthen the paper's structure, argumentation, and use of sources.
II. Comments
1) Introduction
Structure improvement: The introduction could be better structured to present the study's subject matter clearly. The first two paragraphs are not sufficiently clear, particularly in introducing the concept of "big data." It would be beneficial to discuss the characteristic elements of big data and differentiate between structured, unstructured, and semi-structured data. This is crucial for readers to grasp the subject thoroughly. For reference, see Jonathan Grossman and Ami Pedahzur, [Ref:1]
Clarification of concepts: The sentence ‘data “is the new oil and lifeblood of economic development” and just as oil needs to be burnt for it to be useful, raw data must be processed to realise its true potential’ is problematic and contested. This analogy oversimplifies the complexities of data processing in the digital economy. See, e.g., Clemens Mewald, ‘Why Data Is *Not* the New Oil and Data Marketplaces Have Failed Us’ (Medium, 13 July 2023)
https://towardsdatascience.com/why-data-is-not-the-new-oil-and-data-marketplaces-have-failed-us-b42dd87a0ba0
; David Moschella, ‘Data Isn’t the New Oil; That Might Be a Good Thing’ (2023)
https://itif.org/publications/2023/05/30/data-isnt-the-new-oil-that-might-be-a-good-thing/
2) Regulatory Framework
A broader perspective is needed: The paper should more comprehensively address the regulatory challenges of TDM, particularly from a data protection standpoint. This includes not only the technical challenges of big data processing but also the legal issues associated with scraping and mining of publicly available data. Including opinions from public agencies and supervisory bodies would offer a more holistic view of the regulatory landscape. These perspectives could be instrumental in identifying the main challenges to individual rights and public interests.
3) Data Protection Issues
Insufficient coverage of GDPR issues: The paper does not sufficiently address the complexities and challenges that data mining poses for data subjects. The paper relies on some sources of questionable quality. It would benefit from a more careful selection of references, particularly those recognised as authoritative in the field. I recommend citing more reliable sources, especially when describing the fundamental elements of GDPR, such as Christopher Kuner and others (eds), [
Ref:2],A Commentary
(New York, 2020; online edn, Oxford Academic); Indra Spiecker genannt Döhmann and others (eds),
General Data Protection Regulation: Article-by-article Commentary
(München, 2023; Beck).
Concept of "meaningful research": The paper should elaborate on the idea of "meaningful research" and how data protection regulations address data processing for scientific, research, or statistical purposes. This is a critical aspect that currently needs more depth.
Data Protection by Design and Default: When discussing the implementation of the principle of data protection by design and default, it is important to reference guidelines issued by supervisory authorities. For instance, the EDPB Guidelines 4/2019 on Article 25 Data Protection by Design and by Default [Ref 5]; Commission Nationale d’Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), GDPR guide for developers  (2022); Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD), A Guide to Privacy by Design (2019).
Data mining, profiling and inferences: The paper should more comprehensively address the challenges and risks of data mining, especially concerning profiling and the processing of inferred data. The relevance of purpose limitation and data minimisation principles needs more emphasis. Key references include Bart Custers and others (eds), [
Ref
:3,4]
Missing legal aspects. Data scraping: The paper currently lacks a discussion on the legal aspects of data scraping at scale and text mining of publicly available data, which are increasingly relevant, especially for training AI models. It would be beneficial to incorporate an analysis of the legal challenges posed by these practices, e.g., Benjamin Bremert,
Legal Aspects of Text Mining Publicly Available Data
, Unabhängiges Landeszentrum für Datenschutz (ULD), 2017; Zuzanna Gulczynska, ‘Scraping personal data from internet pages - a comparative analysis of the Polish Bisnode decision and the US hiQ Labs v LinkedIn Corp judgment’, European Law Review, vol. 45, no. 6, pp. 857–869, 2020; Catherine Altobelli and others, ‘To Scrape or Not to Scrape? The Lawfulness of Social Media Crawling under the GDPR’, in Jean Herveg (ed),
Deep Driving into Data Protection: 1979-2019: Celebrating 40 Years of Privacy and Data Protection at the CRIDS
(Larcier, 2021); Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens, Richtlijnen scraping door private organisaties en particulieren (2024)
4) TDM and fundamental rights
Effectively addressing bias and discrimination in data mining is critical to safeguarding fundamental rights. Failure to do so can result in inequitable outcomes that disproportionately harm marginalised groups, perpetuating existing social inequalities. See Bettina Berendt and Sören Preibusch, ‘Toward Accountable Discrimination-Aware Data Mining: The Importance of Keeping the Human in the Loop-and Under the Looking Glass’, Big Data. 2017 Jun;5(2):135-152; Solon Barocas and Andrew Selbst, ‘Big Data's Disparate Impact’, 104 California Law Review 671 (2016); Solon Barocas, ‘Data Mining and the Discourse on Discrimination’, Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining (2014).
5) Several footnotes and cross-references need to be revised and improved for accuracy and clarity.
