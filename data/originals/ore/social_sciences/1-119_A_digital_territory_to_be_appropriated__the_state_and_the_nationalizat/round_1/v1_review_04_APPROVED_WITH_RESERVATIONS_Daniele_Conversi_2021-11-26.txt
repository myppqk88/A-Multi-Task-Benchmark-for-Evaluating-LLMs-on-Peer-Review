Article: A digital territory to be appropriated: the state and the nationalization of cyberspace
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.14010.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Daniele Conversi
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2021-11-26
Report ID: 27762
============================================================

This is a well argued and objective analysis of the US, Greek, Dutch, and Israeli ministries of foreign affairs websites. It illustrates the way information is articulated, circulated and disseminated through official channels and networks. The use of content analysis is possibly adequate to the aims and goals of the article as it stresses the choice of specific sets of news against other news. This selective process is connected with the creation and appropriation of networks that the author adequately defines as "the nationalisation of the cyberspace".
Some important aspects are, however, overlooked. Most important, could the author more clearly show how this process of news selection and information retrieval is filtered through those kinds of topics that aliment the ongoing process of splintering the Internet?
Most importantly, I believe the author should emphasise how more cooperative, international and global agendas are ignored in favour of parochial, sectional, regional and state-led agendas that clearly reflect the respective foreign ministries' interests and visions as they are articulated in the analysed websites.
Consequently, the author may need to ask how far crucial security threats, such as the climate emergency
,
are relegated to the background insofar as they do not fit the nationalist agenda.
This is, in turn, linked to one question: what main foreign policy issues are selected for diffusion and why? The content analysis should also reveal how some of the websites deliberately ignore such global news and emergencies in favour of oblivion and, often, denial.
The author mentions national control of the recognised maritime space: In fact, the United Nations’ Law of the Sea Convention (UNCLOS) for the management of marine natural resources should be connected to the way nationalism is unable to tackle global issues, such as biodiversity loss. The very way that natural gas and oil reserves discovered in the respective continental shelves are rebranded is more indicative of the presence of ‘resource nationalism’ - a term that should be better linked to the notion of "nationalization of cyberspace".
On the other hand, the author presents good evidence in the way the four ministries' websites are nationalistically captured resulting in a "nationalization of cyberspace" against the original cosmopolitan dream of the Internet as a neutral arena of international communications
.
Accordingly, the four foreign ministries are active promoters and prominent actors in the broader propagation of the splintering of the cyberspace into tightly bound national areas.
But this should be understood in a very broad sense, both as the propagation of ‘hot’ nationalism in regions characterised by persisting and deeply rooted national conflicts and as forms of "banal nationalism" in more ordinary everyday settings (as described by Michael Billig 1995).
Also, the notion of
cyberspace
needs to be situated with more precision according to which components or elements are privileged in each case. I think this could be resolved by adding a few sentences that could clarify the conceptual reach of the term and identify its components. Among the layers that needs to be added are autonomous forces, such as those arising in the diasporas in the process elsewhere identified as “irresponsible radicalisation”. By briefly considering this important aspect beyond official websites, the evidence of the ongoing nationalization process could be more nuanced and further clarified. Beyond official government and ministries websites, Facebook’s role in inciting or assisting nationalist violence has also been studied in recent research.
In conclusion, while the notion of “nationalization of cyberspace” is very promising, it needs to be supplemented by a short discussion about the broader process of autonomous radicalisation, which within the diasporas does not appear to be necessarily connected to the propaganda efforts of the ministries of foreign affairs. The author also needs to consider how crucial security threats at the global level such as climate change have been often ignored in most of these official websites at least until recently. This could clarify how the "nationalisation of cyberspace" acts in practice against the very security, interests, and survival of nations.
Minor revisions required.
