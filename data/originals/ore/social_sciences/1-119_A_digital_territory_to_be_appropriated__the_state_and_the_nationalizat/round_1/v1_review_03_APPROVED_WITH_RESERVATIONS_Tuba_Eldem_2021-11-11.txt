Article: A digital territory to be appropriated: the state and the nationalization of cyberspace
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.14010.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Tuba Eldem
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2021-11-11
Report ID: 27760
============================================================

This article examines websites of Greek, Dutch, US and Israeli ministries of foreign affairs through content analysis. It outlines how the websites are structured, how the missions and visions of foreign ministries are defined and what the main foreign policy issues are. More importantly, through the content analysis of the four foreign ministry websites, the author unpacks how each official state website constructs and propagates its own nationalist ideology. These sections are informative and well-organized.
I have, however, concerns over the conceptual framework, and thus methodology. The author presents the use of national ideology in the official state websites as evidence of “nationalization of cyberspace” and argues that this “contradicts the original purpose of the Internet as a liberating, post-national medium, offering new forms of community”.
The conceptualization and measurement of nationalization of cyberspace needs to be well-situated in the literature and better developed. To be more specific, can the propagation of the national ideology in the institutional websites of foreign ministries be really considered as ‘nationalization of cyberspace’?  After all, the concept of cyberspace is a term broader than Internet. It includes physical, logical and cyber-persona layers. Physical network refers to the machines – such as the computers, routers, cables, servers, cellphone towers, and satellites – that establish the mechanical and electrical, magnetic, and optical lines of communication. Logical network refers to the logical instructions and software that operate communications traffic, such as domain name system (DNS) and Internet service providers (ISP). And finally cyber-persona or social layer refers to the sphere through which videos, images, sounds, and texts circulate.
When author refers to the nationalization of cyberspace, which layer does he refer to? Since the article only focuses on the official websites, it seems to refer to social layer or what in the military realm is referred to as strategic communications. But even within this limited layer, the evidence provided fails to denote nationalization of strategic communication/social/cyber-persona layer. In order to arrive at such a conclusion, one needs to see further evidence of nationalization attempts, such as a concerted effort in social media networks that propagate same or similar ideas or positions outlined in the foreign ministry websites, use of computational propaganda or nation-specific information control policies that are in line with the national ideology dispersed in the official websites such as filtering, surveillance, legal regulations regulating the dissemination of information online, content control, etc.
In the literature, nationalization of cyberspace generally refers to a shift from laissez-faire and market-oriented approaches towards the state-directed controls and regulations. Although such a trend is also visible in Western liberal democracies, it is more prevalent among those cyber-sovereignist states such as China and Russia, which try to eradicate the borderless nature of cyberspace by controlling all of these three layers through constructing underwater communication networks, various forms of information control and data localization policies as well as attempting to shape international cyberspace governance regimes. Nationalization of cyberspace thus refers more of demarcating new boundaries in national cyberspace generally for security purposes through implementing  cyberspace regulations, internet policies, cyber laws and other  regulatory frameworks, thereby giving rise to a ‘splinternet’.
I therefore consider that the use of “nationalization of cyberspace” as a central conceptual framework may not be appropriate. I suggest reframing the article centering on the reproduction of nationalism or national identities through governmental websites, rather than nationalization of cyberspace.
