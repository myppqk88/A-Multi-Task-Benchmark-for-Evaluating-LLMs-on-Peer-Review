New developments
There are two. The first is the European Health Data Space (EHDS) proposal by the European Commission. That very complex proposal and its comments cannot be discussed here. It also has clauses about ‘joint controllers’.  Though the EHDS will be a major game changer for the secondary use of health data, it claims to adhere to the GDPR with (probably) a new Union law legal basis in the sense of article 9.2 I and j. Which entities are joint controllers may be influenced by the EHDS but cannot be decided by the EHDS as that decision follows from the GDPR. From that perspective it foremost matters whether new case law sheds new light in the ‘joint controller’ discussion. Obviously, new case law could also influence our proposal. A recent judgement of the CJEU in case C-683/21
[1]
does shed new light on this issue and we should discuss that decision first. 

That decision is important for a variety of reasons. We will only highlight the essence of the judgment for the issue under discussion here and abstain from details about the circumstances or other aspects of the interesting judgement, relevant for the interpretation of other clauses of the GDPR.  That essence is, as mentioned in number 30 of the judgment, that (our italics added):

……who exerts influence over the processing of such data,
for his, her or its own purposes
, and who participates, as a result, in the determination of the purposes and means of that processing, may be regarded as a controller in respect of such processing.

The CJEU states that its judgment follows from its earlier decisions. We submit that this is true for a very large extent but that the line in italics is an important refinement which was more implicit in the earlier decisions we discussed in our paper.  A law firm which is consulted about a certain data processing also exerts influence as it will give advice on what certainly shouldn’t be done and how it may be done. But it does not do so for its own purposes regarding the data involved. That ‘influence’ versus ‘own purposes regarding the data involved’ is what we tried to disentangle in the sieve model. As it is more complicated there than in the just given example of the externally hired law firm.  If a law firm or ICT consultant is embedded in an EU-funded research consortium as a partner, that partner exerts influence but is also sort of an ally for the purposes. We will come back to that when we discuss the criticism of the reviewers on the sieve model.
The GDPR and DPD differ on joint controllership
Reviewer Bernier states that joint controllership under the Data Protection Directive (DPD) and the GDPR differ. In the DPD the responsibilities resulting from joint controllership were narrowly constructed while under GDPR joint controllership leads to joint and several liability. It is correct that we did not discuss this issue. However, we note that this does not affect the strictly legal analysis, as in our paper, of whether a situation is at hand. We discussed under what conditions parties are considered to be joint controllers, not the consequences
if
they are considered joint controllers. We have added a clarification regarding this point.  

With present knowledge, it might even be questioned whether the GDPR brought a major change in this respect. In mentioned judgement the CJEU reiterated at point 42 that: 

However, the existence of joint responsibility does not necessarily imply equal responsibility of the various operators involved in the processing of personal data. On the contrary, those operators may be involved at different stages of that processing of personal data and to different degrees, so that the level of responsibility of each of them must be assessed with regard to all the relevant circumstances of the particular case (judgment of 5 June 2018, Wirtschaftsakademie Schleswig-Holstein, C‑210/16, EU:C:2018:388, paragraph 43).

So, even though this review led to a question mark, we did not follow-up on it.
The funnel model with the sieves leave room for interpretation
All reviewers agreed that the funnel model is a good try and, in many ways, practical but still leaves room for interpretation. We acknowledge that and gratefully accepted most of Bahr’s textual recommendations.

But it remains that there are considerations involved that are not always black and white. We try to set thresholds of control and ‘own purposes’, after the CJEU’s recent judgment, which we disentangle from the general overall responsibilities of the parties within a research consortium who are all committed to the overall success of the project and hence also of the data processing in general.
Concluding remarks
At point 29 of mentioned decision the CJEU retained its position that a broad conception of controller contributes to the protection of the fundamental rights and freedoms of data subjects. In doing so it ignored criticism in the literature about such a broad conception, which leads to multiple controllers.
[2]
It seems as if the Court in its judgement wants to accommodate some of that criticism by reiterating its decision under the DPD that joint controllership does not per se lead to joint responsibility for all the data processing of each of the joint parties (see supra point 3) even though some authors have argued that the GDPR would lead to a very different conclusion in this respect.
[3]
How the differentiated level of responsibility in the case of joint controllers works in practice remains unclear at the moment. All together it seems to us that the differentiation should preferable be made at an earlier stage, namely whether one is a joint controller in a common endeavour to achieve data processing in research. We submit that, also given point 42 of mentioned recent judgment and point 33 of the Opinion of AG Emilou of May 4 in this case 2023,
[4]
our funnel model with the sieves is compatible with the (new) case law and does not compromise the objectives of the GDPR.
