Author Response to Review 2 (v1)
Author: author
Date: 
============================================================

Dear Reviewer, we would like to thank you for your valuable and insightful comments. We have proceeded with major revisions on our manuscript, following most of your points, which we found well raised. These changes have bolstered our arguments, slightly extending the text’s wording, without changing its meaning. Please find below our responses to your comments in detail.
Reviewer: The paper does not separate normative from positive statements in a clear way, while lacking the notion of an optimal allocation of talent without which such discussion cannot be done.
Author Response:
To address the first part of this crucial comment, we rephrased various sections of the manuscript. Now, observations based on empirical evidence are clearly stated, allowing the reader to easily distinguish them from statements which express our opinions. Regarding the lack of the notion of an optimal allocation of talent, we have revised the Theoretical and Conceptual Section of our manuscript. Specifically, in this section we open a whole part where we discuss the factors shaping the uneven geography of skill mismatches writing that:
“To maximize the effects of higher educational attainment, however, talent has to be allocated in an optimal way, namely, a worker must employ their skills in the most appropriate setting. It is then through the optimal allocation of talent that a higher educational attainment level generates individual and social returns (Acemoglu, 1995).”
Further below, the concept is scrutinized in detail, using the appropriate literature. Then, in the Discussion, the concept now plays a key role.
In the empirical part, the authors focus on vertical mismatch, by which they implicitly mean high skilled workers employed in non-managerial and non-professional roles (ISCO groups below 3). However, such allocation could arise due to a particular industry structure which favours middle-skilled occupations and therefore attracts a disproportionate amount of workers across all skill levels. The paper could be improved by separating the issues of spatial allocation from mismatch, and by corroborating the results on mismatch by other indicators of skill shortages and oversupply, such as rates of return to education, local unemployment rates and job turnover.
Author Response:
This is a well raised point. However, due to limited space, we could not address both the issues of mismatches’ spatial allocation as well as the nature of vertical mismatches. Unavoidably, we relied on secondary data that do not consider the qualitative aspects of these vertical mismatches. Nevertheless, we linked the concentration of various types of skill (mis)matches with a series of factors, including through macroeconomic, population, innovation, and demographic indicators. Please refer to our response to Comment 8 by Reviewer 1. The issue of  particular industry structures which favour middle-skilled occupations is addressed in the Discussion. Indicatively, we write:
“Apart from tourism, there are many manufacturing- and assembly-related activities along GVCs that favor medium skill-level occupations and therefore generate tendencies towards a disproportionate amount of highly educated workers in such occupations. Such is the case with most French regions, and especially the agri-food heavy Franche-Comté.”
While the paper introduces five different factors of regional specialisation, the empirical approach doesn't in fact leave the realm of the neoclassical human capital theory, as it is largely informed by spatial allocation of workers within the standard labour demand framework with regional specialisation. While the authors mention other determinants such as global value chains and local institutions, there is no systematic characterisation of their variation shown with data. The findings on mismatch in particular lack an interpretation supported by documented evidence on country and regional differences along the dimensions considered in the paper. Given limitations of regional data, it is doubtful that it is feasible to discuss all these mechanisms in detail and isolate their effects. However, the paper can still be informative by developing comprehensive descriptive evidence in a systematic way.
Author Response:
It is correctly stated that limitations of regional data, as well as the paper’s word limitation, did not allow us to make our postulations regarding GVCs more evidence-based. Nevertheless, we addressed your comment by developing comprehensive descriptive evidence in a systematic way around the five factors inquired in the Discussion. Specifically, our Empirical Analysis now provides concrete evidence for our postulations in the Discussion by including a scrutiny of indicators on GDP, innovation performance, population density, and demographic structures. Please refer to our response to Comment 8 by Reviewer 1 for more detail regarding the proxies that we used and the methods we implemented for their analysis. Moreover, all these factors are covered in the Theoretical and Conceptual Section as well. Please refer to our response to Comment 3 by Reviewer 2 for more detail.
The authors' construction of the quotient index assumes that all countries should have similar relative shares of human capital allocation, but this ignores country-level differences in education systems organisation and differences in economic development (as more developed economies tend to have higher investment rates in education in general). For example, vocational education plays a relatively larger role in Germany and provides educational tracks to occupations which would otherwise require a college degree in other countries. Since such country-level differences make the results difficult to interpret, the authors should focus on within-country variation.
Author Response:
Although this is a well raised point, the main goal of our analysis was to directly compare the performance of various EU regions in terms of addressing skill mismatches. For that reason, we went with the relative-to-transnational LQ as our main index. Nevertheless, we accept that structural differences between countries make such comparisons a difficult case. Apart from the latter, we are perfectly aware that the LQ can sometimes lead to contradicting results due to not providing a clear view of the absolute magnitude of the phenomenon under study, or due to the modifiable areal unit problem. To overcome all the above, we tried to consider a wide array of country-specific factors when discussing our findings. For instance, we have extensively relied on relevant research around the employment/educational regimes typology. For instance, we write in the revised Theoretical and Conceptual Section:
“Nevertheless, dual educational systems such as Germany’s have also been praised for their positive effects in school-to-work transitions, albeit having been oversimplified in the relevant literature (Deissinger, 2015). Similarly praised is the universalistic regime in Nordic countries like Denmark, which invest in human capital and match high levels of educational attainment with demand for such workers (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019). [...] …in statist regimes, for instance, employers are not sufficiently involved in matching skills’ demand and supply (Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020). As such, in the sub-protective regime of the Mediterranean EU, in countries like Greece, youth transitions to adulthood are long and problematic (Emmanouil et al., 2023). This leads to a large pool of highly qualified people who either cannot find a job, have a profession for which they are overqualified, or have a profession in a different sector from the one in which they were trained, while at the same time many companies are struggling to fill their vacancies (Biagi et al., 2020).”
Similarly, in the Discussion we write:
“...the universalistic (e.g.Denmark) regime with high investments in education and fast and stable school-to-work transitions is completely different from countries of the sub-protective regime (e.g. Greece), which display severe skills mismatches and low labour demand in general (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019).”.
The paper omits critical details about the sample construction: it is not clear whether the authors imposed age restrictions, used only full-time or part-time workers, included both genders or only males, included or excluded migrant workers (which can have strong implications for the results). It's also not clear whether nation averages of the shares are weighted. How did the authors handle missing data?
Author Response:
To address this comment, we have added to the Methodological Section that
“Eurostat’s sample covers all employed persons of both genders, aged 15 and above. Data imputation methods were not used, given that the extent of missing data is limited and most missing values refer to a specific type of skill mismatch (highly educated workers in low skill-level occupations), making most such methods unreliable.”
I am concerned about specific years chosen for the analysis (2009, 2021) which could affect the results due to strong negative economic shocks which were unevenly distributed. Since the analysis doesn't rely on time variation, the authors should use different years and average the results across 5 or 10 year intervals to reduce noise.
Author Response:
This is another excellent point which could lead to very interesting findings. In practical terms, we could add one or two years between 2009 and 2021. However, this could substantially add to the wordage of the manuscript. Moreover, we believe that the years under study as it is allow for a comparison between two crucial moments in the EU’s recent history: the onset of a period of successive crises and the onset of a period of a renewed round of pandemic- and geopolitically-induced crises. Moreover, please note that our analysis, given that the LQ is a relative indicator, avoids looking into the changes in the extent of skill mismatches between 2009 and 2021. Relatedly, we note in the Empirical Analysis Section:
“It must be noted that the LQ is a relative index, meaning that its changes over time do not indicate neither absolute growth nor retraction of the phenomenon under study. Therefore, correlations pertaining to LQ changes over time must be approached with caution.”
As such, we believe that the noise does not affect the significance of our observations considerably.
