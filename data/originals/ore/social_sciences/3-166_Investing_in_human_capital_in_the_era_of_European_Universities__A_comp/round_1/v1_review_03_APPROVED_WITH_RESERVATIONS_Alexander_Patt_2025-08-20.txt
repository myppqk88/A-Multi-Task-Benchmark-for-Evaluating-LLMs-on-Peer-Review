Article: Investing in human capital in the era of European Universities: A comparative analysis of skill mismatches across the regions of the ERUA countries
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.16470.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Alexander Patt
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2025-08-20
Report ID: 53556
============================================================

I have read the revised version of the paper and I think it is considerably improved. There is greater focus on documenting spatial allocation of high skilled workers across the EU and exploring the patterns descriptively. The paper now offers a clearer conceptual overview of the many factors affecting skill allocation and mismatch. The authors are successful in connecting different literatures and highlighting the importance of the topic for further studying and informing the policy making. The points on the importance of quantifying the extent of skill mismatch and unevenness in skills distribution, their implications for individual well-being and regional development, and the interplay between technological change, labour market adjustments, education policy and early labour market transitions are in particular well-made. In the revised version, most terms are now clearly defined and different dimensions of mismatch are delineated. New descriptive results are intriguing and add weight to the discussion but should be interpreted with great caution since no causal interpretation is possible and there are many potential confounders.
I have a few remaining suggestions for improving the empirical part:
- Given fairly large changes in the quotient index over time and a rather moderate adjustment in relative employment shares (as evidenced by Annex Table), it would be useful to mention how much of it is driven by employment growth at the aggregate level, which drives the denominator in the formula, as opposed to region-level changes, which are captured by the numerator. One could for example compare changes in the index with the values obtained by fixing national-level employment shares (the denominator) as they were in 2009 for both time periods.
- I would be cautious about interpreting values of LQ far from 1 as indicators of skill mismatch, as the index is also affected by overall employment size. A low value of LQ_H, for example, could arise due to a relatively small number of high skilled workers present in a region who are nevertheless perfectly matched to high skill-level jobs. This possibility could be investigated by calculating the correlation coefficients between LQ_H, LQ_M and LQ_L (and possibly other independent indicators of regional skill mismatch).
- I am not sure I understand the nature of the missing value problem (page 8 and Figures 2a, 2b): in the Annex Table, the authors report employment shares of highly educated workers in low skill-level occupations without any missing values. Wouldn't that information be enough to create Figures 2a/2b with values for all regions? Moreover, if total regional employment of highly educated workers is known, one could simply recover ELR from known values of EMR and EHR. Furthermore, It's not clear whether Table 1 uses the full set of regions for reporting correlation coefficients with LQ_L. If this is not the case, the results should be based on the set of regions for which values of LQ_L are known in both time periods, as otherwise they may be driven by changes in the composition of the set of regions.
- Table 1 needs to indicate statistical significance levels of correlation coefficients. Please also report the sample size.
- Weaker correlations with the level of national GDP are not surprising given the nature of the data. I suggest to omit these results due to difficulties of interpreting them and because the analysis already uses GDP per capita which doesn't suffer from comparability issues across countries with vastly different population sizes.
- Instead of calculating correlations with changes in GDP per capita, growth rates (in real terms) should be used as they are independent of the scale.
