Article: Investing in human capital in the era of European Universities: A comparative analysis of skill mismatches across the regions of the ERUA countries
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.16470.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Martin Henning
Status: approved
Date: 2025-05-28
Report ID: 53557
============================================================

This is an improved and much more coherent version of the paper. The literature review is much better structured, although readers should be aware that the idea of the theoretical reflection is not to derive testable hypotheses, but to draw up the theoretical context of the paper in line with its explorative aim. Consequently, the data analysis remains overall descriptive and illustrative of some empirical tendencies, rather than evaluating a specific theory.
Among the most interesting empirical patters detected are those pertaining to the capital cities and how many of these “superstar cities” produce dual results in the matching on their local labour markets, in both ends of the labour market distribution. Many results concerning the more peripheral region are informative too. These findings are related both to the broader literature on within-country divergence in Europe, and the one on polarization of labour markets from a geographical perspective.
The processes creating illustrated empirical patterns and correlations could be further discussed. The authors themselves emphasize the ”economic dynamism at the national level, regional industrial specializations, urbanisation levels, demographic structures, and institutional endowments”. These indicators may themselves be correlated. On a higher level of abstraction, the authors discuss the impact of recent decade’s regional alignment with the location structures of global value chains. This argument resonates well with recent arguments relating to the “geography of functions” hypothesis, but remains to be formally tested. Also, in the paper, the mismatch issues arising in peripheral regions are largely attributed to institutional dynamics and an information problem in these regions. These might well be factors that contribute to the identified patterns, but given the structure of correlations, it seems to me that this is more an issue created by the structure of regional growth (or not growth), and not so much of an issue of information about available jobs per se.
In all, the paper gives interesting perspectives on an important regional development problem in the EU, and its variation across regions. What could and should be done about it, apart from implementing place-based approaches trying to facilitate general economic growth and productive transformation, remains largely an open issue. Ultimately, it also comes down to the opportunities that people perceive in various places, to participate in economic production and growth. I therefore remain sceptical to general statements such as “Instead, policies should be spatially sensitive so as to keep young people with high levels of education in non-urban and/or peripheral regions, where they will work in high-skill occupations and put their human capital in good use.“ Given the nature of economic dynamics nowadays, this is much easier said than done, if we also want to keep the good features that an essentially experimental economy comes with.
