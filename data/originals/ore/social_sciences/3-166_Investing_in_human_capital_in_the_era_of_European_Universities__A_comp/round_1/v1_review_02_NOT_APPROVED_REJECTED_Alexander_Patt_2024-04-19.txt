Article: Investing in human capital in the era of European Universities: A comparative analysis of skill mismatches across the regions of the ERUA countries
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.16470.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Alexander Patt
Status: not approved
Date: 2024-04-19
Report ID: 38002
============================================================

The paper offers a perspective on regional allocation of human capital and skill mismatch by discussing different strands of the literature (human capital theory, economic geography, education policy, institutions, etc.) and showing complementary empirical evidence using variation in the location quotient index. The index is based on education shares across three broad occupational groups in NUTS2 regions from a selected group of countries. The authors document considerable regional variation in skill mismatch.
Overall, I found the paper thought-provoking and I appreciated that it drew upon various literatures. However, I am unsure that it is possible to adequately address all questions raised by the paper within the analysis framework chosen by the authors. More specific comments follow.
i) The paper does not separate normative from positive statements in a clear way, while lacking notion of an optimal allocation of talent without which such discussion cannot be done. In the empirical part, the authors focus on vertical mismatch, by which they implicitly mean high skilled workers employed in non-managerial and non-professional roles (ISCO groups below 3). However, such allocation could arise due to a particular industry structure which favours middle-skilled occupations and therefore attracts a disproportionate amount of workers across all skill levels. The paper could be improved by separating the issues of spatial allocation from mismatch, and by corroborating the results on mismatch by other indicators of skill shortages and oversupply, such as rates of return to education, local unemployment rates and job turnover.
ii) The authors' construction of the quotient index assumes that all countries should have similar relative shares of human capital allocation, but this ignores country-level differences in education systems organisation and differences in economic development (as more developed economies tend to have higher investment rates in education in general). For example, vocational education plays a relatively larger role in Germany and provides educational tracks to occupations which would otherwise require a college degree in other countries. Since such country-level differences make the results difficult to interpret, the authors should focus on within-country variation.
iii) While the paper introduces five different factors of regional specialisation, the empirical approach doesn't in fact leave the realm of the neoclassical human capital theory, as it is largely informed by spatial allocation of workers within the standard labour demand framework with regional specialisation. While the authors mention other determinants such as global value chains and local institutions, there is no systematic characterisation of their variation shown with data. The findings on mismatch in particular lack an interpretation supported by documented evidence on country and regional differences along the dimensions considered in the paper. Given limitations of regional data, it is doubtful that it is feasible to discuss all these mechanisms in detail and isolate their effects. However, the paper can still be informative by developing comprehensive descriptive evidence in a systematic way.
iv) The paper omits critical details about the sample construction: it is not clear whether the authors imposed age restrictions, used only full-time or part-time workers, included both genders or only males, included or excluded migrant workers (which can have strong implications for the results). It's also not clear whether nation averages of the shares are weighted. How did the authors handle missing data?
v) I am concerned about specific years chosen for the analysis (2009, 2021) which could affect the results due to strong negative economic shocks which were unevenly distributed. Since the analysis doesn't rely on time variation, the authors should use different years and average the results across 5 or 10 year intervals to reduce noise.
vi) The paper only shows the results for high skilled workers. What about other groups?
