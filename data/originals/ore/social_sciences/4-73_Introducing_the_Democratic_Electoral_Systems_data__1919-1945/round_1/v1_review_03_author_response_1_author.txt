First, Düzgün Arslantas has raised the concern that our research team did not speak all languages spoken in the countries included in the dataset, potentially introducing bias.
Response
: We agree that this may be a problem, although it is a common in cross-national research. We would like to highlight that our research team was able to read source material in Dutch, English, Finnish, French, German, Italian, Serbo-Croatian, Spanish, and Swedish. Thus, we spoke the languages of 26 of the 34 countries included in the dataset. Moreover, we also relied on data compiled by others in a comparative manner such as Mackie and Rose (1991), Nohlen (2005), and Nohlen and Stöver (2010). There compilations are predominantly authored by country experts, who speak the language and know the cultural specificities of “their” country. We are confident that this allowed us to minimize bias as much as possible. We have clarified this point in the main text (see point 4 in the Section “Materials & Methods”).
Second,
Düzgün Arslantas pointed out that it remains to be seen whether the DES dataset can improve our understanding of the diffusion of electoral rules across Europe and the Americas.
Response
: We agree and apologize for the unprecise wording. We have clarified our statement. We now say: “Finally, the DES 1919-1945 release will prove a valuable resource for scholars who study the origins of electoral systems, for example to explore the role of diffusion of electoral rules across Europe and the Americas.”
Third, Düzgün Arslantas asked for additional information on how many elections are classified as democratic, given the increasing prevalence of regimes that fall between the two extremes of authoritarian and democratic systems.
Response
: We apologize for not having been clear enough. We include all elections that have been classified as democratic by any of the following datasets: Boix, Miller, and Rosato (2012), the dichotomous Democracy and Dictatorship classification (DD), V-Dem, and Polity5. We did not include elections that took place in periods coded as undemocratic by all four datasets listed above. We have chosen this approach to allow researchers themselves to select elections that they deem to be “democratic enough” for their research interest. We have rewritten the first paragraph of the Section “Democratic Electoral Systems, 1919-1949” to clarify our approach.
Forth, Düzgün Arslantas mentions conceptual issues with the term “democratic electoral system” since this suggests that there are also undemocratic elections.
Response
: We carefully disagree here but believe that this disagreement is rooted in a misunderstanding of our position due to a lack of clarity in the previous version of the manuscript. There are indeed elections taking place in non-democratic systems (e.g., Russia as of today), and there is a large body of literature surrounding these elections (see for example Gandhi and Lust-Okar, 2009). But of course, we do not claim that political systems such as Russia are democracies only because they have elections. This is why we fall back on the four datasets listed above to define our universe of cases: elections that happen in a (at least minimally) democratic environment. We ignore elections that take place in non-democratic systems.
