Pedro Riera’s main concern has been that electoral systems per se play a minor role in the description of the dataset.
Response
: We appreciate this feedback. In addition to the Figures and descriptive analyses we had already included in the first version of the manuscript, we have now added Figure 4 and a corresponding description, providing more details about the distribution of electoral systems across the world.
Pedro Riera has also wandered whether the DES data 1919-1945 contains information on the electoral thresholds, and if not, why this is the case.
Response
: The DES 1919-1945 data does not contain information on electoral thresholds because it went beyond the resources of this research project. Consider the following historical and contemporary examples: In Romania, ethnic minority representatives have a lower threshold to reach than their ethnic majority colleagues. In Poland, the threshold is lower for parties than for alliances of parties. In New Zealand and Germany, the electoral threshold only applies to parties that do not win a seat directly. Unlike in countries where thresholds are typically based on the percentage of votes a party gained, Slovenia bases its threshold on the number of seats won. In interwar Czechoslovakia, the threshold was based the absolute number of votes won, not the percentage – only determining whether a party could participate in distribution of votes on the second tier. Thus, deciding which electoral threshold to code and how to deal with many exceptional cases would have taken considerable time. We therefore kept the focus on the original DES coding instructions.
Pedro Riera suggested to include information on the constitutional inter-election periods.
Response
: As collecting data on on inter-election periods was fairly straight forward, we added two new variables to the DES 1919-1945: one indicates the length of the term for presidents and parliaments (in years), respectively, the other indicates the intervals in which elections are held (in years) in case parliaments are only renewed partially. For example, Argentina’s members of parliament were elected for four years, but elections took place every two years for half of the parliament.
Pedro Riera furthermore raised some concerns regarding two “dubious theoretical claims”: The first is about the effect of the geographical clustering of parliamentary systems in Europe on their survival, and the second refers to the non-existing difference between the number of candidates running for presidential elections in plurality vs. absolute majority systems between 1919 and 1945.
Response
: We want to thank Pedro Riera for carefully reading our manuscript. We deleted the first theoretical claim, mostly because this note is supposed to present the dataset, and not to defend novel theoretical arguments. We now claim that the discrepancy between the interwar and post-World War II periods “raises important theoretical questions regarding the stability of presidential versus parliamentary systems.” Regarding the second claim: We added that this is a claim about presidential elections. The p residential and semi-presidential regimes in our sample are younger on average than parliamentary ones. For example, parliamentary Denmark, France, Norway, Switzerland, the UK had been democratic for more than ten years when our data starts recording elections in 1919. Among presidential democracies, only the US had been democratic for more than ten years.
Pedro Riera furthermore suggested to provide examples, primarily when speaking about how uncommon mixed systems were in the interwar period.
Response
: We added France and Iceland as examples in the text. Denmark only ran one mixed election in 1918, a year that does not fall into our sampling period.
Finally, Pedro Riera wondered why we did not include a figure about the frequency of PR electoral formulas.
Response
: The main reason is that such a graph is hard to read. There are 19 different PR electoral formulas, several of them with very few elections. We find it more informative to describe the frequency of the main formulas in the text.
