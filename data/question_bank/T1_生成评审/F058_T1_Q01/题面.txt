Limitations and suggestions for further research\nIt is essential to acknowledge the limitations of this study\nand outline the prospective next steps in similar research.\nFirstly, our primary objective was not to find out whether the\nchatbot can serve as a fully functional evaluator but rather\nto explore whether a P/CVE professional can obtain the key\ninformation and support necessary to plan and implement their\nown P/CVE work with evaluation in mind. Our purpose was also\nnot to assess the chatbot’s overall performance in the P/CVE\nfield or evaluation, but instead to create the first mapping of\nits potential as of an AI tool for professionals.\nA further limitation concerns access to the chatbot. To\nexplore Copilot, we utilised the open-access version of Copilot without logging in, which can be regarded as a constraint\non our study. Accessing the full version, which requires an\nOffice 365 account, would have enabled us to leverage additional functionalities, such as, for instance, image generation or voice communication (in some regions). These are not\navailable in the open-access version but exploring them would\nbroaden the spectrum of use-cases for P/CVE. This is something\nto be considered in further studies. We also explored LLM chatbots mainly as a knowledge-based recommender system but\nmore studies could be done to explore other functions of chatbots, especially task-specific ones, by using the full access to\nthe system and exploring the new agentic features of the chatbot. To add further to research on the performance of such chatbots in various languages, functions such as usability could be\nbetter explored.\n\nThere are also several technical limitations worth noting. One key limitation concerns the number of iterations for\ninputting the prompts. There is an ongoing discussion about the\nvariability of responses from chatbots, which can impact credibility if not managed effectively. In this study we inputted our\nprompts in one iteration, whereas multiple iterations might be\nnecessary to explore the stability of responses. In 2024, we conducted a similar review of Copilot responses13 in three iterations\nand did not spot a significant divergence in the answers received.\nGiven the rapidly improving performance of LLMs and chatbots, including their stability, such an evaluation was deemed\nbeyond the scope of this study. Moreover, the differences in performance between various LLMs and chatbots highlight the\ncomplexity of assessing these models’ capabilities.\nThe discussion with Copilot was based on a range of prompts\nengineered by the authors, aimed at simulating user experience. Prompt engineering refers to the techniques used to guide\nconversations with LLM-based chatbots to yield effective and\nrelevant responses (Ekin, 2023; Giray, 2023; White et al., 2023).\nThis study drew upon the authors’ extensive interactions with\nEuropean and non-European practitioners and policymakers\nin the field of P/CVE, providing insight into their perspectives\non evidence-based evaluation and the needs of the field. It is\nwell established that unlocking the full potential of chatbots\nand achieving optimal results from queries requires the user\nto know how to formulate effective prompts. However, not all\nusers possess this knowledge, which can impact the quality of\ninteractions with these systems. Furthermore, involving real\npractitioners in such a study would have added richness and\ndepth to the research findings. This would be even more so if we\nwere able to detect more nuances in the usage of these systems\nthrough studying interactions of different P/CVE professionals\nwith chatbots.\n\nc. Although\nCopilot provided a response, it listed evaluation frameworks,\nuseful resources, and guides instead. This indicated a certain\ndegree of inaccuracy, but at the same time echoed the wellestablished fact that there are yet not many (publicly available)\nevaluations in P/CVE.\nThere were instances when the answers looked somewhat\nimprecise, with concepts and ideas being mixed. For example, participatory evaluation was listed as an evaluation method\n(Prompt 2), whereas it is instead an approach. Another case\ninvolved cost-benefit analysis being listed both as a method\nand a type of evaluation in responses to prompts no. 2 and\n7. This might be confusing when trying to understand how and\nin which situations it can be applied. Nevertheless, these details\nare relatively minor inaccuracies that might also go unnoticed\nby practitioners given the overall accurate and straightforward\nperformance of the chatbot. Additionally, Copilot’s answers\nin these cases were not necessarily misleading; they simply\nrequired more precise classification.\nIn our simulation case, where we posed as practitioners\nwith limited knowledge of the filed, we explored whether an\nunderstanding of evaluation could be built up gradually through\na series of prompts. The chain of thought prompt provided a\nclear pathway to key knowledge and opened up additional pathways for further exploitation within each suggested category. It\nis worth noting that in older versions of Copilot (2023–2024) the\nchatbot in the open-access version could suggest follow-up\nquestions to continue and drive the discussion, which is no\nlonger the case in the latest version. Such follow-up questions\ncould be instrumental for newcomers to the topic but might\nalso stifle creative and active thinking during learning, potentially turning users into passive learners. This limitation would\nprobably restrict Copilot’s ability to truly “learn” from user\nbehaviour and predict preferences and interests.\nFinally, the original answers sometimes lacked references\nand sources. However, users can request references as part\nof their prompts but not retroactively. The sources offered\nby Copilot were based on a mix of American and European\nopen-source materials available online. There is still room\nfor more precise quotations and references that can be easily\naccessed within the same webpage rather than requiring users to\nvisit external websites.\n2. Relevance and integrity. Our analysis of relevance and integrity includes the study of responses from all 50 pre-designed\nprompts. By carefully reviewing the answers, we established\nthat Copilot is capable of leading conversations about basic\ntopics related to evidence-based evaluation in the field P/CVE.\nAs previously noted, establishing a more thematically rich\ndialogue with Copilot requires a session where all prompts\nare produced sequentially. If all the prompts are generated\nwithin one session, Copilot readily captures the thematic context, behavioural preferences, and interests of the user, providing\nan immersive and engaging dialogue within the topic limits.\n\n\nWe also received promising results from scenario-based prompts,\nwhere the context is described directly in the prompt rather\nthan extracted from the ongoing dialogue. Therefore, both\ntechniques—sequential prompting and scenario-based prompting—\nare effective for obtaining meaningful recommendations from\nCopilot.\nAll key aspects of evidence-based P/CVE evaluation from\nTable 1 were included in the prompts. Copilot’s responses in\nrelation to relevance and integrity were generally much better than satisfactory. For instance, by prompts no. 22 and 22,\nwhich asked about gender and ethics without directly mentioning evaluation or P/CVE (presumably because the context was\nalready established), we received valuable information. The\nresponse to prompt 22 provided insights on the importance of\nconsidering gender in P/CVE evaluations, offered relevant recommendations, included practical examples for gender-sensitive\nevaluations, and supplied external links to sources. Similarly, in\nprompt no. 23, Copilot emphasised the significance of human\nrights in evaluation, gave hands-on guidance on conducting\na human-rights-based evaluation, and directed us to additional\nresources. As a result, through the detailed knowledge and\nguiding instructions provided by Copilot, practitioners can\naccess almost all the details necessary for evidence-based evaluations, enriched with (mostly) valuable examples and supported\nby external sources.\nCopilot demonstrated its great potential as a recommender system by proving hands-on advice and recommendations in both\none-shot and few-shot prompts. When we asked for practical\nassistance to get started with various aspects of evaluation\n(since starting can often be challenging), Copilot offered concrete tips on initial and subsequent steps. In prompts no. 12, 15,\n18, and 43, Copilot provided detailed step-by-step instructions. Each set of recommendations began by setting up relevant\nobjectives before moving on to further steps.\nCopilot was also helpful in guiding us through options. For\ninstance, in scenario prompt no. 41, we asked for recommendations on whether to choose an internal or external\nevaluator for a police case evaluation. Copilot recognised the sensitivity of this context and recommended engaging an internal\nevaluator. The recommendation was highlighted in bold letters\nto emphasise Copilot’s preferred option. While such recommendations are valuable, it is still crucial to weigh all pros and cons\nindependently before making a final decision, ensuring personal\naccountability.\nIn session 1, during our extensive conversation, Copilot demonstrated a clear understanding of our interests and could predict our subsequent prompts somewhat. For example, when\nwe asked about the Theory of Change (Prompt no. 18),\nCopilot provided an example relevant to P/CVE, anticipating our next question (prepared in Prompt 19). This ability to\nanticipate future needs showcased Copilot’s effectiveness as a\nconversational assistant.\nScenario-based single-shot prompts demonstrated that clearer\ncommunication with Copilot can lead to better results. The effectiveness of communication largely depends on practitioners’\n\npreparedness and awareness of various aspects of P/CVE.\nIn scenario prompt no. 42 we posed as a practitioner who\nwas unfamiliar with P/CVE but operated in a disengagement\nprogramme and wanted to understand how to assess whether the\nprogramme was effective. Without directly using phrases like\n“P/CVE” or “evaluation”, our goal was to see if Copilot would\ndiscuss evaluation concepts. Contrary to expectations, Copilot\ndid not explicitly mention “evaluation” but instead focused\non specific forms of it, such as impact assessment and selfassessment. In this context, a real well-informed evaluation\nexpert might recommend referring the practitioner to process evaluations or formative evaluations, which would be more\nrelevant given the scenario. At the same time, self-assessment\nremains a good starting point for practitioners to begin thinking\nabout some evaluation strategies.\nUnderstanding how relevant and integral Copilot’s responses\nare under complex scenarios was one of the main intentions\nof this study. In the second session, each prompt contained\na significant amount of information delivered in a single shot\nwithout building pre-context through prior prompts. We wondered whether Copilot could orient itself effectively to the initial\ndata and respond comprehensively to all the details provided.\nWe were particularly interested in how well Copilot would\nincorporate specific details such as geographical data or programme focus into its responses, producing more tailored and\ncontext-specific answers. As a result, we discovered that\ngeographical details in our prompts (Prompts no. 42, 43, 46,\nand 48) were not consistently taken up to produce more tailored responses. On the other hand, Copilot provided more\ncontextualised information when it was given specific organisational specialisations, such as working with returnees or young\npeople. This suggests that while Copilot can handle complex\nprompts, not all the details provided can lead to a more precise\nand tailored answer.\nSurprisingly, Copilot acted as a rather emotional, supportive, and understanding mentor throughout our interactions.\nFor example, in prompt no. 49, the scenario described the fear\nof evaluations and insecurities about external evaluators. In\nresponse, Copilot was empathetic but also very encouraging, offering practical advice to help overcome these fears and\nresolve the issue constructively. Copilot provided reassurance\nwhile emphasising the importance of preparing the team for the\nevaluation process. This included suggesting ways to familiarise\nthe team with the evaluation methods and objectives, ensuring transparency and open communication, and providing\ntraining or workshops to build confidence and competence.\nBy doing so, Copilot helped create a supportive environment\nthat encouraged the team to face their fears head-on.\n3. Readability and comprehensibility. We all know how overwhelming it can be to absorb new information. The language\nused to explain this information, along with visual aids,\nplays a crucial role in learning as they affect both readability\nand comprehensibility. We explored these criteria primarily through the first 40 prompts in Session 1 and several\nscenario-based prompts in Session 2. To investigate these criteria, we repeatedly informed Copilot that we were practitioners\n\n\nwho lacked extensive knowledge about evaluation and sought\nguidance and assistance.\nDuring the first session, Copilot effectively gauged our level\nof knowledge through a number of prompts. To enhance readability and comprehensibility, Copilot employed several\ntechniques to adapt its responses to our level of expertise.\nFirstly, as we had already concluded during the analysis of\nrelevance, Copilot excelled in breaking down complex queries into simpler parts and addressing each part separately for\nbetter comprehension. This approach was particularly evident\nwhen we explicitly positioned ourselves as novices in evaluation.\nSecondly, Copilot utilised diverse visual aids that effectively broke down content into more digestible chunks (See\nFigure 1 and Figure 2). Although the version of Copilot we\nused (without sign-in) produced graphs with limited creativity and color, given the current capabilities of similar systems,\nwe believe Copilot could offer more impactful and creative visualisations. Notably, Copilot continued to provide these visual aids\nthroughout the session without us directly requesting them.\nThirdly, at the end of some prompts, Copilot proactively summarised responses or proposed checklists, making it easier\nfor us to retain all key points.\nThese methods, such as breaking down complex information into simpler parts, providing diverse visual aids, and summarising responses, could be instrumental in developing\ntailored processes based on individual needs and culture. They\nlay a strong foundation for initiating evaluation efforts.\n\nFigure 2. Visualisation produced by Copilot in prompt no. 16a\n(screenshot).\n\nConclusion\n\nFigure 1. Visualisation produced by Copilot in prompt no. 16\n(screenshot).\n\nIn contrast to widely discussed research on the risks associated with AI LLMs in addressing P/CVE, this study highlights\nthe instrumental value of using LLMs for P/CVE efforts. We\nexamined Copilot, a publicly available chatbot that combines\nan LLM with recommender system capabilities, as a practical\ntool to enhance evidence-based policy and practice in the field.\nThe study involved a literature review and analysis of Copilot’s\nresponses to 50 pre-designed prompts. This analysis provided\npreliminary conclusions about the practical value of using\nCopilot. Although the study had certain limitations and could\nbenefit from more rigorous multi-method approaches, it demonstrated that Copilot can serve primarily as an educational tool\nfor P/CVE practitioners. Crucially, Copilot can also function\nas a companion and mentor in P/CVE work by offering emotional support through natural language dialogues. By engaging\n\n\n\nusers in meaningful conversations, Copilot can inspire and\nencourage them to acquire new knowledge about the subject matter. This approach can help overcome barriers such as fear of\nthe unknown and foster positive planning and utilisation of new\ninformation within organisations.\nIn this study, we investigated how useful Copilot can be in\nunderstanding complex processes, models, and approaches\nrelated to planning and conducting evaluations. Our findings\ndemonstrated that chatbots like Copilot can provide comprehensive knowledge on key aspects of evaluation, including\ndefinitions, evaluation types, methods, and processes. Copilot\nwas particularly effective in demonstrating and visualising evaluation pathways and models, as well as providing ideas for initial steps towards evaluations. The question remains, however,\nwhether Copilot’s responses alone are sufficient to kick-start\nthe process effectively in practise. The chatbot also provided\nrelevant literature and additional sources that can significantly\ndeepen practitioners’ knowledge. Nonetheless, it is crucial\nto ensure that these sources are systematically selected and\nrigorously checked for accuracy and currency, so they can be\nreliably consulted by P/CVE professionals.\nAt this stage, it is essential to address concerns around accuracy and reliability, which may be influenced by the quality and diversity of training data. One major weakness is the\ntrustworthiness and accuracy of all the bits of information provided by Copilot that still require professional knowledge and\nexpertise to filter responses. Evidently, it is still important\nto keep not only a human-in-the-loop but also a professionalin-the-loop to verify the accuracy of the arguments in suggested\nresponses. To increase the value of such tools, incorporating\nmore diverse and high-quality training data specific to the\nP/CVE field would be crucial. Implementing fact-checking\nmechanisms to ensure the accuracy of information provided is\nanother way to enhance its performance and reliability.\nAccording to our review, the pros of this solution still outweigh the cons. The strongest aspect of Copilot was its ability\nto break complex content down into smaller, more digestible\npieces, helping practitioners navigate through the sea of information without having to spend time searching, selecting,\nand reviewing different resources on the Internet in the traditional way. This capability might be especially promising for\n\noptimising practice, making it easier and more efficient for\npractitioners to access and utilise relevant information. The\nvoice function that is already available in some models can\nmake this experience even more natural and effective.\nThe overall development of LLMs is expected to continue influencing the performance of chatbots like Copilot,\nenhancing their accuracy and relevance over time. As these\nmodels improve, they will become increasingly suitable for\nmultipurpose professional use, including in the P/CVE field.\nHowever, it remains crucial to develop and apply user-centred\napproaches to critically assess their output when considering the use of such tools to inform professionals. Pondering\nboth the potential and limitations of using chatbots like Copilot\nfor evidence-based evaluation in the P/CVE field, LLM-based\nchatbots are worth further exploration to identify more opportunities that this technology can bring to other types of P/CVE\nwork. Regular reviews should be conducted to identify new\nopportunities related to the rapid development of LLMs and\ntheir applications in the P/CVE field.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required\n\nData availability\nUnderlying data\n\nZenodo: Copilot’s responses, January 2025.
