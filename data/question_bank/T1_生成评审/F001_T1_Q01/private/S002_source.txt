Exploring self-compassion in adults with disabilities: The roles
of gender, disability history, and leisure-time physical activity

Objectives:
This study aimed to examine self-compassion levels in adults with disabilities and identify key inﬂuencing
factors, focusing on gender, disability history, and engagement in leisure-time physical activity.
Methods:
A cross-sectional study was conducted with 162 adults with disabilities (85 males and 77 females; mean
age = 31.3 ± 10.69). Self-compassion was measured using the validated Self-Compassion Scale (SCS),
which assesses six key components. Leisure-time physical activity was evaluated through self-reported
weekly frequency and duration.
Results:
Participants exhibited a moderate level of self-compassion (M = 3.49, SD = 0.58). Among the
components, over-identiﬁcation (M = 3.71, SD = 0.92) and self-judgment (M = 3.69, SD = 0.90) had the
highest scores, while self-kindness (M = 3.19, SD = 0.82) had the lowest. Regression analysis revealed a
signiﬁcant model, F (3, 158) = 10.833, p <.001, accounting for 17.1% of the variance in self-compassion
(R² = 0.171). Leisure-time physical activity engagement and disability history were signiﬁcant positive
predictors of self-compassion, while gender was not signiﬁcantly associated.
Conclusions:
Adults with disabilities demonstrated moderate self-compassion, with notable tendencies toward selfcriticism and over-identiﬁcation. Engagement in leisure-time physical activity and disability history
emerged as signiﬁcant predictors, highlighting the importance of promoting physical activity and
addressing the unique experiences of individuals with congenital versus acquired disabilities.

Introduction

2

Self-compassion is an important psychological concept that involves being kind and

3

understanding toward oneself during times of struggle or failure, rather than engaging in harsh

4

self-criticism [1]. It includes three key aspects: self-kindness, common humanity, and

5

mindfulness. Self-kindness means offering support and care to oneself in difficult moments

6

instead of self-judgment. Common humanity refers to recognizing that challenges and setbacks

7

are a shared part of being human. Mindfulness involves maintaining a balanced perspective on

8

painful emotions, avoiding both ignoring and exaggerating them [1]. These elements help

9

individuals face challenges with greater resilience and acceptance.

10

Research consistently demonstrates the positive effects of self-compassion on various emotional

11

and psychological domains. It fosters positive emotions such as happiness, gratitude, and

12

optimism, which contribute to a more favorable outlook on life [2]. Moreover, self-compassion

13

enhances resilience, enabling individuals to effectively manage stress and adversity [3]. These

14

benefits underscore the critical role of self-compassion in promoting psychological well-being

15

and emotional strength.

16

Self-compassion is strongly associated with life satisfaction, a fundamental measure of overall

17

wellbeing and quality of life [4]. Extensive research demonstrates a positive relationship between

18

these constructs, showing that individuals with greater self-compassion tend to report higher

19

levels of well-being [e.g., 5, 6].

20

This connection may arise from self-compassionate individuals' ability to manage stress and

21

negative emotions, fostering a more positive and optimistic perspective on life [7]. These

22

attributes are especially relevant for individuals with disabilities, as they assist in accepting

23

limitations and coping with challenges. Additionally, self-compassion is associated with

24

heightened motivation and personal growth, which further contribute to life satisfaction and

25

overall well-being [3].

26

For individuals with disabilities, self-compassion holds unique significance. They frequently

27

encounter societal barriers and challenges that can negatively impact their psychological health.

28

In recent years, there has been growing attention to the psychological well-being of people with

29

disabilities, with research aiming to identify factors that enhance mental health and self-

30

perception in this population. Self-compassion can empower individuals with disabilities by

fostering acceptance of their limitations and promoting emotional resilience. Studies indicate that

32

self-compassion is linked to reduced levels of depression and anxiety, equipping individuals with

33

strategies to manage stress and negative emotions, particularly when dealing with disability-

34

related challenges [3]. Moreover, self-compassion supports adaptation to changes in physical or

35

psychological abilities, enabling individuals to navigate and overcome challenges [8].

36

Beyond emotional resilience, self-compassion contributes to reducing internalized stigma and

37

fostering a healthier self-image. It also strengthens social connectedness, as self-compassionate

38

individuals are more likely to seek support and accept help, thereby enhancing their social

39

networks [9]. This social connectedness is particularly meaningful for individuals with

40

disabilities, as it creates a supportive environment that positively impacts overall well-being.

41

Self-compassion also plays a role in promoting physical health by encouraging behaviors that

42

support well-being, such as regular physical activity and adherence to medical treatments [10].

43

Notably, leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) has been found to enhance psychological well-

44

being by building emotional resilience and reducing stress. The interaction between self-

45

compassion and LTPA is particularly significant, as engaging in LTPA may further cultivate

46

self-compassion by providing individuals with opportunities to care for their bodies and minds,

47

while self-compassion can motivate consistent participation in LTPA by fostering a kinder, more

48

supportive attitude toward personal health and fitness.

49

Building on this evidence, the present study investigates the relationship between engagement in

50

LTPA and self-compassion among individuals with disabilities. While existing research

51

highlights the positive effects of physical activity on life satisfaction and quality of life in this

52

population (e.g., [11]), little attention has been given to its specific influence on self-compassion.

53

Recognizing the established role of LTPA in fostering psychological resilience, this study seeks

54

to explore how LTPA may contribute to self-compassion in individuals with disabilities. By

55

addressing this gap, the research aims to deepen our understanding of the psychological benefits

56

of LTPA and its potential to enhance self-compassion and overall well-being in this population.

Measures

68

Self-Compassion Scale

69

The Self-Compassion Scale (SCS), developed by Kristin Neff [1], is a widely used tool for

70

measuring self-compassion. The scale comprises 26 items designed to measure six key

71

components of self-compassion: self-kindness (5 items; e.g., "I’m kind to myself when I am

72

experiencing suffering"), self-judgment (5 items; e.g., "I’m intolerant and impatient toward those

73

aspects of my personality I don’t like"), common humanity (4 items; e.g., "When I’m down and

74

out, I remind myself that there are lots of other people in the world feeling like I am"), isolation

75

(4 items; e.g., "When I fail at something I tend to feel alone in my failure"), mindfulness (4

76

items; e.g., "When I’m feeling down I try to approach my feelings with curiosity and openness"),

77

and over-identification (4 items; e.g., "When something upsets me I get carried away with my

78

feelings"). Each item of the self-compassion scale is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from

79

1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always), with higher scores reflecting greater levels of self-compassion.

80

Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always), where

81

higher scores indicate greater self-compassion.

82

The SCS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency

83

(Cronbach's alpha > 0.86) and robust construct validity, confirming its reliability across different

84

populations and research contexts [1, 12, 13]. This scale has been extensively used in research to

85

explore self-compassion's connection with various psychological outcomes, such as well-being,

86

resilience, and mental health.

96

97

98

Discussion

151

This study aimed to explore self-compassion and its components in adults with disabilities,

152

shedding light on how psychological and social factors might shape their self-perceptions and

153

emotional well-being.

154

The study's findings indicate that participants exhibited a moderate level of self-compassion,

155

with an average score of 3.49 (SD = 0.58). This aligns with prior research suggesting that

156

individuals with disabilities often face unique psychological and social challenges that influence

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2024:10:107843:0:5:NEW 17 Dec 2024)

Manuscript to be reviewed
157

their self-compassion [14]. Comparable patterns have been observed in other studies [e.g.15],

158

which reported that individuals experiencing external pressures or stigma are more prone to self-

159

judgment and over-identification with their difficulties. Furthermore, the results partially align

160

with findings from a previous study on non-disabled adults in the same context [16], pointing to

161

some similarities in self-compassion levels between the two samples.

162

Among the six self-compassion components, Over-Identification (M = 3.71, SD = 0.92) and

163

Self-Judgment (M = 3.69, SD = 0.90) had the highest scores. These findings suggest that

164

participants may have the tendency to criticize themselves and ruminate negative thoughts.

165

In contrast, Self-Kindness scored the lowest (M = 3.19, SD = 0.82), indicating that participants

166

found it more difficult to treat themselves with compassion and understanding. This is consistent

167

with Gilbert findings, which highlight that persistent stressors, such as living with physical or

168

social limitations, can make it challenging to develop self-kindness [17]. Mindfulness (M = 3.33,

169

SD = 0.82) and Common Humanity (M = 3.45, SD = 0.84) had moderate scores, reflecting an

170

average ability to maintain emotional balance and recognize shared human experiences. These

171

results highlight specific areas of concern, particularly in relation to high levels of self-criticism

172

and difficulties in practicing self-kindness. Interventions aimed at enhancing self-compassion

173

among individuals with disabilities should prioritize reducing self-judgment, fostering self-

174

kindness, and encouraging mindfulness practices to support emotional balance.

175

The regression analysis revealed significant insights into the relationships between self-

176

compassion and key predictors, with disability history and LTPA engagement emerging as

177

meaningful factors. Gender, on the other hand, did not significantly predict self-compassion

178

within this sample.

179

LTPA engagement was identified as a significant positive predictor of self-compassion. The

180

results indicated that higher levels of physical activity were associated with greater self-

181

compassion (B = 0.001, β = 0.196, p = 0.009). This aligns with previous research highlighting

182

the psychological benefits of physical activity, such as improved emotional regulation and self-

183

awareness [18]. Engaging in physical activity may promote mindfulness and help individuals

184

reduce over-identification with negative thoughts, equipping them with tools to nurture self-

185

compassion.

186

The most significant predictor of self-compassion was disability history. Participants with

187

acquired disabilities reported higher levels of self-compassion compared to those with congenital

188

disabilities (B = 0.299, β = 0.257, p = 0.001). This finding supports previous studies suggesting

189

that individuals who acquire disabilities often develop resilience and adaptive coping strategies,

190

which enhance their capacity for self-compassion [19]. Conversely, individuals with congenital

191

disabilities may face long-term exposure to societal stigma, which could negatively affect the

192

development of self-compassion [17].

193

Lastly, the analysis showed that gender was not a statistically significant predictor of self-

194

compassion in this sample (B = -0.153, β = -0.133, p = 0.089). This is consistent with Davies et

195

al. [20], who found that gender differences in self-compassion are typically minor, especially in

196

contexts where individuals share profound life experiences, such as living with a disability.

197

In conclusion, this study highlights the important and independent roles of LTPA engagement

198

and disability history in influencing self-compassion. These findings provide useful insights for

199

creating strategies to promote mental well-being and resilience in different populations.

200

Furthermore, the study emphasizes the need to consider cultural and religious contexts when

201

examining self-compassion, particularly in regions like Oman, where religious teachings heavily

202

influence individual behavior.

203

Future research should prioritize examining LTPA in adults with disabilities, focusing on how

204

interventions that encourage both LTPA and self-compassion can benefit various cultural and

205

demographic groups. Such studies are valuable not only for expanding theoretical knowledge but

206

also for creating practical strategies to enhance mental health in diverse populations.

Limitations

209

A main limitation of this study is the use of a convenience sample of 162 adults with disabilities.

210

Although this approach made data collection easier, it may not accurately represent the broader

211

population of disabled adults, as participants might differ in self-compassion levels from those

212

who did not participate. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported data for both self-

213

compassion and LTPA engagement, which may introduce biases, such as social desirability bias,

214

potentially affecting the accuracy of the findings.
