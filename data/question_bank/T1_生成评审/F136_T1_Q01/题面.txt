Literature review\n2.1 Theoretical literature review\nFI, from a macroeconomic perspective, is seen as a catalyst for “creative destruction,” promoting economic growth,\nreducing inequality, and enhancing financial stability. However, at the micro level, excessive FI can lead to opportunistic\nbehaviors, such as fund misuse and deliberate payment delays, which may undermine financial stability (Hua et al.,\n2023). The impacts of FI are neither uniform nor static; they vary across different temporal and spatial contexts. This has\nled to divergent views among researchers regarding its impact on bank stability, with two divergent schools of thought\n\n\nemerging as the one positing a FI-stability effect and the other suggesting a FI-instability outcome (Damrah et al., 2023;\nMoaz, 2022). To that end, the relationship between FI and bank stability has become a central theme in both academic and\n\u0014\npolicy discourse (Cihák\net al., 2016). This study employs a multi-theoretical approach, drawing on asymmetric\ninformation theory, financial intermediation theory, systemic risk theory, and portfolio theory. These theoretical\nunderpinnings collectively offer a nuanced understanding of the pathways through which FI can effect bank stability\nin Ethiopia.\nAsymmetric information theory, highlighted by Akerlof (1970), addresses the challenges posed by uneven information\ndistribution in financial markets. In FI, this imbalance is particularly significant as institutions serve previously unbanked\npopulations. Difficulties in assessing creditworthiness can lead to adverse selection, hindering credit risk management\nand operational efficiency, which may compromise financial stability (Oanh et al., 2023; Moaz, 2022; Sethy & Goyari,\n2022; Barik & Pradhan, 2021; Khan, 2011; Bofondi & Gobbi, 2003). Balancing information asymmetry within FI context\ncan thus strengthen the financial system.\nThe financial intermediation theory underscores the pivotal role of banks in linking savers with borrowers (Scholtens &\nWensveen, 2003; Diamond, 1984), thereby facilitating FI. By providing liquidity, managing risks, and bridging\ninformational gaps, banks contribute to the efficient allocation of capital and the mitigation of risks, which enhances\noverall financial stability (Servigny & Renault, 2004). This theory emphasizes that by expanding financial access,\nparticularly in developing economies where financial exclusion is significant, banks can improve their performance by\nbroadening their customer base, increasing deposits, and fostering a more resilient financial system (Kim et al., 2018;\nDiamond, 1984). This phenomenon lessens information asymmetry, thereby lessens market imperfections (Oanh et al.,\n2023).\nSystemic risk theory, as developed by Minsky (1982) and later refined by Borio (2011), addresses the interconnectedness\nof financial institutions and the amplification of shocks through mechanisms such as leverage, asset price bubbles, and\nprocyclicality. Borio differentiates systemic risk into temporal and cross-sectional dimensions. Temporal risk involves\nthe dynamic evolution of aggregate risk, while cross-sectional risk pertains to the distribution of risk within the financial\nsystem, influenced by common exposures and counterparty risks. Within the context of FI, Khan (2011) suggests that\nexpanding borrower bases can lower lending standards, potentially destabilizing the financial system. Conversely,\nHannig and Jansen (2010) argue that FI can enhance stability by diversifying deposit and loan bases, involving segments\nless susceptible to economic cycles, while Prasad (2010) highlights the positive effects of credit access on employment\nand economic growth.\nPortfolio theory highlights the significance of diversification in mitigating risk (Markowitz, 1952). In the context of FI, a\ndiversified loan portfolio can potentially reduce the impact of credit losses on individual banks and the overall financial\nsystem (Beck et al., 2013; Wang & Lin, 2021). Adem (2022) concurs, noting that diversification lowers bankruptcy risk\nfor banks. Khan (2011) outlines three main ways of mitigating the FI on financialinstability: diversification of bank assets\nthrough increased lending to smaller firms reduces portfolio risk; an expanded and stable deposit base lessens reliance on\nvolatile non-core financing; and improved monetary policy transmission enhances stability. However, as Huynh & Dang\n(2021) argue, the effectiveness of diversification hinges on the correlation between loan segments and the portfolio's\noverall risk profile.\nDrawing on these theoretical foundations, this study offers a unique lens through which the impact of FIonbank stability\ncan be comprehended. The theories collectively guide our understanding of how FI initiatives can be designed to support\nthe broader financial servicesaccess and the long-term stability of the financial system.\n2.2 Empirical review\nEmpirical research regarding the impact of FI on the stability of financial systems presents varied outcomes (Damrah\net al., 2023; Feghali et al., 2021; Vo et al., 2020). Specifically, past levels of\nbank stability significantly impact present stability, as corroborated by the study of Yitayaw et al. (2023) within the\nEthiopian context. The positive effect of lagged stability on current conditions underscores its validity as a reliable\ninstrument for stability analysis, given that banks generally exhibit continuity in their stability across different periods,\nreflecting a trend of persistence over time.\n\n\n\nOur findings confirm a significant positive relationship between FI and bank stability, supported by existing research\n(Koudalo & Toure, 2023; Nguyen & Du, 2022; Vo et al., 2020; Le et al., 2019; Neaime & Gaysset, 2018; Morgan &\nPontines, 2014). Specifically, a 1% increase in FI is associated with an 84.07 unit increase in bank stability in the short\nrun, ceteris paribus. Enhanced FI strengthens bank stability by diversifying bank assets, reducing systemic and liquidity\nrisks, and improving monetary policy transmission (Ahamed & Mallick, 2019; Morgan & Pontines, 2014; Khan, 2011;\nHannig & Jansen, 2010). It also addresses information asymmetry by allowing lenders to better assess borrowers and by\nproviding banks with critical proprietary information (Nguyen & Du, 2022), thus fostering a more resilient banking\nsector.\nOur findings indicate that control variables such as lnTA, EF, IND, and GDP growth rate significantly affect bank\nstability. Larger banks exhibit decreased stability, with a 10.03 unit decline per percentage increase in total assets in the\nshort run, ceteris paribus. This may be due to increased operational and systemic risks associated with larger institutions,\naligning with Vo et al. (2020) and Mu and Lin (2016), but differing from Nguyen and Du (2022) and Ahamed and Mallick\n(2019). Efficiency positively effect stability, with a 1% increase in efficiency leading to a 26.24 unit rise in stability in the\nshort run, ceteris paribus. This suggests that higher efficiency improves profitability and stability, consistent with Nguyen\nand Du (2022), and Ahamed and Mallick (2019). Income diversification negatively impacts stability, with a 1% increase\nresulting in a 101.7 unit decrease in stability in the short run, ceteris paribus. This might reflect the risks introduced by\ndiversification, aligning with Nguyen and Du (2022) but differing from Ahamed and Mallick (2019). In the Ethiopian\ncontext, where banks are predominantly dependent on deposit revenue, an abrupt or poorly managed shift towards greater\nnon-interest income might contribute to instability. GDP growth rate shows a positive effect on stability, with a 1%\nincrease leading to a 229.5 unit rise in stability in the short run, ceteris paribus. This suggests that economic growth\nenhances bank stability, consistent with Nguyen and Du (2022), Vo et al. (2020), and Ahamed and Mallick (2019).\nThe two-step System GMM analysis, as detailed in Table 9, shows that the standard diagnostic tests are supportive of the\nmodel’s validity. The AR(2) test (Prob > χ2 = 0.479) does not reject the null hypothesis, indicating no evidence of secondorder residual autocorrelation. Similarly, the Hansen test (Prob > χ2 = 0.951) confirms the validity of the instruments,\nverifying their appropriateness. The F-test (Prob > F = 0.000) demonstrates a strong goodness of fit for the model. In\naddition to the two-step robust System GMM results, findings from robust FE, robust Pooled OLS, robust one-step\nDifference GMM, robust two-step Difference GMM, and robust one-step System GMM are largely consistent. The main\nvariable, FI, is significant in both one-step and two-step System GMM models, while the GDP growth rate is significant in\nthe two-step robust System GMM. Overall, these results underscore the robustness of the employed models.\nIn addition, based on the result revealed in Table 10, the Juodis et al. This underscores the substantial role of FI in bolstering the resilience and stability of Ethiopia's banking\nsector. Additionally, reverse causality from bank stability to FI is significant at the 5% level, indicating that bank stability\ncan predict enhancements in FI. Conclusion and policy implication\nFinancial inclusiveness has increasingly become a focal point for policymakers, practitioners, and economists due to its\npotential to drive inclusive growth. Despite numerous efforts by policy makers and international financial institutions to\nbolster bank stability and inclusivity, the relationship between FI and bank stability remains contentious and inconsistent\nin existing literature. This study addresses this gap by examining the impact of FI on bank stability within the Ethiopian\ncontext. Using panel data from seventeen commercial banks over the period 2015-2023, the researchers employed a twostage PCA to develop FI index based on ten Convensional and five digital indicators. The effects of FI on bank stability\nwere analyzed using a two-step robust System GMM approach, complemented by Granger causality tests to determine\nthe direction of causality between FI and bank stability.\nOur results from the two-stage PCA indicate that Convensional availability is the most important factor in FI score,\nfollowed by Convensional usage, with digital accessibility and digital availability ranking third and fourth, respectively.\nThe System GMM analysis confirms that FI has a significant and positive effect on bank stability. Control variables such\nas efficiency and GDP growth rate are positively associated with bank stability, while total assets and income\ndiversification exhibit negative effects. Additionally, the historical level of bank stability positively effects current\nstability. The Granger causality tests indicate a bi-directional relationship between FI and bank stability, suggesting that\nimprovements in FI contribute to enhanced stability, and vice versa.\nThe policy implications are profound. To promote both FI and stability, policymakers should focus on expanding\ndemographic and geographic outreach, enhancing both convensional and digital financial services. Ensuring that current\nstability supports future stability is also crucial. FI and bank stability are mutually reinforcing; efforts to enhance FI\ndirectly contribute to improved stability and vice versa. Consistent with the policy implication of Vo et al. (2020),\nbroadening FI is essential for strengthening banking sector stability in Ethiopia. Expanding financial services to\nunderserved populations not only provides banks with stable funding sources but also enhances overall sector stability\nand profitability. Additionally, echoing Oanh et al. (2023) implications, governments should address financial exclusion\nby removing barriers to access, improving financial education, and developing infrastructure in underserved areas.\nPolicymakers must also ensure that initiatives to promote FI are supported by appropriate macroeconomic policies to\nsustain the positive relationship between FI and bank stability.\nEthics and consent\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\nData availability\nUnderlying data\nFigshare: Dataset for financial inclusion and stability in Ethiopia case, [external resource identifier omitted]\n(Arebo et al., 2024).\nData are available under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC-BY 4.0).\nReferences\nAbdu E: Financial distress situation of financial sectors in Ethiopia:\nA review paper.
