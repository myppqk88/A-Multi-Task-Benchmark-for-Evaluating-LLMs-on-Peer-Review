Background: Autonomous Sensory Meridian Response (ASMR) is a perceptual phenomenon in

26

which specific audiovisual stimuli consistently elicit tingling sensations on the scalp and

27

neck. These stimuli (“ASMR triggers”) are typically social in nature (e.g., watching someone

28

brush their hair, hearing whispering,) and often elicit a calm and positive emotional state that

29

may last up to several minutes. ASMR experiences phenomenologically overlap with

30

mindfulness; however, no research has directly examined how mindfulness might relate to

31

ASMR. Methods: In the current study, 284 individuals with ASMR completed the Toronto

32

Mindfulness Scale (TMS), the Mindful Attention and Awareness Scale (MAAS), and a

33

questionnaire examining ASMR experiences. Age- and sex-matched control participants were

34

asked to view two ASMR-eliciting videos to ensure that they did not experience tingling

35

sensations associated with ASMR; they then completed the TMS and MAAS questionnaires.

36

Results: When compared with matched controls, individuals with ASMR generated significantly

37

higher scores on the Mindful Attention and Awareness Scale, a global measure of mindfulness,

38

as well as significantly higher scores on the Curiosity subscale of the Toronto Mindfulness Scale.

39

40

ASMR may be partially explained by a distinct subset of characteristics associated with

41

mindfulness.

Autonomous Sensory Meridian Response (ASMR) is a perceptual phenomenon in which

43

specific stimuli, known as “ASMR triggers”, consistently elicit tingling sensations on the scalp,

44

neck, and shoulders, often spreading to the body’s periphery. These sensations are generally

45

accompanied by a sense of calm as well as by positive affect (Barratt & Davis, 2015). The

46

triggers that elicit these experiences vary widely between individuals and may be auditory,

47

visual, tactile, and/or olfactory in nature. Despite their heterogeneity, the most popular ASMR

48

triggers tend to be audio-visual; a recent survey study of individuals with ASMR found that

49

whispering and close-up attention elicited tingles in over half of the 450 respondents (Barratt &

50

Davis, 2015).

51

Although the phenomenology of ASMR overlaps with other atypical conscious states,

52

there are elements of the ASMR experience that appear to be unique. For instance, unlike other

53

sensory-emotional experiences such as frisson (i.e., “chills” elicited by music or an emotional

54

experience), which tend to last up to 10 seconds, individuals with ASMR may experience the

55

tingling sensations induced by a triggering stimulus for several minutes or more (del Campo &

56

Kehle, 2016; Fredborg, Clark, & Smith, 2017). In addition, the intensity and duration of ASMR

57

experiences are often under the control of the individual, a characteristic that distinguishes

58

ASMR from the automatic sensory responses of synesthesia. Another noteworthy quality of

59

ASMR is that the tingles tend to induce relaxation and positive affect in the individual. The fact

60

that ASMR is associated with both atypical sensory experiences and an emotional response

61

suggests that in order to fully understand ASMR, it will be necessary to examine other conditions

62

or experiences that produce similar emotional and sensory changes.

63

One conscious state that shares some phenomenological characteristics with ASMR is

64

mindfulness (Barratt & Davis, 2015). In their operationalization of mindfulness, Bishop and

3

65

colleagues (2004) define the construct as a two-component process by which one engages in both

66

intentional self-regulation of attention and a nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance of the

67

present moment. Other researchers have suggested that mindfulness involves an openness to

68

sensations, attentional control, emotional regulation, and resilience (Kabat-Zinn, 1990). Both of

69

these complementary descriptions of mindfulness overlap with elements of the ASMR

70

experience. For example, the focused attention method of mindfulness meditation requires

71

individuals to focus on a specific external stimulus or internal thought (Lutz, Slagter, Dunne, &

72

Davidson, 2008). During ASMR experiences, individuals focus attention on an external stimulus

73

that triggers tingling sensations. Both mindfulness and ASMR can lead to a feeling of relaxation

74

that enhances people’s subjective well-being (Barratt & Davis, 2004; Bishop et al., 2004). Given

75

these similarities, an examination of the relationship between ASMR and mindfulness seems

76

warranted.

77

The current research investigated the relationship between ASMR and both state and trait

78

mindfulness. State mindfulness reflects a transient, moment-to-moment conscious experience

79

(Tanay & Bernstein, 2013) whereas trait mindfulness is a more stable and enduring tendency to

80

attend to and experience the world in a mindful manner (Brown & Ryan, 2003). Individuals with

81

ASMR and matched control participants completed two well-established self-report measures of

82

mindfulness―the Toronto Mindfulness Scale, which is more sensitive to state mindfulness

83

(TMS; Lau et al., 2006), and the Mindful Attention and Awareness Scale, which is more

84

sensitive to the attentional component of trait mindfulness (MAAS; Brown & Ryan, 2003).

85

ASMR participants also completed the ASMR Checklist (Fredborg, Clark, & Smith, 2017),

86

which examines ASMR triggers and the phenomenology of ASMR experiences. We predicted

87

that individuals with ASMR would report higher levels of mindfulness, as measured by both the

4

88

TMS and the MAAS. We also predicted that individuals with ASMR who reported higher

89

intensity tingles in response to common triggering stimuli would score higher on both the TMS

90

and MAAS than individuals who reported lower intensity tingles. Together, these data would

91

provide novel insight into the phenomenology of ASMR, while also showing how this atypical

92

conscious state is related to mindful states and traits.

Questionnaires

115

The survey included several well-established questionnaires: the Big Five Inventory

116

(BFI; John, Donahue, & Kentle, 1991), the Mindful Attention and Awareness Scale (MAAS:

117

Brown & Ryan, 2003) and the Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS: Lau, Bishop, Segal, Buis,

118

119

120

121

Big Five personality traits and ASMR have been published separately (Fredborg et al., 2017).

122

The present report focuses on the TMS and the MAAS, and their relationship to data from the

123

ASMR Checklist.

124

The TMS consists of 13 self-report items that assess experiences of mindfulness

125

immediately preceding a meditation session (Lau et al., 2006). Individuals respond to each item

126

using a 5-point Likert-type scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“very much”). The 13 items on the

127

TMS load onto two subscales. Six items comprise the Curiosity subscale of the TMS, which is a

128

measure of one’s curiosity about any thoughts, emotions, and sensations that arise while

129

engaging in a mindful state. Examples of items on the Curiosity subscale include, “I was more

130

invested in just watching my experiences as they arose, than in figuring out what they could

131

mean,” and, “I remained curious about the nature of each experience as it arose.” The remaining

132

seven items comprise the Decentering subscale, which measures people’s awareness of their own

6

133

experiences. Items on the Decentering subscale include, “I experienced myself as separate from

134

my changing thoughts and feelings,” and, “I was aware of my thoughts and feelings without

135

overidentifying with them” (Lau et al., 2006). In its validation sample, both the Curiosity and

136

Decentering subscales of the TMS demonstrated good levels of internal consistency as measured

137

by Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.88 and α = 0.84, respectively). The instructions to this questionnaire

138

were slightly modified to be suitable for individuals with ASMR experiences.

139

The MAAS consists of 15 self-report items that load onto a single trait mindfulness factor

140

(Brown & Ryan, 2003). Items assess people’s ability to attend to and be aware of the present

141

moment, and are rated using a 6-point Likert-type scale with response options ranging from

142

“almost always” to “almost never.” Examples of items, which are all reverse-coded, include, “I

143

could be experiencing some emotion and not be conscious of it until sometime later,” and “I rush

144

through activities without being really attentive to them.” Studies have reported that the MAAS

145

has strong psychometric properties; in its validation sample, the authors reported good internal

146

consistency as measured by Cronbach’s alpha (α = .82) as well as high levels of convergent and

147

discriminant validity. Similarly, in their study of self-report measures of mindfulness, Baer,

148

Smith, Hopkins, Krietemeyer, and Toney (2006) reported a Cronbach’s alpha value of .86 for

149

this measure.

150

The ASMR Checklist (Fredborg et al., 2017) consists of questions regarding the ASMR

151

participants’ experiences including the intensity of respondents’ responses to 14 triggers that

152

often elicit ASMR as well as the speed of onset of these tingling sensations. Examples of stimuli

153

include “Whispering,” “Watching others apply makeup and/or nail polish to another person,”

154

“Tapping sounds,” and “Dentist simulation.” Individuals were asked to rate each stimulus on a 7-

155

point Likert scale from 0 to 6 (from “no tingles” to the “most intense ASMR experience.”), or

157

Additional questions examine the frequency that respondents view videos that will elicit ASMR,

158

Discussion
The current study strongly supports the hypothesis that ASMR is related to mindfulness.

310

Individuals with ASMR generated higher scores on the MAAS, a measure of the attentional

311

component of mindfulness. They also produced higher scores on the Curiosity subscale of the

312

Toronto Mindfulness Scale, suggesting a greater interest in and openness to their own conscious

313

experiences. This latter result is also consistent with our previous research indicating that



MINDFULNESS AND ASMR
314

individuals with ASMR have higher levels of the openness-to-experience personality trait

315

(Fredborg et al., 2017).

14

316

The results of the Decentering subscale of the TMS were less clear due to differences in

317

reliability between the groups. Although the internal consistency for the control participants was

318

congruent with previous research (Cronbach’s α = .83; Lau et al., 2006), the internal consistency

319

for the ASMR participants was much lower (Cronbach’s α = .60). This difference suggests that

320

individuals in the ASMR group did not interpret the questions on this subscale of the TMS in the

321

same way. Some participants may have interpreted these items in a more general (i.e., trait-like)

322

fashion while others interpreted them as being related to the ASMR ‘tingling’ experience (i.e., a

323

state response). Moreover, exploratory factor analyses of the TMS for ASMR participants

324

revealed a three-factor structure in which the Decentering subscale was split into two factors.

325

Given these mixed results, it is problematic to compare the control group to the ASMR group on

326

this subscale. However, the fact that individuals with ASMR reported higher levels of curiosity

327

as well as increased mindful attention suggests that ASMR may be a cognitively ‘active’ process

328

rather than a more automatic response to stimuli.

329

The current study is not the first to find inter-group differences in the interpretation of

330

items in mindfulness scales. Williams and Dalgleish (2014) found that meditators and

331

nonmeditators differ in their interpretation of some items on the Five-Facet Mindfulness

332

Questionnaire (FFMQ; Baer, Smith, Hopkins, Krietemeyer, & Toney, 2006). These group

333

differences were largest for items relating to the “observe” facet of mindfulness, which focuses

334

on the observation of external and internal stimuli and includes such questions as, “When I’m

335

walking, I deliberately notice the sensations of my body moving.” This component of the FFMQ

336

appears to overlap with the Decentering subscale of the TMS. Given that individuals with



MINDFULNESS AND ASMR

15

337

ASMR are more mindful than controls, the notion that individuals with ASMR may be more

338

mindfully curious, but equally aware of their experiences, as controls is actually consistent with

339

previous research (e.g., Aguado, Luciano, Cebolla, Serrano-Blanco, Soler, & Garcia-Campayo,

340

2015; Williams & Dalgleish, 2014). Further research is necessary to determine whether

341

individuals with ASMR are more or less adept than controls at other facets of mindfulness, such

342

as decentering (e.g., the ability for people to view themselves as separate from their thoughts and

343

feelings).

344

Additional research is also necessary to characterize the sex differences found in our

345

study. Both male and female ASMR participants scored higher than controls on the MAAS, a

346

measure of the attentional component of ASMR. On the Curiosity subscale of the TMS,

347

however, females with ASMR generated higher scores than males with ASMR. These data

348

suggest that males and females may experience ASMR in different ways, with males using

349

attentional mechanisms to initiate ASMR tingles and females using a more cognitive process to

350

initiate these responses. Future studies combining self-report measures with

351

psychophysiological and/or neuroimaging methods would help elucidate these potential sex

352

differences.

353

The current study also addressed the relationship between ASMR intensity, specific

354

ASMR triggers, and mindfulness. The overall ASMR intensity score, MTotal, was indeed

355

correlated with the Curiosity subscale of the TMS. This result suggests that individuals who are

356

curious about stimuli will experience ASMR more intensely than less-curious individuals. This,

357

again, suggests that ASMR is a cognitively active process. The pattern of ASMR triggers also

358

suggests that perceptual curiosity is a factor in ASMR experiences. The “Touching” and

359

“Repetitive Sounds” triggers significantly correlated with Curiosity. It is possible that these

create a mental structure of the perceived stimuli. Individuals who are proficient at doing so—or

363

who have the motivation or curiosity to do so—would be more likely to experience tingles

364

associated with these stimuli.

365

The current results raise an interesting question: Given that mindfulness is associated

366

with increased ASMR intensity, would mindfulness training lead to enhanced ASMR

367

experiences? The current data suggest that it would, although additional research is necessary to

368

test this hypothesis. If such a study produced positive results, it would present individuals with

369

ASMR with some interesting possibilities. Many individuals with ASMR enjoy ‘tingles’

370

hedonically or as a form of relaxation, whereas others use ASMR to help reduce the intensity of

371

some of the symptoms associated with anxiety and depression (Barratt & Davis, 2015). If

372

mindfulness training were to enhance the subjective effects of ASMR, it is possible that this

373

training might enhance any potential benefits that ASMR has on subjective well-being.

374

However, these possible benefits of ASMR are, at present, speculative. To date, the positive

375

impact of ASMR on mental and physical health has only been discussed anecdotally and in self-

376

report survey form. Future research investigating the clinical utility of ASMR, alone and in

377

conjunction with other therapies, would help address this intriguing possibility.

378

Conclusions

379

In conclusion, the results from the Curiosity subscale of the TMS, as well as the MAAS,

380

suggest that the experience of ASMR and mindfulness appear to be related constructs. The

381

current data also suggest that females with ASMR have a higher Curiosity score than males with

382

ASMR, suggesting that the attentional and/or cognitive underpinnings of ASMR may differ

383

between the sexes. Future studies are necessary to thoroughly characterize these mechanisms.

384

Additional research is also necessary to determine whether ASMR could be used to enhance

385

well-being in a manner similar to many mindfulness-based treatment programs.
