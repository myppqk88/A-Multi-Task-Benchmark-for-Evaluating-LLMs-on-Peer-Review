When the literature\nalludes to rational choice, it considers it as a cognitive faculty similar to memory, intelligence, linguistic ability,\nmathematical skill, and so on. As such, rationality is the subject of natural selection akin to these cognitive faculties.\nEven some economists, whose discipline canonizes rationality as the central pillar in explaining choice, concur and treat\nrationality as a cognitive faculty and, hence, the product of natural selection (e.g., Robson, 2001; Robson & Samuelson,\n2010).\nThis paper questions this literature. It is rather, similar to natural\nselection, an optimization method:\nCore Thesis: Rationality radically differs from cognitive abilities such as memory, intelligence, language faculty,\nmathematical skill, and so on. Rationality is rather an optimization method of determining the best choice or fit\ntrait.\nThis core thesis stands irrespective of the many detractors and critics of rational choice theory in the social and behavioral\nsciences.1\nThis paper advances its thesis via a distinction borrowed from set theory (Rai et al., 2012). In contrast,\nany cognitive faculty acts as a binary operator, i.e., as a transformational function. Such rules are the operational function of faculties such as memory, intelligence, and\nsupposedly rationality. In this picture, the organism follows rules in the final analysis as an automaton.\nThis paper ignores sophisticated versions of the The Organismus automaton hypothesis (e.g., Godfrey-Smith,\n1996, 2009). Behavior may involve\nimagination, creativity, aspiration, or what economists recognize as “entrepreneurship” that goes beyond mechanical rules of optimization\n(see Khalil, 1997, 2010). TFk is merely the\ntechnical listing of outcomes that the TFk can produce from the given set of inputs.\nThe symmetry of the two hypotheses\nThe structure of the Organismus automaton hypothesis is almost identical to the structure of Organismus oeconomicus\nhypothesis. The input in the former is the population of individuals where each individual is characterized by an input set,\nwhereas each gives rise to a different end, called a phenotype. The input in the latter is the bundle of goods, whereas each\ngives origin to a different end, called utility.\nThe Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis, based on standard rational choice, also consists of the binary relator assuring\nus the production of the optimum, i.e., the employment of OM, and the binary operator that expresses the transformational\nfunction, say, at k-level, i.e., the employment of TFk. Again here, the binary relator (OM) has two roles. As for the first\nrole, the theory of rationality assures us the ends, that is, the utility values, are consistent and, correspondingly, that the\ninputs are consistently ranked. Consistency depends on many axioms, the most important are two: transitivity and\ncompleteness (see Gilboa, 2012; Khalil, 2023). As for the second role, once constraints are determined, the binary relator\nidentifies the optimum end, that is, the optimum utility, and its corresponding set of inputs.\nAs for the binary operator at k-level (TFk), there is a set of technologies, institutions, and beliefs transforming each set of\ninputs into output. As in the case of the Organismus automaton hypothesis, the binary operator merely lists the technical\naspect of what is the outcome of each set of inputs if the given TFk is employed in their transformation. It neither assures\nany internal consistency nor determines the optimum outcome.\nWhen the optimum is disturbed\nOnce an optimum is selected by nature or by choice, the optimum is stable as long as shocks disturb neither the set of\ninputs nor the set of environmental constraints. The stable decision equilibrium, not to be confused with market\n\n\nequilibrium, can be upset if at least one of the following two conditions changes: (i) a mutation of traits-as-inputs (t) or\ngoods (x) takes place; or (ii) a change in the set of environmental constraints.\nThe set of environmental constraints is implicit in the above schemata. Such a limited optimization function should not be taken, à la “soft” neoDarwinians or à la critics of standard economists (see Khalil, 2013), as a shortcoming that undermines or repudiates the\nnotion of optimization per se. To wit, the raison d'être of optimization is the existence of limits taken as given. So, the\nterm “limited optimization” is pleonasm.\nThere are other nuances, which this paper ignores, of the common structure that underpins the Organismus automaton\nand Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis. What matters for this paper, the structure of either hypothesis is the same\nand can handle different kinds of complications and qualifications at secondary and tertiary approximations. What is most\nimportant in this proposed structure is that the trait-as-technology, i.e., the binary operator, functions as a transformation\nfunction that varies according to the level of the hierarchy. In contrast, the binary relator (OM) remains constant,\ni.e., irrespective of the level of hierarchy.\nThe conflation of the binary operator and the binary relator sheds another insight regarding the controversy between\n“soft” and “hard” neo-Darwinians--as well as between “soft” and “hard” neoclassical economists. This cannot be detailed\nhere. What concerns this paper is the seperation of rationality or any OM from the object of optimization. As shown next,\nsuch separation has one important ramification, rationality cannot be a trait.\nCould rationality ever be a trait?\nThe incoherence problem\nThe proposed distinction between OM, which acts as binary relator, and transformational function (TFk), which functions\nas binary operator, entails the incoherence of what can be called “Rationality-qua-Trait Thesis”.\nIt should be immediately clear that OM (the binary relator) can be either the standard rational choice or, equivalently, the\nneo-Darwinian natural selection. In contrast, TFk (the binary operator) is the given technology or cognitive faculty, such\nas intelligence, memory, and other so on. OM cannot be TFk, as OM is not about transforming inputs to output in the\ntechnical sense.\nMore importantly, rational choice or OM cannot be trait-as-input, that is, an element of the left-hand inputs set. OM\n(binary relator) cannot be a member of the set—when its function is about making sure that the elements are consistently\nranked. To treat OM as a member of itself leads to self-contradiction, i.e., incoherence.\nAs shown above, TFk—such as intelligence, memory, or other cognitive faculties—can be an element of the left-hand\ninputs set—but then the transformation is taking place at a deeper level. There is a hierarchy of technology or body-plan.\nThe transformation function (TF) cannot apply to OM, as OM cannot become a member of the left-hand inputs set. There\nis no hierarchy of rationality as the case with TF. What applies to TF cannot apply to OM. And if we conceive OM as a\nmember of the inputs set, this leads to incoherence à la Russell Paradox.\nThe Russell Paradox\nThe Russell Paradox (Russell, 1956) emerges when one conceives a set as a member of itself. To treat rational choice as a\nmember of the set that is the object of rational choice is an example of the Russell Paradox.\nOne helpful illustration of the Russell Paradox is the well-known Cretan liar paradox and, better, the Barber Paradox\n(Irvine & Deutsch, 2021). The only barber in a village has a sign posted in his shop that states, “I cut the hair of everyone in\nthe village that does not cut his own hair.” But who cuts the hair of the barber? If he cuts his own hair, then the barber\ncannot cut it. And if he does not cut his own hair, then the barber cuts it. Such a self-contradiction arises from treating the\nset, which is the barber’s statement, as a member of itself. Such treatment entails that the barber is an element of the set of\npeople who do not cut their own hair, on one hand, and the barber is the set itself as the one who cuts the hair of such\npeople, on the other.\n\n\n\nThe Russell Paradox arises from what philosophers recognize as the problem of self-reference. In the case of the\nRationality-qua-Trait Thesis, it treats rationality both as OM as well as an element of the set that is the subject of OM. This\nself-reference is best illustrated by some of the drawings of M.C. Escher (see Hofstadter, 1980). The first role of the binary\nrelator (OM) is to make sure that the output is consistently ranked and, corollary, the corresponding inputs are also\nconsistently ranked. Such an organization of inputs cannot also include OM: how could the OM calls for the substitution\nof one input in favor of another when the OM itself is one such input?\nWho’s afraid of the Organismus Oeconomicus hypothesis?\nThe implication of the above analysis is that rationality cannot be selected or chosen. Rationality cannot be a trait such as\nmusical skill, intelligence, or any cognitive faculty. As an OM, it is analytically akin to natural selection.\nIt is obvious that some species, such as Homo sapiens, behave rationally. A difficulty arises when a researcher draws a line\nbetween such species and supposedly species deemed to be non-rational. Such a line, most obviously, leads to the creation\nof a fictitious dichotomy that juxtaposes “rational species” and “non-rational species.” This dichotomy is fictitious as it\nsupposes a natural set that includes species such as bacteria and, say, lower primates as having a common trait that sets\nthem apart from another set that includes only upper primates. To avoid such fictious sets, researchers need to examine\nwhether rationality is a trait in the first place. The Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis certainly avoids such pittfalls, as it\nascribes rationality to all living entities (Khalil, 2023).\nHowever, if we treat rationality as OM, and hence, cannot be seen as a trait, this gives rise to questions about the status of\nrationality with respect to living entities, if not to questions that conjure the specter of metaphysics—the specter of\nconceiving rationality as existing separately from living entities.\nBiologists need not fear the Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis for at least five reasons.\n1. The rationality concept, at least as used by economists (e.g., Becker, 1978; Gilboa, 2012; Khalil, 2023), amounts\nto a rule or a method that does not specify any particular content—not to mention free will, imagination, creative\naction, or ambition. Rational choice means organisms generally make the best decision possible given the\nenvironmental constraints and their technological capacities that include cognitive faculties, physiological\ntraits, technologies, and institutions.\n2. Biologists and psychologists usually restrict rationality to mental or cognitive processes (e.g., Chater et al.,\n2018; Felin et al., 2017). They conclude that organisms with limited cognitive processes, and especially plants\nand other brainless organisms, cannot be rational. However, once one understands rationality properly, one\ncannot conflate it with cognitive faculties (see Khalil, 2010).\n3. Biologists (e.g., Gardner, 2009) are afraid of what philosophers call “reification”—that is, the thinking of an\nidea such as rationality and supposing it is true, i.e., choices made by organisms express rational choice. This\nraises the specter of metaphysics (see Winn, 1939)—the belief in an “uncaused cause” (Dennett, 1995). For\ninstance, the supposed reification of rationality may conjure up uncaused cause and, hence, uphold creationism,\nthat is, the idea there must be an external agent (e.g., a god) who created rationality. However, the specter of\nreification need not arise if one can trace rationality to a defining feature of being a living being. But this task is\noutside the scope of this paper.\n4. Biologists who use the foraging theory pioneered by Eric Charnov (e.g., 1976; see Stephens & Krebs, 1986) or\nwho employ the tools of “bioeconomics” (e.g., Ghiselin, 1974; see also Tullock, 1971, 1994; Khalil &\nMarciano, 2010) already use cost-benefit calculations, that is, rational choice. A few biologists have recently\ntaken the first step to recognizing openly the relevance of rationality to the understanding of behavior or\norganisms (e.g., Vermeij, 2004; Hurley & Nudds, 2006).\n5. Biologists implicitly employ the rational choice approach when they explain behavior as elastic, that is, as\nresponsive to changes in environmental constraints. They call such elasticity “phenotypic plasticity” (Gould,\n1997, 2002; Stanley, 1979; Matsuda, 1987; West-Eberhard, 1989; Raff, 1996; Müller & Newman, 2003; Hall\net al., 2004; DeWitt & Scheiner, 2004). They are making great strides in showing how even invertebrate\norganisms make decisions in light of uncertainty (e.g., Oberhauser & Czaczkes, 2018). They are documenting\nhow animals generally make decisions in reaction to the so-called “contrast effect” (e.g., McNamara et al.,\n2013).\n\n\n\nRegarding phenotypic plasticity, biologists need to recognize explicitly and openly the fact that it amounts to rational\nchoice. As shown elsewhere (Khalil, 2009, 2010), rational choice theory is simply the proposition that the organism’s\nchoice changes in light of changes in environmental constraints or, in short, incentives. With each choice, the organism\nmaximizes the benefit (fitness) given the cost or, what is the same thing, minimizes the cost given the benefit.\nConclusions\n1. There is an incongruent gap in the natural sciences. On one hand, the sciences that focus on the study of nonhuman organisms do not largely give weight to rationality. On the other hand, the sciences, especially\neconomics, that focus on the study of human organisms pay great attention to rationality.\n2. The sciences that focus on non-human organisms do not need to appeal to rationality, as they largely appeal to\nnatural selection as the optimization method. Still, they have to explain rationality if they are natural sciences,\ni.e., sciences that cannot exclude humans from nature. Such sciences, if pushed, tend to explain rationality as a\ntrait—i.e., similar to intelligence and other cognitive faculties. However, such explanation leads to the selfreference problem, a variety of incoherence à la Russell paradox: how could rationality that is an optimization\nmethod be a trait, i.e., the subject of natural selection that is an optimization method?\n3. We can easily avoid the Russell paradox regarding rational choice or any optimization method if we employ a\ndistinction from set theory, namely, the difference between “binary relators” and “binary operators”.\n4. Rationality cannot be a trait given that it is a binary relator that allows us to identify the optimum. In contrast,\ncognitive faculties are traits, as they are binary operators that transform inputs into outputs.\n5. Once we distinguish between binary relators and binary operators, we can conceive rationality as an OM (binary\nrelator) that cannot be a trait (binary operator).
