For professional development, this framework\nsuggests that training should address both dimensions: providing\nnew knowledge and opportunities for practice, while also\nsupporting teachers in reconstructing their identity—including\nbeliefs, confidence, and sense of professional belonging. Thus,\nthe Van Zoest and Bohl model offers a holistic conceptual\nframework for studying the development of mathematics\nteachers by integrating the cognitive and social dimensions of\ntheir growth.\n\nFrom conceptual analysis to model construction\nThe previous sections have examined a range of theoretical\nframeworks related to teacher knowledge and identity in\nmathematics education. While these models offer valuable\ninsights, they tend to treat teacher profile and identity as distinct\ndomains, often resulting in parallel yet disconnected analyses.\nThis separation limits our ability to capture the full complexity\nof teacher development as a dynamic and situated process. To\naddress this, we move beyond the juxtaposition of conceptual\nmodels and propose an integrated framework: the Interdependent\nProfile-Identity (IPD) model.\nTo support the model’s design, a comparative synthesis\nof key frameworks was conducted, focusing on how each\n\nconceptualization addresses three fundamental dimensions:\n(a) the epistemic dimension (knowledge and cognition),\n(b) the relational dimension (self-perception and positioning),\nand (c) the contextual dimension (institutional and sociocultural influences). Table 1 summarizes this synthesis, highlighting\nconvergences and gaps among selected models.\nThis comparative analysis reveals an asymmetry: while some\nmodels (e.g., MTSK, MKT) provide robust accounts of\nprofessional knowledge, they neglect how this knowledge connects to the teacher’s sense of self or role within a community.\nConversely, identity-oriented frameworks (e.g., Wenger; Sfard\n& Prusak) focus on narrative, social, or relational aspects of\nteacher identity but lack an operationalization of professional\nknowledge structures.\nThe design of the IPD model thus emerges from the need to\nreconcile these dimensions. Its architecture is organized into\nthree interrelated subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Knowledge and Beliefs (Epistemic Core): comprising\nboth content-specific knowledge and pedagogical\nreasoning, informed by MTSK and enriched with\nnotions such as professional noticing and reflective judgment.\n•\t\u0007Identity Configuration (Relational Layer): incorporating narrative positioning, perceived agency, and\nemotional self-understanding, based on identity theory\nand discourse-based models.\n•\t\u0007Situated Membership (Contextual Interface): including institutional norms, community participation,\nrecognition processes, and social expectations, grounded\nin practice-based and sociocultural theories.\nThese subdomains are not conceived as separate layers but as\ninteracting spheres that co-construct the teacher’s professional\ndevelopment over time. Their interdependence supports the\nhypothesis that changes in one dimension (e.g., identity\nreconfiguration) will affect the others (e.g., uptake of professional\nknowledge or pedagogical stance).\n\nTable 1. (2024), support the need to adopt an interdependent\napproach to analyzing TP and TI, particularly within\nmathematics teacher education. Their study demonstrates that\nidentity construction processes do not occur in isolation, but\nrather emerge from a complex dialectic among personal,\nprofessional, academic, and disciplinary factors. In this view,\nmathematics teacher professional identity (IPD) is conceived\nas a dynamic construction that integrates family and social\nbackgrounds, academic trajectories, the influence of previous\nteachers, and—critically—the teacher’s relationship with mathematics and mathematics education as fields of knowledge.\nBezerra and colleagues conclude that IPD cannot be analyzed\nas a closed category or merely as a self-image but is deeply\nintertwined with the practices and knowledge that comprise the\nteacher’s profile.\nA framework aiming to recognize IPD interdependence must\nuphold the central thesis that the professional teacher profile\ncannot be defined exclusively by observable competencies or\nfunctional traits but must instead be understood as the outward\nprojection of an identity process in constant evolution. Likewise, teacher identity is shaped and redefined in response to\nthe profile the teacher expects to assume—or is expected (or\npressured) to assume—by the institution. Based on the sources\ndiscussed, an interdependent IPD model can be synthesized\naround the following categories:\n•\t\u0007The distinctive elements of the mathematics teacher’s professional profile, particularly those identified\nwithin the MTSK framework (Carrillo-Yañez et al.,\n2018).\n•\t\u0007The development of teacher identity as a situated\nprocess (Hodgen, 2011).\n•\t\u0007The cognitive aspects, especially those related to\nthe development of professional noticing (Jacobs\net al., 2010).\nThe construction of this model assumes that teacher identity is the product of situated practice. Studies such as those by\nChávez and Llinares (2012) indicate that teachers negotiate their\nidentity through daily classroom participation. These authors\noffer empirical support for the sociocultural claim that identity\nemerges from engagement in social practices—professional\npractice thus acts as both the setting and catalyst for identity\ndevelopment.\nAdditionally, the development of professional noticing is\ninherently interdependent, grounded in cognitive elements and\nconcrete aspects such as reflective capacity and student perception. In this regard, research presented at SEIEM, such as that of\n(Contreras et al., 2011), shows that preservice teachers, when\nreflecting on their past experiences as students and comparing\nthem with their current training, identify tensions and continuities that shape their identity, for example, rejecting traditional\n\nrote methods and aspiring to become more dynamic teachers.\nThese and other contributions add an important nuance to the\nsociocultural approach: while identity is socially constructed,\nself-reflection is the tool through which individuals assimilate and internalize social influences, giving coherence to their\nown professional biography. Consequently, the proposed model\n(see Figure 3) considers a broad set of cross-connections and\ninterdependencies.\n\nStructure of the model\nThe interdependent IPD model proposed here is organized\naround three main pillars: the aspects of the “self-in-mind,”\nthe “self-in-community,” and the mixed elements that mediate\nbetween them. This structure revisits and updates the theoretical\nframework of Van Zoest and Bohl (2005), who conceptualized\nteacher identity as the result of the interaction between\nindividual cognition and community participation. Building on\nthat foundation, categories from the MTSK model are integrated\nto characterize the teacher’s professional profile, and the construct of “professional noticing” is incorporated as a key axis to\nexamine the connection between practice and identity.\nThe professional component (teacher profile) of the model\nemploys the categories from the MTSK framework and its\ndevelopmental philosophy to distinguish two internal domains:\nthe “self-in-mind” and the “self-in-community.” It is composed\nof the following subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Self-efficacy (SE): defined as the teacher’s perception\nof their own ability to teach and their mastery of the\nprofessional knowledge and skills they believe they\npossess. It is thus a judgment about one’s own capacity.\n•\t\u0007Self-improvement (SI): reflects the individual’s commitment to continuous improvement, training and\nlifelong learning. It indicates the extent to which one\nadopts a growth mindset that enables Professional\nAgency.\n•\t\u0007Professional Agency (PA): this subdomain reflects\nthe extent to which the teacher feels responsible and\nautonomous in transforming their own practice. It\nrefers to the will, decision-making, and responsibility\nto act upon oneself (and one’s professional environment).\nWhile the concepts of self-efficacy and self-training are\nwell-established in mathematics education—and are included\nin validated questionnaires for characterizing teacher identity,\nsuch as the MTEBI (Enochs et al., 2000; Segarra & Julià,\n2022)—Professional Agency is defined in the literature\n(Gardee, 2019) as the teacher’s capacity and willingness to\nimplement transformative agency in the classroom. Such\ntransformative practice affects both the students’ mathematical\nidentity and the teacher’s own.\nMeanwhile, the aspects of situated identity are considered\nwithin the “self-in-community” component, which encompasses the social, contextual, and biographical factors that shape\n\n\nFigure 3. IPD framework.\n\nteacher identity. These include participation in educational\ncommunities, the teacher’s self-perception, and how they are\nperceived by others. This dimension recovers the notion of\nidentity as a situated and negotiated process built through\ninteraction (Hodgen, 2011), in which shared practices and\ndiscourses shape not only what the teacher does, but who\nthey are as a professional. It incorporates key elements in identity construction—educational background, school experiences,\nshared values, professional networks—as factors directly influencing identity formation. Thus, the “self-in-community” domain\nrepresents the relational, situated, and socially constructed\ndimension of professional identity. It includes three subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Negotiability of the Common Regime (NCR): refers\nto the teacher’s ability to interpret, question, appropriate, and modify—explicitly or implicitly—the\nnorms, practices, and shared meanings that shape their\nprofessional community.\n•\t\u0007Relational Recognition (RR): refers to the extent to\nwhich the teacher is recognized, valued, and legitimized\nby other members of the professional community\n(peers, students, families, institution) as an active,\ncompetent, and authorized participant. This recognition not only contributes to the sense of belonging but\ndirectly influences identity formation.\n\n•\t\u0007Accountability to an Organization (AO): refers to\nthe way in which the teacher assumes responsibility to\nexternal entities that regulate, assess, or supervise their\nprofessional practice. This subdomain encompasses\nboth formal obligations to comply with curricular\nstandards, evaluation policies, or institutional norms,\nand the ethical and professional disposition to respond\nto these expectations reflectively and critically.\nThe RR subdomain complements NCR and AO by introducing\nthe intersubjective dimension—how identity is also constructed\nexternally. It connects to the emotional component of recognition,\nas feeling valued by others reinforces one’s commitment to the\ncommunity. Moreover, RR has clear practical implications:\na teacher who does not perceive themselves as recognized or\nincluded tends to withdraw, reduce participation, or abandon\nprofessional leadership.\n\nInner dialogue and possibilities\nThe IPD model presents a robust and well-balanced conceptual\narchitecture, filled with internal connections worth highlighting.\nIn this regard, expectations form the central organizing element\nof the model’s “identity” dimension. Teachers’ expectations\nguide their judgments of self-efficacy (SE), their decision to\npursue professional learning (SI), and their willingness to act\n\n\n(PA). Simultaneously, external expectations shape the context\nin which the teacher is held accountable (AO), seeks recognition (RR), and negotiates meanings (NCR). The model thus\nrecognizes how teachers accept, reject, or reinterpret these\nexpectations—and the impact this has on their identity. By placing expectations at the core of the identity dimension—acting as\na sort of “semi-permeable membrane” between the “self-in-mind”\nand the “self-in-community” domains—the model allows for\nthe integration or filtering of social mandates in accordance\nwith the teacher’s values, knowledge, and aspirations.\nHowever, the model’s interconnections extend beyond\nthis, as every potential crossover within the “identity” dimension\ncan be explored. While not all intersections will be discussed\nhere, a few are worth noting:\n•\t\u0007SI–AO: The commitment to improvement may be\ndriven by external demands that the teacher transforms\ninto personal goals. Lifelong learning thus becomes an\nethical response to institutional and social demands for\nprofessional quality.\n•\t\u0007SE–RR: Perceived personal competence is reinforced\nor undermined by the recognition received from\nothers. An environment that validates a teacher’s\nknowledge and actions enhances self-efficacy; one that\ndiscredits them may erode it—even if the teacher has\nsolid training.\n•\t\u0007PA–NCR: Agency is externally manifested in the\nability to influence collective norms. A teacher\nexercising transformative agency tends to actively\nparticipate in the construction and renegotiation of the\nshared repertoire.\nAdditional interconnections can also be explored within\neach domain. In the “self-in-mind” domain, for instance, the\nfollowing relationships can be observed:\n•\t\u0007SE–SI: A strong self-efficacy judgment promotes a\ncommitment to lifelong learning by reinforcing the\nbelief that training efforts will yield results.\nConversely, commitment to professional development\nmay enhance self-efficacy by providing new tools,\nstrategies, and successful experiences.\n•\t\u0007SI–PA: Self-training is a key enabler of agency.\nTeachers who take responsibility for their own growth\nare better positioned to act autonomously, make\ntransformative decisions, and innovate in their practice.\n•\t\u0007SE–PA: Agency is mediated by perceptions of\ncompetence: a teacher who doubts their knowledge\nor skills is unlikely to act agentively. At the same\ntime, acting successfully reinforces self-efficacy in a\nvirtuous cycle.\nBeyond these internal mechanisms, the model offers several\npractical possibilities for analysis. One such use is identifying\nidentity tensions that arise when subdomains from different\n\nareas are misaligned or in conflict. For example, a teacher with\nstrong professional agency (PA) and commitment to learning (SI) may feel frustrated or alienated if their professional\nenvironment limits participation in renegotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) or fails to provide adequate relational\nrecognition (RR). This tension between self-image and contextual\nconditions may lead to identity redefinition, withdrawal, or active\nresistance, depending on how internal and social-professional\nexpectations are managed.\nSecondly, the model enables descriptions of identity development\nprocesses through the progressive strengthening of subdomains.\nFor instance, the trajectory from emerging self-efficacy to\nconsolidated professional agency could be understood as a\npath of identity maturation—one involving not just increased\nperceived competence but also deeper transformative engagement.\nLikewise, a teacher’s gradual integration into the shared\nrepertoire—from passive compliance to active participation—can\nbe seen as a process of professional community appropriation.\nFinally, the model provides a tool for mapping diverse identity\ntrajectories, understood as specific combinations of subdomains\nthat shape distinct identity profiles. For example, a teacher\nwith high relational recognition (RR) but low professional\nagency (PA) may adopt a more conformist identity, while one with\nstrong self-training (SI) and openness to negotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) may embody an innovative, transformative\nidentity. These trajectories are not fixed, but subject to\nchange—reinforcing the idea of identity as a situated and\ncontinuously evolving process.\n\nConclusions\n\nThis paper has explored existing literature with the aim of\ninvestigating and synthesizing the co-determination between the\nteacher profile and teacher identity in mathematics education,\nin order to integrate both within a common framework that\nbetter captures the complexity of teaching practice and promotes\nprofessional growth in a comprehensive manner. In this regard,\nthe paper discusses the transformation that the characterization of the teacher profile has undergone in recent decades:\nfrom early work focused on content knowledge and pedagogy\n(Carrillo-Yañez et al., 2018; Shulman, 1986) to more integrative approaches that simultaneously address cognitive, affective,\ncontextual, and social dimensions (Blömeke et al., 2020;\nLutovac & Kaasila, 2019). These studies are framed within an\ninternational research landscape that has also examined the\nrelationship between teacher profiles and other elements such\nas professional identity (Beijaard et al., 2004), instructional\nquality, assessment (Mandujano et al., 2025), and learning\nopportunities. However, a persistent question in the literature—\nwhich this paper seeks to address—remains: how can cognitive,\nprofessional, identity, and discursive dimensions of the teacher\nbe articulated within a single model?\nDrawing on a review of existing theoretical frameworks, this\npaper proposes a model that seeks to overcome the dichotomy\nbetween professional profile and identity, integrating them\nwithin a unified analytic matrix. This matrix aims to describe\n\n\nmathematics teachers’ training trajectories, understand their\nprofessional decisions and career paths, and most importantly,\ndesign teacher education programs that take into account not\nonly observable aspects of teaching performance, but also the\nsubjective and relational components that shape what it means to\nbe a teacher.\nThis perspective is especially relevant in contexts where\ntensions persist between disciplinary training and pedagogical\npreparation, and where professional recognition of university\nteaching has yet to be systematically linked to reflection on\nidentity. In this sense, the model proposed here is not merely a\ntheoretical framework, but also a tool to rethink teacher education\nprograms and quality assessment systems, aligned with a more\nhumanized, situated, and transformative vision of mathematics\neducation. The framework presented contributes to ongoing\ndiscussions about the complex interrelationship between identity and teacher profile, acknowledging the inherent limitations\nof constructs that are, by nature, constantly evolving. Thus, the\nmodel is intended to be more structural than merely descriptive,\nin order to facilitate its operationalization in training contexts.\nThe model presented in this article responds to a critical gap:\nit proposes a conceptual structure in which teacher profile\nand professional identity are not parallel dimensions, but mutually constitutive. In contrast to existing models that tend to\nanalyze “knowing how to teach” and “being a teacher” separately,\nthe interdependent proposal put forth here builds on the MTSK\nframework and expands it to show how professional knowledge\ninteracts with the construction of identity meanings—and how\nparticular identity configurations influence the appropriation\nand development of such knowledge.\nTaken as a whole, the model not only allows for the analysis of\nteacher identity as a structural construct, but also as a dynamic\n\nfield of possibilities, shaped by tensions, interactions, and shifts\nthat define the professional development of mathematics\nteachers.\nOn the other hand, while the IPD model offers a comprehensive\nframework for understanding teacher development, it is not\nwithout limitations. Its emphasis on reflective practice, identity\nnegotiation, and situated knowledge may render it less applicable in highly prescriptive educational systems where teacher\nautonomy is limited and professional roles are tightly standardized. In such contexts—often characterized by rigid curricula,\ncentralized accountability, or limited opportunities for teacher\nagency—the dynamic interplay between identity and knowledge\nmay be constrained, reducing the explanatory power of the\nmodel. Furthermore, the model assumes access to collaborative\nand dialogic professional environments, which may not be present\nin all institutional settings.\nFinally, it is worth noting that among the many challenges\nidentified, one of the most salient is the coexistence of\nmultiple profiles within the same teaching staff. This difficulty\nalso presents an opportunity to explore the unique potential\nand limitations of each profile (Giadas et al., 2024) and implies\nrecognizing that there is no single model of what it means to be\na teacher, but rather multiple ways of being one, each associated\nwith distinct professional identities.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\n\nData availability\n\nNo data are associated with this article.\n\nReferences\nAguilar-González A, Muñoz-Catalán C, Carrillo-Yáñez J, et al.: ¿Cómo establecer\nrelaciones entre conocimiento especializado y concepciones del\nprofesorado de matemáticas?
