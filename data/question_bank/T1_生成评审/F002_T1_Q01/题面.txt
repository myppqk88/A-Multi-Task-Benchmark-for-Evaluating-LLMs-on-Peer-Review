But a question then arises: how can there be\nany ethics guidance, if ethics is primarily a matter of attention to specific situations? In other words, isn’t this approach\nto ethics as attention rendering any recommendations inadequate as they would take away from the specificities of the\ncontext? Although this essay argues that ethics is primarily a\nmatter of attention to specific situations, guidance is still\nneeded. However, the status of such ethics guidance needs\nto be specified (the guidance formulated in this piece is precisely\nof this nature).\nThe recommendations formulated here do not aim to say what one\nshould do or should not do, but rather aim to promote the conditions of possibility of doing ethics, i.e., of being able to identify\nthe right course of action from within a particular situation.\nIn that sense, the form of ethics that is promoted here does\nnot determine the ‘right’ or the ‘good’ from a distanced\nposition; it is not prescriptive or imperative. Rather, it aims to\nprovide tools and resources to ensure actors, in their situated\nposition – such as a developer in an organisation, within her\nown role, position, organisation, socio-political-environmental\ncontext, and confronted with a particular engineering challenge – can make ethical choices. Rather than determining\nfrom a distanced position what the ethical thing to do is, it\nshould be ensured actors are in a position to determine what\nis the right thing to do. To use the terminology proposed\nby Canguilhem, it is not a matter of determining norms, i.e.\nthe right thing to do, but of developing, actors’ “normative\ncapacity”, which we can also define as ethical agency43.\nThis approach avoids the risk of ethics guidance being patronising. This is one issue that was raised by a SIENNA\nstakeholder from a big technology company: the risk of the\nethicist “coming on high horse” with predetermined ideas of\nwhat engineers should do. As Miller and Coldicutt show, technology workers are sensitive to the impacts of their products:\n“79% agree it’s important to consider potential consequences\nfor people and society when designing new technologies”44.\nThey have called this capacity “personal moral compass”45. For\nAI ethics, it is essential to recognise this already existing sensitivity to issues and ability to respond to ethical challenges.\nHowever, as participants in the SIENNA workshop on multistakeholder strategies for ethical AI highlighted, this is not sufficient. This ethical sensitivity and ability need to be further\nenhanced, promoted and protected. This is precisely the objective\nof the practical recommendations below.
