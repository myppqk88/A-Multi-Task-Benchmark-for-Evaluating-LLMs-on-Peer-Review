The project also trained 200+ community\nmembers and humanitarian staff on inclusive practices, reinforcing the rights to adequate housing and equality for vulnerable\nsurvivors17.\nThese case studies demonstrate that applying human rights\nprinciples in disaster contexts is feasible and beneficial.\n\nApproaches that uphold dignity, non-discrimination, and participation through inclusive planning, targeted protections for\nthe vulnerable, and community engagement have resulted in\nmore just and effective outcomes.\n\n5.2 Key challenges in DSS integration and\nimplementation\nDespite significant advancements, several challenges persist in\nthe development and implementation of DSS for multi-hazard\nDRM. One key issue is data interoperability, where disparate\ndatasets from different agencies and sources often lack standardization. Ensuring seamless integration of diverse data\nstreams remains an ongoing research priority. Another limitation\nis the computational cost of high-resolution simulations, which\ncan limit real-time applicability in resource-constrained settings. DSS frameworks need to evolve to incorporate climate\nadaptation strategies dynamically, given the increasing frequency and intensity of climate-induced disasters. Future\nresearch must explore the use of hybrid AI models that combine deep learning with expert-driven rule-based systems to\nenhance predictive reliability and decision support.\nData integration and interoperability issues are among the\nmost cited barriers in implementing DSS for multi-hazard\nmanagement. A multi-hazard DSS must pull together information from numerous sources, such as meteorological data,\nseismic sensors, social datasets, and infrastructure networks,\nwhich often exist in incompatible formats and siloed systems.\nStudies highlight that many failures in disaster coordination\nstem from different agencies and systems being unable to\nseamlessly share data and models. Without interoperability,\ncrucial information can fall through the cracks, undermining\nthe very purpose of a DSS. Data quality and reliability present\nconcerns, as incoming streams may be incomplete, outdated,\nor incorrect, especially in chaotic disaster conditions. DSS\ndevelopers must grapple with uncertainties and ensure the system can function with imperfect data. There is also the risk\nof information overload: a comprehensive DSS might flood\ndecision-makers with more data and model outputs than they\ncan interpret under time pressure. Machine learning can filter\nout “unrelated data” and flag the truly relevant signals,\nmitigating cognitive overload during crises18.\nAnother set of challenges lies in the ethical and social domain.\nAs AI-driven and data-intensive DSS become commonplace,\nthey raise questions about bias, transparency, and accountability. Algorithms trained on historical data may inadvertently perpetuate biases, such as underestimating risks to\nunder-served communities if those areas were historically\nunder-reported. The often “black box” nature of advanced\nAI models can make it unclear how a DSS arrived at a given\nsuggestion, creating a transparency problem19.\nPractical implementation barriers can impede DSS deployment\neven when technical issues are solved. Resource constraints\nare a common hurdle, as developing and maintaining a sophisticated DSS requires funding, skilled personnel, and infrastructure. Interdisciplinary and inter-agency coordination is another\n\n\npractical challenge, as a multi-hazard DSS often requires collaboration between meteorologists, engineers, planners, first\nresponders, government officials, and communities. Developers must address human-factor issues like interface design,\ntraining programs, and change management to make DSS\nwork in practice.\n\n5.3 Emerging trends and innovations in DRM Decision\nSupport Systems\nTechnological innovations, such as artificial intelligence\n(AI) and blockchain, have significantly accelerated the capabilities of DSS for DRM, making them indispensable for\nreal-time data processing, risk modeling, and scenario-based\ndecision-making. However, the efficacy of these tools depends\non their ethical and legal foundations, such as data privacy, algorithmic fairness, and stakeholder inclusion. An\n“ethics-by-design” approach to DRM involves embedding\nethical considerations into the system development lifecycle,\nincorporating safeguards, and addressing legal requirements\nlike GDPR. This approach maintains public trust and ensures\ntechnology-driven disaster management does not compromise\nhuman rights.\nAI is being harnessed to improve risk assessment and early\nwarning, with AI-assisted emergency response plans being\ndeveloped by UN-led initiatives. AI is being used to quickly\nanalyze multi-hazard data while maintaining transparency\nand bias mitigation. Pilot applications include predictive analytics for flood forecasting, computer vision for damage\nassessment via satellite imagery, and optimization algorithms\nfor relief logistics. These tools can greatly improve the speed\nand accuracy of decisions, but human oversight and fairness\ncriteria must guide their deployment20.\nBlockchain technology is being used to enhance trust and data\nintegrity in DRM. It provides a decentralized, tamper-proof\nledger for critical data like resource inventories, aid distribution, and identity records. Studies during the COVID-19 era\nsuggest that blockchain can build trust, transparency, and\naccountability in complex disaster operations. Projects have\nused blockchain to track aid funds and maintain secure medical\ndata exchanges during disasters21,22.\nGeospatial technology and open data are enabling more\ninclusive, bottom-up risk mapping through tools like\nOpenStreetMap, mobile data collection apps, and participatory GIS platforms. These tools allow local communities to\ncontribute directly to mapping their environment and hazards,\nimproving data availability for early warning systems and capturing community knowledge in risk models. This approach\nfosters inclusivity, transparency, and public engagement, leading\nto more effective local risk reduction measures.\nComprehensive DRM platforms that integrate multiple hazard\nmodels, big data analytics, and user-friendly interfaces for\ndecision-makers are emerging trends that promote transparency\nand inclusive governance. As these innovations mature, they\nare likely to become integral to how we approach multi-hazard\nrisk management in an inclusive manner.\n\n6. Recommendations and future directions\n\nTo truly be people-centered, MEDiate’s DSS should facilitate\nmeaningful stakeholder engagement not only in its development but also in its deployment. Building on the PAR approach\nalready used in testbeds, MEDiate can set up formal mechanisms for participatory governance. One approach is to\nestablish local stakeholder committees in each pilot region\n(including community representatives, NGOs, and civic leaders) who regularly interact with the DSS team. These committees could co-design scenario assumptions, validate data\ninputs, and discuss the DSS’s recommendations, ensuring local\nknowledge and values are incorporated. Establishing regular participatory workshops for scenario validation involving\nlocal stakeholders and emergency managers and organizing\nmandatory comprehensive training in GDPR compliance, data\nprotection practices, and ethical principles for all DSS users\nand administrators would be beneficial for technology adoption. As noted in disaster ethics research, involving community\nstakeholders leads to more fair, culturally appropriate decisions.\nFuture research extending beyond the scope of MEDiate\nshould focus on implementing standardized data-sharing protocols and interoperability guidelines across DRM platforms.\nAdditionally, ethical considerations, such as bias in predictive\nmodels and privacy concerns in data collection, require\nfurther investigation. Research could be focused on AI-driven\nDSS to improve computational efficiency, enhance real-time\ndata integration, and increase explainability of decision-making\nalgorithms.\nThe integration of blockchain technology for secure, decentralized data sharing is another promising avenue that could\naddress trust and data integrity challenges possibly leading to\ntamper-proof data exchanges and enhancing trust in DRM\npractices. AI for risk assessment tools may be useful but can\ninadvertently reproduce societal biases in training data, leading to unfair outcomes. This raises equity issues in disaster\nresponse. To address this, ethical DRM DSS require bias mitigation measures, such as auditing algorithms for biased patterns, using diverse datasets, and applying fairness metrics.\nOngoing monitoring and transparent validation are recommended to detect and correct any unfair impacts. This might\ninvolve algorithmic impact assessments or incorporating ethical\nAI frameworks that uphold principles of justice and respect\nfor human rights. Ensuring explainability of AI decisions is crucial for stakeholder understanding and challenge23. Moreover,\nexplainable AI (xAI) technology can generate synthetic data\nto enhance predictive models, aiding in disaster risk identification. However, ethical concerns arise regarding the accuracy\nof synthetic data in models and concerns about data privacy and\nsecurity. The use of synthetic data in predictive models may\nnot accurately reflect real-world conditions, and its application\nin disaster response raises questions about data security24.\nEnsuring long-term ethical adherence and privacy protection\ncould be accomplished with data anonymization techniques\nincluding aggregation and pseudonymization of sensitive\ninformation and algorithm transparency checks through regular fairness audits, impact assessments, and xAI integration.\nOrganizational mechanisms, such as appointing dedicated Data\n\n\nProtection Officers (DPOs) and Ethics Advisory Boards, overseeing compliance and ethical practices should be imperative\nfor widespread MEDiate DSS adoption.\n\nstakeholder feedback.
