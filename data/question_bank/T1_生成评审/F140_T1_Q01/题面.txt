    "manuscript_text": "Abstract\nSufficiency measures are potentially decisive for the decarbonisation\nof energy systems but rarely considered in energy policy and\nmodelling. Just as efficiency and renewable energies, the diffusion of\ndemand-side solutions to climate change also relies on policy-making.\nOur extensive literature review of European and national sufficiency\npolicies fills a gap in existing databases. We present almost 300 policy\ninstruments clustered into relevant categories and publish them as\n\"Energy Sufficiency Policy Database\". This paper provides a\ndescription of the data clustering, the set-up of the database and an\nanalysis of the policy instruments. A key insight is that sufficiency\npolicy includes much more than bans of products or information tools\nleaving the responsibility to individuals. It is a comprehensive\ninstrument mix of all policy types, not only enabling sufficiency action,\nbut also reducing currently existing barriers. A policy database can\nserve as a good starting point for policy recommendations and\nmodelling, further research is needed on barriers and demandreduction potentials of sufficiency policy instruments.\n\n\nIntroduction\nSufficiency is a potentially low-cost, fast and socially just mean of global greenhouse gas (GHG) mitigation. It is linked to\nmultiple benefits for human well-being.1 The understanding of sufficiency varies across disciplines and discursive\nspheres; in relation to energy, sufficiency is the strategy that aims at achieving absolute reductions in the use of energybased services.2 Energy sufficiency, renewable energies and energy efficiency are all methods to reduce emissions\nassociated with energy use. Renewables substitute fossil fuels, efficiency means finding ways to reduce losses by\nimproving the input/output relation, and sufficiency equals avoiding/shifting energy services altogether.\nJust as renewable energy and efficiency technologies, energy sufficiency is enabled and promoted by policy action. It is a\ngenuine field of policy making, a case for regulatory frameworks and infrastructures, much beyond micro-level\nindividual behaviour changes.1,3–6 We differentiate three sufficiency types to categorise policy instruments by whether\nthey are aimed at a clear avoid strategy, i.e. a reduction of services; at shift, i.e. substituting energy intensive practices by\nless energy-intensive ones; or in general supporting reductions and substitutions.[1]\nInformation gap: a sufficiency policy database\nThe need for a sufficiency policy database has been formulated by various researchers and ministerial representatives,\ne.g. Toulouse et al.7 and Zell-Ziegler and Förster.8 Sufficiency is underrepresented in climate change mitigation policy\nand energy and climate scenarios so far (e.g. Samadi et al.9). A database on energy sufficiency could serve as a starting\npoint for the collection of policy instruments, their assessment in a structured format, as precondition for further research\nand policy consulting and to complement the discussion on mitigation policy options.\nSuch database should provide information on good practices, implementation examples and insights on emission and\nenergy reduction potentials. An openly accessible database can collect findings, ideas and examples and make them better\naccessible to policy makers than’hidden’ in e.g. a project report.\nWhen consulting existing climate end energy policy databases, for example International Energy Agency’s (IEA)\nPolicy Database10 and European Environment Agency’s (EEA) Database on Greenhouse Gas Policies and Measures\nin Europe,11 it is striking that demand-side solutions to climate change and especially sufficiency options are underrepresented. Many sufficiency-minded bottom-up initiatives are included in the database on Transformative Social\nInnovations12 hosted by Dutch Research Institute For Transition (DRIFT), but this database does not focus on policy\ninstruments. The NewClimate Institute’s Climate Policy Database13 and the Mesures d’Utilisation Rationnelle\nde l’Energie (MURE) database on energy efficiency measures14 include sufficiency and/or the reduction of energy\nservice demand in their search masks. However, the scope of these databases is different to our purpose: the Database of\nNewClimate aims at tracking climate action worldwide and thus focuses on mitigation plans and decarbonisation\nroadmaps rather than on specific policy instruments. The MURE database explicitly considers sufficiency policies and\nindicators since spring 2021,15 including detailed descriptions and quantification, but only for policies that are already\nimplemented, which limits the openness for new proposals. Another database is the Policy Ideas Database for Sustainable\nProsperity from the Institute for Future-Fit Economies (ZOE).16 The ZOE-database is not a classical database, but a\nnarrative and graphical visualisation, however, it is a valuable source for visionary cross-sectoral policies.\nMethods\nThis section briefly describes the structure of the database, starting with sources for entries. To improve accessibility, and\nto allow for filtering, clustering and potential further analyses, we propose a number of structuring categorisations\nincluding information on sectors, goals, target indicators, policy instruments and sufficiency types. Moreover, we outline\nour internal review and harmonisation process and the technical setup of the database.\nData sources\nPolicy instruments are mainly extracted from existing literature and databases using the classifying parameters outlined\nbelow. Two key sources are first an extensive and detailed report describing policy measures for a “German Zero”\nemissions scenario until 203517 and second a list of policy measures extracted from all European National Energy and\nClimate Plans (NECPs) published by Zell-Ziegler et al.2 and condensed to merge instruments described by various\nNECPs. This is complemented by databases such as the Sustainable Prosperity Database16 and by further literature18–30 as\nwell as exchange with experts. structuring the data\nTo allow for the analysis of policy instruments, we structured the database along several key categories:\nSector includes information on the covered sector: agri-food, buildings, industry/production, transport, LULUCF\n(land use, land-use change and forestry), energy and cross-sectoral (for overarching policies targeting various sectors).\nGoal/policy strategy describes what the respective policy aims at, while\nMeasure/action explains the more concrete changes envisaged to achieve the policy goals and the mitigation target.\nIndicator lists quantifiable units to be used to estimate the effect of the policy instrument/policy.\nSufficiency type categorises instruments according to the strategies avoid, shift or generally supporting.\nPolicy instrument lists the specific policy instrument from the respective source that is intended to lead to change and the\ndescription gives more details on the specifications of the instrument.\nInstrument type categorises the policy instrument types according to UNFCCC categories,32 adding one for \"not\nspecified\", where actions are listed in sources but respective instruments are not system,34,35 policy interactions, and\nwhether they constitute a sufficiency promotion or reduction of barriers.\nInternal review\nIn order to increase consistency and interrater reliability of the database and to avoid duplicate entries, the data collection\nand cleaning process was organised with multiple internal review loops:\n• Assignation of main sector responsible to each sector (out of the four first authors)\n• Initial policy collection by main sector responsible\n• Assignation of reviewer to each sector (out of the four first authors), review of all entries including: clarity of\nentries, correct coding, plausible entries (esp. goal/policy strategy, measure/action, indicator, instrument type)\n• Bilateral coder meetings for clearance of divergences where necessary\n• Loops of coder-meetings to sort out inconsistencies and establish a common understanding and coding\nprocedure\n• Where multiple policy instruments were listed, prioritisation on main instrument or splitting of policy in\nrespective separate instruments (rows)\n• Harmonisation of goals and indicators within and across sectors\nBuild-up of database\nThe database needs to meet the following requirements in order to be of highest utility and used most efficiently:\n• Online availability with open access\n• Clear design and easy navigation through the dataset\n• Possibility to filter and search for keywords\n\n\n• Possibility to insert additional information on policies (description as floating text)\n• Possibility to include attachments (e.g. sufficiency policy impact chains, planned to be developed)\n• Possibility to download the data\nFurthermore, the possibility to propose new policies and an integrated feature for a review process is desirable.\nIn order to find a software solution that may deliver on these aspects we exchanged with the hosts of comparable policy\ndatabases and with persons from our networks and a person from the EnSu project’s advisory board[2].\nResults\nThe database version published with this article contains 281 policies from seven sectors (Figure 1 shows freight transport\nseparately). One third of these are from the transport sector, followed by 62 policies in the building sector and 48 crosssectoral measures that address more than one sector. Some provide indirect connections with sufficiency measures such\nas reductions of subsidies for fossil fuels. The sources we analysed contained only very few sufficiency measures for the\nLULUCF (4) and energy (6) sector. The sufficiency measures in the energy sector concentrate on the overall policy goal\n‘reduce energy consumption’ with such instruments as e.g. ‘subsidise energy savings’.28\nWe include modal shift measures in the transport sector, labeled as sufficiency type shifting from high-energy to lowerenergy modes (shift). Of the 95 transport entries, 56% aim at mode shift from cars to public transport and active modes\n(walking, cycling) (see Figure 2). In the transport sector, shift policies dominate, indicating that this is a key policy\nstrategy for transport. Somewhat less common is the reduction of necessary trips and thus traffic (avoid), e. g. through\nsupport of teleworking or city planning to reduce distances between points of interest. Shift policies also dominate in the\nindustry and agri-food domain.\nIn the building sector, sufficiency instruments of the ‘avoid’ type dominate in our database so far, aiming at an efficient\nuse of living space and development of the existing building stock, focusing on quality of living and by that reduce overall\nliving space and required land sealing. In the industry/production sector, avoid measures target at enhancing product\nlifetimes by increasing reparability and durability.\nThe database includes existing and proposed policy instruments of all types as categorised by UNFCCC.32 Most\nof the policy instruments in the database are of regulatory nature (108), economic (45, e.g. taxation) or fiscal (66, e.g.\ninfrastructural or subsidies) (see Figure 3). The high share of regulatory instruments is in contrast to sufficiency policy\ninstruments that are implemented or planned by EU member states, as listed in NECPs2 where regulatory instruments are\nunderrepresented. We consider it\nstill helpful to depict each policy instrument in detail in such a database on the one and provide the heterogeneity of\nsufficiency policy on the other side.\nThe database published along with this article is a first version and proof of concept. So far, we have a strong focus on\nEuropean and German sources. We do not claim completeness and since it is a dynamic field, additional policy\ninstruments will be added. We will also invite external persons to propose additional policies and plan to team up with\nother research stakeholders in the energy sufficiency field at German and EU level to investigate options for a successful\ncontinuation of the database including potential future (co-)hosts and possibilities for maintenance. Regarding its format,\nwe plan to implement the sufficiency policy database as an online database.\nFurther planned developments of the database are the inclusion of quantitative effects (energy demand & GHG-reduction\npotentials) of the policies. To this end, policy impact chains need to be investigated and the policy representation in\nmodels be expanded to include them in ex-ante assessments and scenario studies.37 Another possible addition is a\nprioritization regarding maturity, replicability and impact.38 To make more transparent which policies are actually\nreducing barriers for sufficiency, we will add the categorisation of barrier-reduction and sufficiency-supporting.\nFurthermore, we will add good practice and implementation examples to the entries of the database.\nIn the field of barriers, more research is required to identify current political barriers which might prevent the\nimplementation of sufficiency. Due to the partly fundamental character of the changes some policy instruments of\nsufficiency represent, further research on economic effects, macroeconomic dynamics and culture is required which is\nbeyond the scope of the database itself but is fundamental for a successful implementation of sufficiency policy.\nConclusion\nOur energy sufficiency policy database of currently 281 policy instruments can serve as a basis to develop policy\nrecommendations in the so far underrepresented field of sufficiency policy. Moreover, it provides a starting point for\nfurther research on sufficiency and climate change mitigation policies, especially for modelling and scenario studies. The\nidentification of indicators is a first step to include sufficiency into modelling and be able to assess the effect of the\ndifferent policy measures.\nAs the variety of different instrument types, goals and policy levels shows, sufficiency is a diverse policy field with\nmultiple options of implementation and effect. The database encompasses specific instruments but also fundamental\nstructural changes of societal organisation principles. Our interdisciplinary approach is helpful to cover the heterogeneity\nof sufficiency. The description on concrete information on sufficiency as policy, with goals, instruments and indicators as\n\n\nan interface to modelling can help to overcome existing barriers on sufficiency policy implementation.
