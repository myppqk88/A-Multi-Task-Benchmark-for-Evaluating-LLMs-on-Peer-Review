The main contribution of assemblage is analysing\nsocio-spatial phenomena and understanding them relationally\nas dynamic wholes, in constant flux, or becoming. We would like to understand the type of transformation\nthe CWS’s enact is enabling ‘intentional economies’ in the\nwords of Gibson-Graham (2008) or ‘real utopias’ as Wright\nemphasised (2010). Both represent open ended post-capitalist\nrealities, by facilitating pluriversality (Escobar, 2021) or economic diversity (Gibson-Graham, 2008), working towards creating many different worlds, rather than the perceived dominant\ncapitalist world, destabilizing its perceived hegemony.\nRather than providing an exact post-capitalist strategy, the\nCWS provide a “multitude of possibilities of what could come\n\nafter, as well as building daily competences to leverage social\nchange.” (Chatterton, 2016: 405)”. Moving beyond certainty,\nwe aim to provide one potential pathway of many possibilities towards creating such a future. To do so, we would like\nto infuse Wright’s thinking with that of DCE, understanding\nsocial transformation as post-capitalism, and Wright’s three\ntypes of transformation, as three stages of transformation.\nAs highlighted in the literature review section of the paper,\nWright provides three pathways towards social transformation – ruptural, interstitial and symbiotic. Interstitial transformation may be the social transformation most closely linked\nto grassroots SI, referring to the ‘various kinds of processes\nthat occur in the spaces and cracks within some dominant\nsocial structure of power’ (p. 229). Wright gives examples of\ninterstitial transformations such as various forms of co-ops,\nworkers councils, community based social economy organizations etc., which are also common examples of SI. Such\ninitiatives offer viable alternatives to state and capitalist\nways of organizing, however, are often critiqued as operating\nin isolation, whereby they do not offer a real viable alternative, but merely as heterotopias that operate within ‘niche’\nor ‘protective spaces’ (Smith & Raven, 2012). This is what\nwe conceptualize as our first level of transformation, which\nwe can clearly see as the two CWS’s work towards satisfying\ncommunity needs and build community economies, through\ndesiring production and building affective relations.\nBoth spaces act as heterotopias, or syntopias (Vidaillet &\nBousalham, 2020), that allow users to experience other ways\nof being in the world. While both hubs offer this, CUA is\nmore explicit about its interstitial function of the space – “we\nhave, kind of research Lab to try out things outside of the\nnormal economic, a free space where it’s not based on the\nnormal ongoing system status and that was one of the basic\nideas with CUA to create the spaces, to create ideas. Maybe\nfor the future. Maybe it is quite small, but maybe change\nsomething if we need.” (CUA co-founder, interview).\nRegarding symbiotic transformations, Wright differentiates\nthem from interstitial mainly due to their orientation towards\nthe state, which they use as a mediating body to reduce capitalist power, with the main example given as class compromise, where workers and capitalist firms negotiate through\nthe state over workers’ rights, pay etc. However, we would\nlike to offer a different conceptualization of symbiotic transformation, as a second stage of interstitial transformation\ndiffusion, as they influence other spatiotemporal arrangements. This may initially be in antagonism, and lead to\ntheir practices being copied/replicated by state or municipal\norganizations, or it may mean these institutions recognising and supporting interstitial transformations as important\nsocial economy actors. In both cases we also observe this level\nof transformation, with long term institutional collaboration\nof CUA and the municipality and more recently in WGH as\nthey have begun to collaborate with the new municipality\nthrough organizing a public consultation regarding the town’s\ndevelopment. Moreover, both CWS also offer their resources\n\n\n\nand capacities to local actors such as schools, local businesses and other market entities, NGOs, and other local\nassociations, blending DCE economies with the market economy. While interstitial transformation through the DCE\nframework is usually performed inside the CWS, symbiotic\ntransformation happens outside the bounded environment\nof CWS and offers to other entities and the state to experience the alternative socio-economic arrangements, through the\nDCE framework that CWS’s assemblages perform.\nWithin SI literature, SI is often deemed to be transformative\nwhen it pierces this institutional level (Haxeltine et al., 2016;\nMoulaert & MacCallum, 2019), when SI bottom-up initiatives are supported by institutions, it is referred to as\nbottom-linked SI that “develops when citizens’ collective initiatives result in agreements with local institutions that enable\nand sustain such initiatives through sound, regulated and\nlasting practices” (Garcia et al., 2015: 93). This symbiotic\napproach has many merits and is key for SI’s transformative\npotential to move beyond their space in the margins. However, SI being recognized by institutions does not equate to\nthe neoliberal rhetoric by governments over the last twenty\nyears (for example see Fougère et al., 2017 for a full discussion), something that ‘limits social innovation to marginal\nchange’ (Bartels, 2017). Initiatives need to be given resources\nand power rather than just responsibility (Bragaglia, 2021).\nMoreover, through symbiosis it allows us to move beyond\nthe binary of initiatives either rejecting institutions or\nbeing co-opted by them, allowing for real change to be enacted\nand further institutionalized (Bartels, 2017).\nFigure 1 demonstrates how as DCE practices are performed and\npracticed, moving from the edifices and margins as interstitial\n\ntransformations, to piercing the institutional dimension, and\nbeyond we envisage CE practices spreading in a form of contagion\nuntil they reach a saturation point of post-capitalist becoming, signifying not necessarily a straight clean break from capitalism to\npost-capitalism, but a revolution of becoming, as different\naspects of life become recoded and DCE are heavily territorialized and enacted in society. Both cases do not reflect this\nlevel of transformation (yet, at least), but an example of such\na ‘real utopia’ would be the widely documented case of\nMondragon in the Basque country, with the co-operative\nmodel completely institutionalized in the region. While as an\nexample it is highly contextual and non-replicable, it gives\nan example of possibility (Gibson-Graham, 2008). We cannot state this is the case for either case studies, both remain\nstill in their symbiotic stage. The passage to a ruptural transformation requires CWS to be supported by various local and\nextra-local actors to be acknowledged as the only alternative and post-capitalist solution, where DCE can become the\ndominant mode of production and gain a hegemonic place\nin society.\n\nConclusion\n\nThe paper discussed the role of CWS as initiators and facilitators of SI processes in two community led CWS in two\nrural areas in Austria and Greece. The research methodology\nemployed a qualitative case study approach, utilizing participant observation and semi-structured interviews to explore\nthe multiple sets of relations found in these rural CWS, aiming to understand how these arrangements cover local needs\nand foster new social relations. The findings reveal that\nCWS play a significant role in addressing social needs and\nfacilitating grassroots SI practices, creating affective relations\nthat contribute to the transformation of individual to collective subjectivities. CWS seem to provide a site where the\nunmet needs of the local people are negotiated, redefined\nand are covered in a co-creative and collective manner. This\nis happening in a DCE framework, where affective labour\nmostly performs.\nCWS have a significant impact on individual subjectivities\nby transforming them into collective subjectivities through\naffective relations and desiring production, through the\nimplementation of collaborative projects. The CWS play a crucial role in changing social relations and increasing affects\nand capacities towards community interests, which is an essential part of becoming post-capitalist subjects. They provide\nopportunities for actors to affect and be affected, increasing their agency and assisting them with their own desires\nby providing resources such as space, knowledge, materials,\nnetworks, and equipment. This process enables desiring\nproduction and creates non-alienated and significant relationships with the world and with others, which is essential\nfor subject formation and the development of collective\nsubjectivities.\n\nFigure 1. How Diverse and Community Economy practices\ndiffuse throughout the three levels of transformation.\n\nFurthermore, the affective relations formed within CWS’s\nhave a transformative potential, as they create new collaborations and relationships in places where the narrative that\n\n\nnothing is happening. These affective relations also extend to\nthe larger community in a symbiotic manner, creating positive\naffects for the town and fostering collective action and affective labor to create a window into what could be for the\ncommunity. Additionally, the CWS’s influence other larger\nassemblages through the concept of relations of exteriority,\nallowing them to ‘infiltrate’ other more strictly coded and\nhighly territorialized assemblages, such as schools and\nmunicipalities, while maintaining their autonomy.\nFuture research could explore similar community led CWS\nassemblages in other contexts, including outside of Europe, to\nsee what we can learn and how their transformative potential can be institutionalized. Furthermore, both cases were\nmainly led by an individual, who had a significant impact\non the overall assemblage, regarding the resources they\nput into it and their decision making. Their power seems to\nhave a (dis)empowering (Avelino et al., 2019) impact on\nthe overall assemblage, as despite their energy and intensity\nincreasing its affects, it could sometimes be overwhelming\nfor other participants, causing one staff member in the Greek\ncase to leave. Thus, future research could focus on more\nrhizomatic forms of SI initiatives, with less control of one\nindividual, and how it differs to these SI assemblages.\n\nEthics and consent\nConsent\n\nInterview Participants were given an information sheet\nabout the study and were required to sign a consent form\nprior to being interviewed. An ethical approval committee at\nPanteion University approved the study (Protocol Number:\n44/ 30-9-2022). An example of the information sheet and\nconsent form can be found above in the ‘extended data’ section.\nRegarding participatory observation, the researcher made his\nidentity as a researcher known to the users of the CWS by\ncontacting the CWS in advance via email, requesting that\nthey inform relevant stakeholders that there will be a\nresearcher present for the given time period, and to notify them\nshould they not consent to participate in the study to contact the researcher through contact details provided. When\npresent on site, the researcher made his identity as a researcher\nknown to the those participating in the CWS’ activities, and\nverbal consent was required because of the short time duration of their visits. Moreover, although minors took part in\nsome workshops (as stated in section 5.1.), the participants in the workshops were not interviewed or subjects of\nparticipant observation, and therefore it did not require\nconsent.\n\nData availability\n\nDue to ethical restrictions on data sharing should any reader or\nreviewer request data to be made available, small sections of\nde-identified quotes may be available on request by emailing\nthe corresponding author.
