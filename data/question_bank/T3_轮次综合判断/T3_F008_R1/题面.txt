It aims at gaining insights into the ways that\na more balanced approach can be achieved, by taking into\nconsideration the (mutual) interests, barriers and expectations of the\nmunicipality and local citizen initiatives. Additionally, clear mutual communication on short\nand long-term planning and ambitions of the involved parties is of key\nimportance for the alignment of the interests and the course of\nactions. In 2020, there\nwere a total of 623 LEIs in almost every municipality in the\nNetherlands. This includes activities aimed at\nenergy generation, energy conservation, energy supply,\ncollective purchasing or commissioning and other energy-related\nactivities. They focused on ten LEIs spread over\ndifferent municipalities in the Netherlands. Various success\nfactors and barriers that arose during this process have been\nformulated. A large number of these barriers relate to the role\nof the municipality in facilitating the activities of the local\ninitiatives. Examples vary from barriers in legislation and\nregulations to diverging interests between local initiatives and\nthe municipality.\nDespite the growth in the number of LEIs, the impact of these\ncollectives on the national and international climate objectives is still limited (Wiekens & Germes, 2020). LEIs and\ntop-down energy planning procedures rarely align. The\npractical studies that are mentioned within the literature review\non LEIs, show that the role of the municipality in facilitating\nLEIs is often dominant and top-down oriented (Klösters\net al., 2020; van der Windt et al., 2021). However, according to\nSabatier & Mazmanian (2005), a more balanced approach,\ntaking into account the needs, interests, but also the barriers in\nthe relationship between local energy initiatives (LEIs) and\nmunicipalities could accelerate citizen engagement and result\nin more successful local energy transition projects.\nThis study aims at gaining insights into how a more\nbalanced approach of top-down and bottom-up processes\ncan be achieved within the context of energy transition, by\ninvestigating the interests, barriers and expectations of the\nGroningen LEIs.\nThe case study area of this study is the Groningen municipality,\nwhich is located in the North of the Netherlands.\nGroningen is one of the two lighthouse cities participating\nat the Making City Horizon 2020 research program, in the\ncontext of which this study has been conducted. The various\n\ndistricts of Groningen are characterized by different stages of\nenergy transition, as well as by a variety on the ways with\nwhich citizen engagement is approached. In some districts,\nLEIs have been formulated by residents, while in other districts\nthey have been established and facilitated by the municipality. It is interesting to notice that usually the vision and the\ninterests of these LEIs do not exclusively revolve around the\ntheme of energy transition, but are broader. For instance, they\nusually relate to enhancing the overall well-being and the\nquality of life of the local residents, or making the district\nmore sustainable. Then, energy transition usually constitutes\nan important aspect within this broader frame of interests and\nambitions. It aims at gaining a better\nunderstanding of the needs of the local energy initiatives. On this\nbasis, policy recommendations are formulated to help the\nmunicipality of Groningen to facilitate local energy initiatives,\nultimately leading to a more balanced approach of the local energy\ntransition. Thus, this study explored the (mutual) interests, barriers\nand expectations of the municipality and local citizen initiatives. The\ntheoretical framework is the implementation analysis framework,\ndistinguishing top-down and bottom-up approaches. Specifically, this\nqualitative (thematic analysis) research study investigates the\nmismatch in expectations between a number of local energy initiatives\nand the municipality of Groningen regarding their roles within the\nlocal energy transition context. To this end, semi-structured interviews\nhave been conducted with members of the municipality of Groningen,\nGrunneger Power (a local energy intermediary), and four local energy\ninitiatives. Need and expectation gaps have been identified and\npotential solutions have been explored. The main findings of the study\nillustrate the need of professional support for citizen initiatives, at\nboth technical and organizational level, especially in the first phases of\ntheir development. Additionally, clear mutual communication on short\nand long-term planning and ambitions of the involved parties is of key\nimportance for the alignment of the interests and the course of\nactions. Consequently, a clear context is needed, within which an\nexchange of feedback on the envisioned strategies, and the\nsubsequent energy saving or generation interventions, can take place\nin an efficient and effective way. Additionally, such a context increases\n\n\nO\n\nconfidence and provides a clear understanding to the citizen\ninitiatives regarding their role and the level and nature of support\nthey can expect in their intended projects and activities. Based on\nthese findings, policy implications have been drawn.\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nThe theoretical framework of this study is the implementation\nanalysis framework (Sabatier & Mazmanian, 2005), according\nto which, two transition processes are distinguished: the\nones emerging from a top-down, and those stemming from a\nbottom-up approach. To catalyse the energy transition, there\nis a need to broaden the commonly adopted strictly top-down\napproach, by embracing and fostering the roles that local\ngovernment, entrepreneurs and citizens can play together\n(Allen et al., 2012) (Hargreaves et al., 2013) (Klösters et al.,\n2020) (Psarra et al., 2024). They also point to the importance of\ncapacity building for local energy activities.\nIn the Netherlands, numerous local grassroot energy initiatives have emerged with the goal of accelerating the local\nenergy transition (Rotmans, 2011). There has been an enormous\nincrease in the number of Local Energy Initiatives (LEI)s\nover the last seven years (HIER; RVO, 2021). In 2020, there\nwere a total of 623 LEIs in almost every municipality in the\nNetherlands. This includes activities aimed at\nenergy generation, energy conservation, energy supply,\ncollective purchasing or commissioning and other energy-related\nactivities. The collective focuses on its own living environment\nand community (neighbourhood, village, city or region) and\ncombines energy goals with social, societal and economic\ngoals.” (HIER; RVO, 2021)\nIn previous research conducted by the Dutch Organisation\nfor Applied Scientific Research (TNO), insight was gained\ninto the role of local initiatives in the energy transition, and\nthe steps they go through when carrying out their activities\n(Klösters et al., 2020). They focused on ten LEIs spread over\ndifferent municipalities in the Netherlands. Various success\nfactors and barriers that arose during this process have been\n\nformulated. A large number of these barriers relate to the role\nof the municipality in facilitating the activities of the local\ninitiatives. Examples vary from barriers in legislation and\nregulations to diverging interests between local initiatives and\nthe municipality.\nDespite the growth in the number of LEIs, the impact of these\ncollectives on the national and international climate objectives is still limited (Wiekens & Germes, 2020). LEIs and\ntop-down energy planning procedures rarely align. The\npractical studies that are mentioned within the literature review\non LEIs, show that the role of the municipality in facilitating\nLEIs is often dominant and top-down oriented (Klösters\net al., 2020; van der Windt et al., 2021).
