The results of this research can\ninform decisions about how to teach critical thinking and future\nresearch. However, well-designed, up-to-date systematic reviews are\nstill needed for many teaching strategies.\n\n\nIntroduction\nCritical thinking about health\nWe designed the Be Smart about your Health resources for secondary school teachers and their students (in the 8th or\n9th year of school, age 13-15) in Kenya, Rwanda, and Uganda.1 The aim of these resources is to help students cope with\nthe abundance of information and misinformation about how to care for their health. Critical thinking is important for\nmaking informed choices and it is a core competency in the curricula for all three countries.2–4 However, it is often not\ntaught in the classroom, partly due to a lack of carefully developed educational resources, and a lack of information about\neffective teaching strategies. The Be Smart about your Health resources were developed to help fill this gap. These\nresources focus on critical thinking about health actions – things that people do to care for their health or the health of\nothers. Critical thinking about health actions is especially important because:\n• Good health is essential for daily life and depends on informed health choices, which depend on critical thinking\nabout health actions.\n• Health is important to everyone, which makes it a good starting point for learning critical thinking skills that\nstudents can transfer to other subjects, such as the environment.5\nThe Be Smart about your Health resources focus on nine key concepts that people need to understand and apply when\ndeciding what to believe about the effects of health actions and what to do.6,7 We undertook this overview of systematic\nreviews of the effects of teaching strategies to inform the design those resources.\nHow we have conceptualised critical thinking\nLearning to think critically is widely held to be an aim of education.8 However, there is not agreement on the definition of\n“critical thinking”, or which frameworks (conceptual structures intended to serve as a support or guide) best support\ncritical thinking.9–13 Ennis has defined critical thinking as “reasoned, reflective thinking focused on deciding what to\nbelieve or do”,14 and we use that definition in this overview.15\nThinking evolved to help us choose what to do to achieve our goals after taking account of estimates of the likely effects of\nour actions.16 A fundamental goal of critical thinking is to improve decision making by increasing the likelihood that we\nwill believe and act on those claims that are more likely to help us achieve our goals.17\nWhat we mean by “teaching strategies”\nDefinitions of teaching (instructional, or pedagogical) strategies (techniques, methods, or approaches) vary. Some\nauthors distinguish between strategies, techniques, methods, and approaches. However, there is overlap in how these\nterms are used. Our focus is on “different ways of helping students to learn - that is, different ways of helping them to\nachieve the learning outcomes that [teachers] have decided are important”.18 We refer to these as “teaching strategies”.\nThere are several lists of teaching strategies, organised in different ways.18–24 Beck surveyed 25 teacher education\ntextbooks and was unable to find two similar lists of teaching strategies.19 Pomerance and colleagues reviewed\n48 textbooks for elementary and secondary teacher training and found that none of the textbooks accurately described\nsix fundamental instructional strategies.24 At most, only two of the six were covered in any textbook, and when textbooks\ndid mention the strategies (allowing for a broad range of terminology and descriptions), the discussion could be as brief as\n1-2 sentences.\nWhy we have taken a broad focus in this overview\nAlthough our specific interest is in primary and secondary school students and critical thinking outcomes, we have not\nlimited this overview to that population or those outcome measures. There are four reasons for this. First, there are not\nmany reviews that focus specifically on critical thinking.25–36 To the extent that those reviews do consider the effects of\nspecific strategies, they tend to be broad categories and comparisons of strategies tend to be made indirectly (in metaregression analyses). For example, Abrami and colleagues explored differences in the effect of three types of instruction\n(dialogue; authentic or anchored instruction; and mentoring, coaching, or tutoring) across 341 comparisons with different\npopulations, outcome measures, and study designs.25 Thus, an overview that only included critical thinking as an\noutcome would be limited.\nSecond, although some learning outcomes may be of little relevance to learning to think critically, it is difficult to specify\na priori which outcomes are completely irrelevant and which might provide useful information despite not directly\nmeasuring critical thinking. For example, on the one hand it can be argued that outcome measures that only require\nretention of knowledge are irrelevant to critical thinking. In inquiry-based instruction students learn by engaging in\nthe thinking processes and activities of scientists, including asking scientifically oriented questions, collecting and\nanalysing evidence, developing explanations of scientific phenomena, and communicating those explanations.\n• Active learning\nActive learning engages students in the process of\nlearning through activities or discussion in class, as\nopposed to passively listening to an expert. It\nemphasizes higher order thinking and often involves\ngroup work.\n\nActive learning is probably more effective than\npassively listening to a lecture, especially for higherlevel cognitive skills, such as critical thinking.\n\n• Concept mapping\nA concept map is a node-link diagram in which each\nnode represents a concept, and each link identifies the\nrelationship between the two concepts it connects.\n\nConcept maps probably improve learning.\n\n• Applying knowledge to new problems\n“Generating and testing hypotheses” is a technique that\nrequires students to apply previous or developing\nknowledge to novel situations (problem solving).\n• Inquiry-based instruction\nIn inquiry-based instruction students learn by engaging\nin the thinking processes and activities of scientists,\nincluding asking scientifically oriented questions,\ncollecting and analysing evidence, developing\nexplanations of scientific phenomena, and\ncommunicating those explanations.\n• Student question-based inquiry\nStudent-question-based inquiry in science education is\nthe kind of inquiry that begins from the students’\nquestions.\n• Problem-based learning\nIn problem-based learning, students work\ncollaboratively to clarify and define a problem, propose\nsolutions based on what they already know, and identify\nlearning gaps associated with the problem. By engaging\nin self-directed learning, students work on filling those\ngaps, and conclude the process of learning by sharing\ntheir newly acquired knowledge and finalizing and\npresenting their solution.\n• Scaffolding for inquiry-based learning\nScaffolds are tools, strategies, and guides to support\nlearning by students. Computer-based scaffolding in\ninquiry-based or problem-based learning assists\nstudents as they generate solutions to complex and illstructured problems, goals, or tasks, helping students\nenhance knowledge and higher order thinking skills.\n\nActivities that involve applying knowledge to new\nproblems probably improve learning compared to\ntraditional instructional activities.\n\nInquiry-based instruction probably improves learning\nand guidance probably improves the effectiveness of\ninquiry-based instruction.\n\nThe effects of student-question-based inquiry are\nuncertain.\n\nIt is uncertain whether problem-based learning is\nmore effective than traditional, lecture and discussionbased, teacher-controlled teaching strategies in\nincreasing content knowledge, problem-solving\nability, or critical thinking skills.\n\nComputer-based scaffolding for inquiry-based or\nproblem-based learning is probably effective.\n\n\n\nTable 8. They provide a valuable\nbasis for informing decisions about the use of these strategies. However, these reviews have some important limitations,\nincluding:\n• Inconsistent terminology and definitions\n• Unnecessary duplication of efforts\n• Failure to consider adverse effects\n• Inadequate assessments of the risk of bias\n• Inadequate assessments of the credibility of subgroup effects (effect moderators)\nWe developed and revised a framework for categorising teaching strategies that met our needs. It is uncertain whether that\nframework will prove useful to others. Some of the reviews included in our overview could be classified in more than one\nway. Nonetheless, we found the framework helpful for organising the reviews and the teaching strategies and making\nsense of what is known. It would be helpful for users of this research if researchers studying the effects of teaching\nstrategies could reach a consensus on a taxonomy. Similarly, it would be helpful if agreement could be reached on the\nterminology that is used to characterize outcome measures. Coordination by an organisation such as the Campbell\nCollaboration might help to reduce unnecessary duplication of efforts and make it easier to update the reviews. It might\nalso help reduce the risk of bias in systematic reviews of teaching strategies.48\nOnly three of the systematic reviews included in this overview even mentioned adverse effects. Researchers commonly fail\nto consider potential adverse effects of educational and public health interventions.49–52 An adverse effect on an intended\noutcome is sometimes found, but other types of adverse effects are rarely considered, including misunderstanding,\nmisapplication of learning, distrust, cognitive dissonance, stress, inequities, conflict, and wasted time or resources.\nMany of the systematic reviews did not assess the risk of bias for studies included in the reviews using explicit criteria.\nThis made it difficult to judge the certainty of the evidence, i.e., confidence in the reported effect estimates.53–55 In\naddition, none of the reviews systematically assessed the credibility of reported subgroup (moderator) effects.56–58 The\ncredibility of most subgroup claims in randomized trials, even strong claims, is usually low.59,60\nStrategies for teaching critical thinking in primary and secondary schools\nAlthough we have included effect sizes in our summaries of strategies that we considered most relevant to teaching\ncritical thinking in primary and secondary schools, the average effect sizes summarised in Figure 3, as well as those\nreported in other overviews such as Hattie’s,61 are difficult to interpret for several reasons in addition to those noted\nabove, including:\n• Variable quality of the included meta-analyses\n• Variable quality of the studies included in the meta-analyses62\n• Variable outcome measures\n\n\n• Variation in what each strategy was compared to\n• Variable statistical methods\n• General challenges to interpreting effect sizes63\nFor these reasons, the reported effect sizes should not be used to compare the effectiveness of different teaching strategies\nand firm conclusions cannot be drawn about the magnitude of effects for most of the teaching strategies.\nNonetheless, the findings of the systematic reviews that we considered most relevant for teaching critical thinking in\nprimary and secondary schools helped to inform the design of the Be Smart about your Health resources. Because we\ndeveloped the resources in East Africa, many of the teaching strategies that we considered potentially relevant in other\nsettings were not practical in low-resource settings, particularly ones that depend on the availability of digital technology.\nWe initially pilot tested resources that used different teaching strategies for each lesson. However, this was inefficient,\nsince the strategies were new to both teachers and students, and this distracted from the learning goals of the lessons.\nTherefore, we selected a small number of strategies that we used consistently across lessons and only varied the main\nlesson strategy a few times (Table 9).1\nThe Be Smart about your Health intervention is being evaluated in randomized trials and process evaluations in Kenya,\nRwanda, and Uganda.64–69\nLimitations\nLimitations of this overview and our findings include:\n• The searches were limited to two databases (Education Research Complete (EBSCO) and Education Resources\nInformation Center (ERIC).\n• Coding the included reviews was challenging and there were frequent disagreements.\n• We did not systematically assess the risk of bias for included reviews.\n• The assessments of the certainty of the evidence for reported effects were based on information provided in the\nincluded reviews, which was frequently incomplete.\n• We did not assess the relevance of the reviews identified by the updated search or summarise the findings for any\nof those reviews.\nImplications\nA coordinated effort by educational researchers and education professionals is needed to:\n• Reach a consensus on terminology and definitions of teaching strategies,\n• Set priorities for systematic reviews of teaching strategies for which a high quality, up-to-date systematic review\ncannot be found, and\n• Reduce unnecessary duplication of efforts.\nTable 9. It similar to awarding recognition via\nphysical status icons, such as ribbons, medals, and\ntrophies.\n• Practice tests\nStudents take a practice test on studied material before\ntaking a final test on the same material.\n\n• Self-grading and peer-grading\nSelf-grading and peer-grading involves students\nmaking judgments about their own and others’\nacademic performance. They evaluate the extent to\nwhich performance criteria and standards have been\nmet and provide criterion-referenced feedback, that is,\ngrading, to themselves or others.\n\nBottom line\nComputer-based, elaborated feedback may improve\ncritical thinking. Immediate feedback may improve\nlower order thinking, such as understanding terms.\n\nDigital (and physical) badges may improve learner\nengagement and motivation. However, they can also\nhave a negative impact on engagement.\n\nPractice tests are probably more effective than\nrestudying for both retention of information and\ntransfer of knowledge or skills.\n\nSelf- and peer-grading probably improve learning.\n\nIndividual learning\nWe have included here strategies that individual students can do on in a classroom or at home, as well as\nindividualise instruction, which is tailored to the needs, skills, and interests of each student.\n• Note taking and summarising\nNote taking is the process of capturing key ideas and\nconcepts. Summarising is the process of identifying\nessential information and restating it in a condensed\nform.\n\nNote taking and summarising may improve learning\nand instructing students how to take notes and\nsummarise may be beneficial, but the size of the\neffects is uncertain and may depend on the type of\nnote taking and the instructional strategy that is used.\n\n• Non-linguistic representations\nNon-linguistic representations are strategies that\nteachers can use to encourage students to create, store,\nand manipulate visual or non-linguistic representations\neither in their minds or with concrete tools and displays.\n\nTeaching students to use non-linguistic\nrepresentations is probably effective, but the size of\nthe effect is uncertain.\n\n• Homework and practice\nHomework (tasks assigned to students by teachers that\nare meant to be completed outside of school hours) can\nserve a variety of purposes, including opportunities to\npractice or review materials, introducing new materials,\nencouraging the transfer of skills to new situations, and\nintegrating separately learned skills.\n\nHomework may have a small effect on academic\nachievement. The benefits of homework are likely to\ndepend on the nature of the homework and students’\ncircumstances.\n\n•\n\nComputer-based scaffolding for reading\ncomprehension\nScaffolds are tools, strategies, and guides to support\nstudents in regulating their learning.\n• Computer-based personalised instruction\nComputer-based personalised instruction includes\nautomated, adaptive guidance and technology\nprograms. Automated, adaptive guidance is broadly\ndefined as any guidance that occurs within computerbased instruction with two components: computer\nscoring of students’ work and computer delivery of a\nresponse to students’ work based on the automated\nscores.\n\nThe effectiveness of various types of computer-based\nscaffolding for reading comprehension is uncertain.\n\nComputer-based personalised instruction is probably\neffective, but the size of the effect varies.\n\n\n\nTable 8. These conditions are\nestablished to assuage the negative aspects of group\nbehaviour while maintaining the benefits.\n\nConstructive and interactive activities are probably\neffective for teaching scientific reasoning and scientific\nargumentation, but the size of the effect varies.\n\nCooperative learning probably improves learning.\n\n• Small group discussion\nSmall group discussion is a learner-centred, activelearning strategy. The aim is to stimulate learners’\ninterest in what they are studying by providing them\nwith autonomy over the learning activity. We\nincluded information about the need for teacher training or experience, whenever this was addressed in a review.\nDiscussion\nHundreds of systematic reviews of teaching strategies have been produced over the past 20 years. They provide a valuable\nbasis for informing decisions about the use of these strategies. However, these reviews have some important limitations,\nincluding:\n• Inconsistent terminology and definitions\n• Unnecessary duplication of efforts\n• Failure to consider adverse effects\n• Inadequate assessments of the risk of bias\n• Inadequate assessments of the credibility of subgroup effects (effect moderators)\nWe developed and revised a framework for categorising teaching strategies that met our needs. It is uncertain whether that\nframework will prove useful to others. Some of the reviews included in our overview could be classified in more than one\nway. Nonetheless, we found the framework helpful for organising the reviews and the teaching strategies and making\nsense of what is known. It would be helpful for users of this research if researchers studying the effects of teaching\nstrategies could reach a consensus on a taxonomy. Similarly, it would be helpful if agreement could be reached on the\nterminology that is used to characterize outcome measures. Coordination by an organisation such as the Campbell\nCollaboration might help to reduce unnecessary duplication of efforts and make it easier to update the reviews. It might\nalso help reduce the risk of bias in systematic reviews of teaching strategies.51\nOnly three of the systematic reviews included in this overview even mentioned adverse effects. Researchers commonly fail\nto consider potential adverse effects of educational and public health interventions.52–55 An adverse effect on an intended\noutcome is sometimes found, but other types of adverse effects are rarely considered, including misunderstanding,\nmisapplication of learning, distrust, cognitive dissonance, stress, inequities, conflict, and wasted time or resources.\nMany of the systematic reviews did not assess the risk of bias for studies included in the reviews using explicit criteria.\nThis made it difficult to judge the certainty of the evidence, i.e., confidence in the reported effect estimates.56–58 In\naddition, none of the reviews systematically assessed the credibility of reported subgroup (moderator) effects.59–61 The\ncredibility of most subgroup claims in randomized trials, even strong claims, is usually low.62,63\n\n\nStrategies for teaching critical thinking in primary and secondary schools\nAlthough we have included effect sizes in our summaries of strategies that we considered most relevant to teaching\ncritical thinking in primary and secondary schools, the average effect sizes summarised in Figure 3, as well as those\nreported in other overviews such as Hattie’s,64 are difficult to interpret for several reasons in addition to those noted\nabove, including:\n• Variable quality of the included meta-analyses\n• Variable quality of the studies included in the meta-analyses65\n• Variable outcome measures\n• Variation in what each strategy was compared to\n• Variable statistical methods\n• General challenges to interpreting effect sizes66\nFor these reasons, the reported effect sizes should not be used to compare the effectiveness of different teaching strategies\nand firm conclusions cannot be drawn about the magnitude of effects for most of the teaching strategies.\nNonetheless, the findings of the systematic reviews that we considered most relevant for teaching critical thinking in\nprimary and secondary schools helped to inform the design of the Be Smart about your Health resources. Because we\ndeveloped the resources in East Africa, many of the teaching strategies that we considered potentially relevant in other\nsettings were not practical in low-resource settings, particularly ones that depend on the availability of digital technology.\nWe initially pilot tested resources that used different teaching strategies for each lesson. However, this was inefficient,\nsince the strategies were new to both teachers and students, and this distracted from the learning goals of the lessons.\nTherefore, we selected a small number of strategies that we used consistently across lessons and only varied the main\nlesson strategy a few times (Table 9).1\nThe Be Smart about your Health intervention is being evaluated in randomized trials and process evaluations in Kenya,\nRwanda, and Uganda.67–72\nSome of the teaching strategies included in this review may be more effective for some subjects than others, however this\nis outside the scope of this review, but could benefit from further investigation.\nLimitations\nLimitations of this overview and our findings include:\n• The searches were limited to two databases (Education Research Complete (EBSCO) and Education Resources\nInformation Center (ERIC).\n• Coding the included reviews was challenging and there were frequent disagreements.\n• We did not systematically assess the risk of bias for included reviews.\nTable 9.
