The RED framework and its subsequent\nupdates provide the legal foundation for the energy transition\nby removing barriers to renewable deployment, promoting\nrenewable heating, cooling, and transport fuels, and supporting investment in cost-effective technologies (Plan Bleu, 2022).\nThe 2030 Energy and Climate Framework, initially set in 2014,\naimed to reduce emissions by at least 40%, increase renewable energy use, and improve energy efficiency by 27% by 2030\n(European Commission, 2014). These targets have been\nstrengthened under the Fit for 55 package to align with the\nEU’s goal of reducing net greenhouse gas emissions by 55%\nby 2030 (European Commission, 2023e). A 90% reduction\nin transport-related emissions is also needed, alongside a\ntargeted increase in inland waterways and short-sea shipping\nby 25% by 2030 and 50% by 2050, though these measures may\nimpact marine and coastal environments (European Commission,\n2019). More recently, the Clean Industrial Deal (European\nCommission, 2025a) outlined a roadmap to boost industrial\ncompetitiveness and accelerate decarbonisation by expanding\nrenewables, electrification, and circular economy practices by\n2030. Renewable energy is central to cutting greenhouse gas\nemissions. Building on previous COP commitments, COP29\nreaffirmed efforts to advance the Paris Agreement by accelerating energy system decarbonisation, expanding clean energy,\nphasing out fossil fuels, and aiming to limit global warming\nto well below 2°C, with efforts toward 1.5°C (UNFCCC, 2025).\nWind energy is the EU’s second-largest source of power generation capacity, closely following gas installations (deCastro\net al., 2019). Within this sector, offshore wind, implemented\nthrough offshore wind farms (OWFs), is expected to play\nan increasingly significant role in meeting the EU’s energy\nand climate objectives. Offshore turbines are installed at sea\nusing foundation systems adapted to water depth and seabed\nconditions. Fixed-bottom foundations are typically suitable for\nshallow waters up to 50 metres, whereas floating platforms are\nrequired in deeper areas (Nikitas et al., 2019). Offshore wind\noffers several advantages over onshore systems, including\n\n\nexposure to stronger, more consistent wind conditions, which\nimproves generation efficiency (Bilgili et al., 2011). The\nMediterranean presents a distinct opportunity for offshore\nwind development. Although there are currently no operational\nOWFs in the region (Plan Bleu, 2022), growing interest has\nbeen driven by favourable wind resources, declining technology costs, and increased international attention. Due to the\nprevalence of deep waters, fixed-bottom foundations are generally not feasible, making floating wind the most suitable\napproach (Martinez & Iglesias, 2021). Floating turbines can\naccess up to 80 per cent of global offshore wind resources\nlocated in waters deeper than 50 metres (WindEurope, 2023),\nand may offer additional benefits such as reduced visual impact\nand less interference with maritime and fishing activities\n(Farr et al., 2021; Le Visage, 2022). Recognising this potential,\nthe European Commission launched a strategy in 2020 to\nexpand marine renewable energy, setting targets to increase\noffshore wind capacity from 12 gigawatts to 60 gigawatts by\n2030, and to 300 gigawatts by 2050 (European Commission,\n2020a). In October 2023, a revised strategy raised the 2030\ntarget to 111 gigawatts and introduced measures to streamline permitting, enhance regional cooperation, and strengthen\nsupply chains (European Commission, 2023c). In addition to\nsupporting climate goals, the expansion of offshore wind has\nthe potential to reduce fossil fuel imports, improve air quality,\nand mitigate climate-related risks (WindEurope, 2023).\nAchieving these objectives will require coordinated action across\nMember States, including the development of national lowcarbon transition plans. In France, several floating wind farms\nare under development in the Golfe du Lion off the southern\ncoast (Occitanie region), notably the “Éoliennes Flottantes du\nGolfe du Lion” (EFGL) project, which comprises pilot and\npre-commercial sites along with two additional pilot projects\n(Gouty, 2022). Similarly, Italy is actively advancing floating\noffshore wind projects, particularly around Sicily, Sardinia,\nand Apulia, with government-supported initiatives reflecting a\nstrategic emphasis on floating technology (Watson Farley &\nWilliams, 2025). Spain’s offshore wind activities have historically focused on the Atlantic coast, where a mixture of fixedbottom and floating farms exist; however, recent efforts on the\nMediterranean coasts, specifically in the Balearic and Catalan\nregions, are concentrating on floating wind pilot projects to\naddress the challenges posed by deeper waters, where Spain\nis developing the “Parc Tramuntana” Floating Offshore Wind\nFarm Project (Diez-Caballero et al., 2022). Greece has also\nrecently enacted legislation to facilitate offshore wind development, with maritime spatial planning underway to identify\nsuitable zones predominantly for floating wind farms in the\nAegean, Ionian, and certain Mediterranean-facing coastal areas\n(Alma Economics, 2021). These initiatives align with national\nplans outlined in the Maritime Spatial Planning Directive\namendment 2023 (European Parliament and Council, 2023b).\nHowever, given the unique characteristics of the Mediterranean\nSea, evaluations of potential impacts remain speculative due\nto the lack of concrete operational examples. Research focuses\non OWFs in the North, Baltic, and North Atlantic, which differ\n\nfrom the Mediterranean’s diverse shelf morphology and fragile\nbenthic habitats like seagrass meadows and coralligenous\nreefs (Lloret et al., 2022). The limited and fragmented nature\nof existing research on offshore wind development in the\nMediterranean has fostered an overly optimistic view of its\ncontribution to the green transition, often overlooking regionspecific risks. Much of the current literature prioritises technical\nfeasibility and economic potential, while long-term environmental and socio-economic implications remain underexplored.\nIn particular, the impacts of deep-water floating infrastructure,\nnow central to Mediterranean deployment plans, are insufficiently understood. Concerns include pollutant release from\nturbine materials, electromagnetic fields from subsea cabling,\nand underwater noise, alongside broader disruptions to marine\necosystems and coastal livelihoods. While these issues have\nbeen studied in Northern European contexts, Mediterraneanspecific conditions, such as its semi-enclosed geography, high\nbiodiversity, and densely populated coastlines, necessitate a\nmore tailored analysis. This paper first examines the legislative\nframework governing offshore wind at EU level, highlighting\nits limited applicability in the Mediterranean and identifying\nkey regulatory and enforcement shortcomings. It then assesses\nthe environmental, social, and economic challenges associated with offshore wind deployment in the region, drawing\non recent studies and pilot projects. The findings suggest that\nwithout integrated, context-specific governance, the benefits\nof offshore wind may be undermined by unaddressed harms,\nhighlighting the need to align decarbonisation with strong\nenvironmental and social protections.\n\nMarine environmental governance and policy\nframeworks in the Mediterranean\n\nSince the 1992 Rio Declaration on Environment and Development\n(United Nations, 1992), the international community has intensified efforts to promote the sustainable use of marine resources.\nThe Declaration remains a foundational legal and policy instrument, having established core principles, such as the precautionary principle, polluter pays, and common but differentiated\nresponsibilities, that continue to shape global and regional\nenvironmental governance frameworks today. A notable advancement in this regard was the adoption of Sustainable Development Goal 14 in 2015, which seeks to “conserve and sustainably\nuse the oceans, seas, and marine resources for sustainable\ndevelopment” (United Nations General Assembly, 2016). In parallel with these global commitments, the Mediterranean Action\nPlan (MAP) was instituted in 1975 as a multilateral environmental agreement under the auspices of the United Nations\nEnvironment Programme (UNEP). Its principal aim has been\nthe protection of the marine environment through a harmonised\nregional framework. Over subsequent decades, UNEP/MAP\nhas evolved into a model of global governance, instrumental in the negotiation and adoption of the Convention for\nthe Protection of the Marine Environment and the Coastal\nRegion of the Mediterranean (Barcelona Convention) and its\nrelated protocols. With seven protocols under the MAP, the\nBarcelona Convention is the region’s key legally binding multilateral environmental agreement (UNEP/MAP, 1995), which\ncame into force in 2004. Its Contracting Parties (CP) consist\n\n\nof 21 Mediterranean countries and the EU, encompassing the\nMediterranean Sea eastward from the Strait of Gibraltar. The\nConvention allows each party to extend its application to its\nnational territory, ensuring a broad and adaptable scope for\nregional cooperation.\nCooperation mechanisms, such as those under the Barcelona\nConvention, aim to coordinate the use of maritime resources,\nstrengthen governance, and foster economic growth while promoting sustainable practices (Plan Bleu, 2022). These efforts\nalign with broader frameworks of renewable energy policies\nand marine ecosystem protections. According to Art.4, the CPs\nagree to take all necessary measures, individually or jointly, to\nprevent, reduce, and eliminate pollution of the Mediterranean\nSea and to protect the marine environment to support sustainable development (UNEP/MAP, 1995). A key component is the\nDumping Protocol, entered into force in 1978, and amended in\n1995 (PNUE/PAM, 1976). Excluding Montenegro, all CPs\nto the Barcelona Convention are also parties to this protocol,\nwhich prohibits dumping activities, incineration at sea, and\nwaste disposal, except for specific materials (PNUE/PAM,\n1976). In 2021, during the 22nd Meeting of the CPs in Turkey,\na decision was made to amend the Annex to the Dumping\nProtocol. These amendments, binding in the EU under Art. 29\nof the Barcelona Convention, revised the criteria for issuing\npermits for disposal at sea. The updated criteria outline the\ncharacteristics and composition of dumped materials and the\nattributes of dumping sites and deposit methods (European\nCommission, 2021b). This reflects the CPs’ commitment to\nreducing pollution and ensuring sustainable management of the\nMediterranean Sea.\nIn the Mediterranean, efforts are guided by the Mediterranean\nStrategy for Sustainable Development (MSSD), adopted during\nCOP19 of the Barcelona Convention in 2016. The MSSD provides\na framework to integrate regional, sub-regional, and national\npolicies with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development\n(Mancini, 2022). This approach aligns with the EU Marine\nStrategy Framework Directive (MSFD) 2016–2025, which states\nthat ecosystem protection is a cornerstone of economic and\nenvironmental policy (European Commission, 2020b). It builds\non this by targeting the Good Environmental Status (GES) of\nmarine and coastal environments. The MSFD fosters ecosystembased management, harmonising efforts among 23 coastal\nand five landlocked EU states and coordinating with non-EU\ncountries to sustainably manage a 5.72 million km2 marine\narea (European Commission, 2020b). In 2018, the CPs of the\nBarcelona Convention adopted the Ecosystem Approach\n(EcAp) and agreed on a roadmap for its implementation in the\nMediterranean. The EcAp is a strategy for the integrated management of land, water, and living resources that promotes\ntheir conservation and equitable use. As such, it is the guiding\nprinciple for policy development and implementation undertaken under the auspices of the Barcelona Convention, such as\nachieving GES in the Mediterranean marine and coastal environment through informed management decisions based on\nintegrated quantitative assessment and monitoring (SPA/RAC,\n2015). At the Mediterranean level, the EcAp and the MSFD\n\nare implemented in synergy and coherence, and they are among\nthe most ambitious international frameworks for marine environmental protection (European Commission, 2025b). Additional\nmeasures include Art.9 of the Integrated Coastal Zone Management Protocol (ICZM) and the Union for the Mediterranean’s\n2015 Ministerial Declaration, all of which emphasise ecosystem preservation. EU policies, such as the Water Framework\nDirective (WFD) and its updates, further reinforce these efforts\nby aiming to achieve good ecological status for water bodies.\nDespite delays from funding and implementation challenges,\nthe WFD created a governance framework for integrated water\nmanagement, reducing chemical pollution and connecting human\nactivities to environmental impacts (European Parliament and\nCouncil, 2000).\nThis integrated approach ensures marine ecosystems remain\nfunctional and resilient for future generations. After launching the world’s first global framework to finance a sustainable\nOcean Economy (European Investment Bank, 2018), the EU\nintroduced its new strategy on Biodiversity for 2030, focusing\non “making nature healthy again” (Bassetti, 2021). The strategy boosts the protection of marine ecosystems by expanding\nthe protected areas and stressing the importance of the EcAp\nfor managing human activities at sea. Sustainable innovation\npresents significant opportunities in green ports, shipping, aquaculture, water management, conservation, recycling, and maritime ecotourism, all key to the region’s future. Meanwhile, the\nEU Commission emphasised the benefits of sustainable blue\neconomy activities, advocating for an international Blue Economy Sustainability Framework (European Commission, 2021c).\nHowever, according to the results of the 2020 report on the\nfirst implementation cycles of the MSFD, the EU framework\nfor protecting the marine environment needs to be strengthened (European Commission, 2020a). Indeed, GES is achievable only if the pressures on the marine and coastal environment\ndecrease and, therefore, if the uses of the marine and coastal\nenvironment that cause these pressures are adjusted. Surprisingly, the notion of GES appears only once in the text of the\nEuropean Green Deal and only once in the Commission’s\nCommunication on the Sustainable Blue Economy (European\nCommission, 2021a). This may indicate a persistent lack of integration of marine protection into broader policies or a dominance of economic priorities over marine conservation, even\nunder the guise of climate change adaptation.\nTo address environmental challenges, the EU approved a\ntaxonomy defining criteria for economic activities contributing\nto climate adaptation and prioritising adaptation solutions\n(European Commission, n.d.). Recognising the scale and diversity of its natural heritage, the EU has developed extensive\nlegislation to protect and enhance Mediterranean biodiversity.\nCentral to this is the Nature Directives, which establish\nNatura 2000, a network of protected areas comprising Special\nAreas of Conservation and Special Protection Areas under the\nHabitats Directive (Council of the European Communities, 1992)\nand the Birds Directive (European Parliament and Council,\n2009). Natura 2000 ensures the conservation of rare, threatened,\nor endemic species, including all wild bird species, through\n\n\nprotection, management, and monitoring measures. It covers\n18% of the EU’s land area and 6% of its sea area, although\nsignificant gaps remain in marine coverage, and only half of\nthe Natura sites have management plans with conservation\nobjectives (European Commission, 2014). The “Fitness Check”\nevaluation confirmed the directives’ effectiveness but emphasised the importance of practical implementation. Since then,\nlegislative advancements have enhanced marine protection\nwithin the Natura 2000 framework. In 2023, Implementing\nDecision (EU) 2023/2806 standardized site-specific data formats, improving transparency in reporting across Member States\n(European Commission, 2023b). Additionally, the Nature\nRestoration Law, adopted in June 2024, introduced legally\nbinding restoration targets aiming to restore at least 20% of\nthe EU’s land and marine ecosystems by 2030, focusing on\nNatura 2000 sites, and up to 90% of degraded habitats by 2050\n(European Parliament, 2024). These initiatives are complemented\nby the ongoing revision of the Marine Strategy Framework\nDirective to strengthen the assessment and achievement of\nGood Environmental Status (European Commission, 2025b).\n\nLegal context of offshore wind in the\nMediterranean\n\nIn the Mediterranean, OWFs are vital to the Blue Economy, a\nsector generating significant regional income. As the offshore\nwind industry expands, its environmental impacts, particularly\non marine ecosystems, remain insufficiently studied. The sector has evolved from utilising fixed-bottom turbines in shallow waters up to 60 m deep to deploying floating turbines in\ndepths exceeding 120 m, with current technological developments exploring installation in waters as deep as 1,000 m (Farr\net al., 2021). The Blue Economy encompasses traditional\nmaritime activities such as fishing, aquaculture, coastal tourism and emerging sectors such as renewable energy and marine\nbiotechnologies (European Commission, 2021a). The Sustainable Blue Economy focuses on clean technologies, renewable\nenergy, and circular material flows to secure long-term social\nand economic stability within planetary boundaries (World Wide\nFund, 2015). It seeks to balance the optimal use of maritime\nnatural capital with human and social capital, advancing sustainable development, social equity, and reduced ecological risks.\nThe EU’s taxonomy for climate change mitigation, outlined\nin Regulation (EU) 2020/852, provides a framework for determining whether economic activities and investments qualify\nas environmentally sustainable, including provisions for the\nmarine environment (European Parliament and Council of the\nEuropean Union, 2020). The regulation focuses on transitioning to renewable energy, reducing fossil fuel reliance, and\npromoting carbon-neutral energy sources. It allows activities\nwith no viable low-carbon alternatives as long as they limit\nglobal temperature rise to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.\nSuch activities must comply with strict GHG emissions standards and support the development of low-carbon alternatives. In\nJune 2023, the Commission adopted delegated acts expanding\nthe Taxonomy Regulation beyond climate objectives to include\ntechnical screening criteria for four additional environmental\n\ngoals, including the sustainable use and protection of water\nand marine resources (European Commission, 2023d).\nCommission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2485 establishes\nthe general framework for determining whether an economic\nactivity and its investments qualify as environmentally sustainable and includes several chapters related to the marine\nenvironment (European Commission, 2023a). It ensures the\nconservation of rare, threatened, and endemic species, including\nall wild bird species in the EU, through measures for their\nprotection, management, and monitoring. It also highlights the\nrestoration of wetlands and the development of renewable\nenergy sources such as wind, ocean, hydropower, geothermal\nenergy, and cogeneration technologies. Furthermore, it states\nthat offshore wind projects must not hinder achieving GES as\ndefined by the MSFD, requiring measures to prevent or mitigate\nimpacts on biodiversity and seabed integrity, supported by an\nenvironmental impact assessment and necessary mitigation and\ncompensation measures. However, the references to the proper\nuse of offshore wind turbines contained in the Regulation are\ninsufficient to address their wide-ranging environmental impacts,\nincluding harm to marine ecosystems and pollutant emissions\nduring their creation and dismantling. Furthermore, their buoys\nor mooring systems could contain one or more environmentally\nhazardous substances.\nNatura 2000 sites, including Special Protection Areas for\nbirds, are among the EU’s most biodiverse and valuable areas\nand must be protected from OWFs developments. Damage to\nthese sites risks their legal status and may result in condemnation of member states (Gouty, 2021). Although the Habitats\nDirective does not outright prohibit wind farms within Natura\n2000 sites, it requires a case-by-case assessment demonstrating\nthat the project will not cause significant adverse impacts, such\nas habitat loss, degradation, fragmentation, species disturbance,\nor other indirect effects on environmental quality (Fabrégat,\n2021). Such assessments must consider the scale and magnitude of impacts and ongoing projects in the area. When appropriately integrated with other economic activities, including\nother renewable energy sources, the negative impacts of OWFs\ncould be reduced, and biodiversity may even benefit; however, there is no current legal basis at the national or EU level\nto guarantee this outcome. The European Commission’s guidance document on wind energy developments in Natura 2000\nsites aims to align with updated EU renewable energy and\noffshore wind policies (European Commission, DirectorateGeneral for Environment, 2020). It clarifies the application of\nNatura Directives and updates the 2011 guidance on wind farms\nin protected areas, emphasising the need for further knowledge, best practices, and policy integration. Nonetheless, as\nEC communications are non-legally binding (EUR-Lex, 2007),\nthis guidance serves primarily an informational role and lacks\nenforceability (European Parliament and Council of the\nEuropean Union, 2020). This limits its credibility and legal\nweight, underscoring the necessity for thorough case-by-case\nanalyses prior to OWF development and revealing gaps in EU\n\n\n\nlegislation where advances in renewable energy may conflict\nwith biodiversity protection goals.\n\nfunctions, and their disruption can lead to a cascade of\necological consequences.\n\nEnvironmental challenges of offshore wind farms:\nDifferentiating fixed and floating technologies\n\n(2) The risk of bird and bat collisions with turbine blades\nis a significant concern, especially for migratory species.\nSeabirds in the Mediterranean region, many of which are\nendangered, may have their migration routes altered or\ninterrupted due to the placement of turbines (Conseil national\nde la protection de la nature, 2021; UNEP/MAP, 2022). The Mediterranean’s status as one of the world’s\nmost heavily trafficked seas further complicates OWF deployment. The installation of wind farms may reduce navigable\nspace, increase collision risks for commercial and passenger vessels, and redirect traffic onto already congested routes\n(Le Visage, 2022).\nThe generation of wind energy depends on materials associated with high supply risks. Modern turbines employ advanced\npermanent magnet (PM) generators and lightweight, fatigueresistant blades designed for high efficiency and durability.\nTurbine components are typically constructed from steel alloys\nincorporating nickel, manganese, titanium, and other elements,\nwhile blades are composed of fibreglass and resin. Critically,\nthe production of these technologies is heavily reliant on rare\nearth elements (REEs) such as neodymium and dysprosium,\nresources that are largely imported from outside the EU with\nChina and Latin America accounting for the majority of\nsupply (Bobba et al., 2020). Demand for these materials\nis projected to increase substantially by 2050 (Hund et al.,\n2020). Although the processing of raw materials remains environmentally intensive, emerging technologies offer opportunities to enhance both competitiveness and sustainability. For\ninstance, iron nitride-based PMs, which utilise more abundant\n\nmaterials and offer cost advantages, are expected to become\ncommercially viable in the near future. Similarly, advancements\nin recycling techniques for REEs could substantially reduce\nenvironmental impacts. While recycling REEs remains challenging at every stage, recovering them from consumer materials presents a promising alternative to conventional extraction\nmethods (Balaram, 2019). However, to capitalise on these developments, the EU must implement robust legislation to support\nmaterial collection, recycling infrastructure, and transparent\nlabelling systems for PM content (Hund et al., 2020). Despite\nprogress, complex component separation and limited data on\nREE content in end-of-life products make recycling costly and\ninefficient. Continued innovation, policy support, and industry\ncollaboration are essential for sustainable material cycles.\nThis dependency raises concerns about the EU’s strategic\nautonomy and the long-term viability of its energy transition.\nAlthough the cost of renewable energy is decreasing, the extraction and processing of raw materials pose significant environmental challenges. The mining sector currently accounts for\nbetween 2% and 11% of global energy consumption (Guilbaud,\n2016). Climate-smart mining practices are necessary to prevent shifting emissions from power and unsustainable extraction\ncould undermine the environmental benefits of wind energy.\nClimate-smart mining practices will be essential to prevent the\ntransfer of emissions from energy production to raw material\nsourcing. Moreover, wind turbine costs are highly sensitive to\nfluctuations in metal prices, particularly those linked to REEs.\nThe supply chain for wind technology is vulnerable at various\nstages, from raw material sourcing to component manufacturing. China’s near-monopoly on REEs and PMs remains a strategic concern, exemplified by the 2011 market restrictions that\ntriggered global price volatility (Bobba et al., 2020).\nIn response, several European states have accelerated seabed\nexploration to identify domestic sources of REEs. Polymetallic nodules and cobalt-rich crusts in deep-sea environments are\nemerging as key sources of critical materials, drawing growing interest due to their vast metal content and mining potential\n(Hund et al., 2020). While seabed mapping aligns with\nUNESCO’s target of charting 80% of the ocean floor by the\nend of the decade, it often precedes commercial exploitation.\nDeep-sea mining raises serious environmental concerns, including\nsediment disturbance, noise, light pollution, and the introduction\nof invasive species (United Nations Environment Programme,\n2014). Exploring deep seabeds is often followed by exploiting\nthese areas, which are still mysterious and widely unknown. However, deep-sea mining presents environmental threats. Extraction\ninvolves pulverising mineral crusts pumped to vessels, releasing\npollutants, noise, light, and invasive species. The light affects\ndeep-water species, while the altered water composition\nreduces oxygen and clouds the environment (United Nations\nEnvironment Programme, 2014). These activities may significantly alter marine ecosystems, reduce oxygen levels, and\nimpair water clarity. Deep-sea vent communities are especially\nvulnerable and can take years to recover from disturbance\n(Joseph, 2017; Van Dover, 2011).\nThe strategic implications of OWF development are increasingly evident in the Mediterranean, where coastal states are\nasserting claims to marine resources through the establishment\n\n\nof Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs), in accordance with the\nUnited Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)\n(United Nations, 1982). Unlike hydrocarbon exploitation, wind\nenergy development requires similar territorial claims, which\nhas led to overlapping EEZs and escalating disputes due to the\nregion’s complex geography (Le Visage, 2022). The seabed has\nbecome a contested space, with increasing militarisation and\nconcerns over the security of underwater infrastructure, as evidenced by the deployment of deep-operating drones by the\nFrench Navy (Kloetzli, 2022). Despite these tensions, joint\nmanagement and cooperation would benefit regional stability\nand environmental protection. Historically, Mediterranean\nstates limited maritime claims to territorial seas and ecological\nprotection zones. However, the discovery of fossil fuel deposits and the advancement of renewable energy have prompted\na shift towards full EEZ declarations (Le Visage, 2022;\nPellen-Blin et al., 2019).\nConcurrently, the offshore wind sector is progressing beyond\nthe demonstration stage. However, large-scale deployment\nis constrained by supply chain bottlenecks, insufficient port\ninfrastructure, logistical challenges, and a lack of specialised\ninstallation vessels. As turbines increase in size and are sited\nfurther offshore, these barriers become more pronounced\n(Torres et al., 2023). The construction process is also weatherdependent, requiring uninterrupted operations and substantial\nstorage facilities. High vessel rental costs and port constraints\npose further difficulties (Vis & Ursavas, 2016). Therefore,\naddressing component availability, infrastructure limitations,\nand logistical capacity will be essential to enabling the sector’s\nfuture growth.\n\nDiscussion and conclusion\n\nClimate change is a principal driver of biodiversity loss in the\nMediterranean, affecting both terrestrial and marine ecosystems. While substantial legal frameworks exist to reduce landbased greenhouse gas emissions and environmental degradation,\nmarine governance remains fragmented and poorly aligned with\nrecent technological advancements in marine renewable\nenergy. This regulatory gap risks prioritising climate mitigation\nat the expense of marine conservation, thereby undermining\nthe region’s ability to GES and a genuinely sustainable Blue\nEconomy. OWFs are often promoted as key instruments of the\ngreen transition, yet their environmental and socio-economic\nimpacts, particularly in the Mediterranean, remain underexplored (Defingou et al., 2019; Plan Bleu, 2022). Technological\ninnovations such as radar and camera systems for monitoring\nbird movements, ultraviolet-reflective turbine blades, acoustic\ndeterrents, and temporal shutdowns during migratory periods\nmay reduce ecological harm. However, these must be supported\nby rigorous ecological baselines, real-time monitoring, and\nlong-term studies, especially as floating wind technologies are\ndeployed at increasing depths and scales. The region’s deep\nwaters and ecological sensitivity necessitate floating wind technology, a solution still in its early stages and not yet deployed\nat scale. While wind energy is generally regarded as a clean\nalternative to fossil fuels, the construction and operation of OWFs,\nboth fixed and floating, pose significant risks including underwater noise, habitat disturbance, chemical and electromagnetic\n\npollution, and cumulative impacts from multiple installations.\nThese threats are particularly acute in biodiversity-rich or\npoorly studied marine areas.\nCurrent legislative frameworks exhibit inconsistencies and\ngaps that hinder effective risk assessment and mitigation. The\nlack of harmonisation across EU directives and limited coordination among Member States exacerbate these challenges.\nComprehensive environmental impact assessments must be\nmandated for all project stages, from site selection through\nto decommissioning, integrating cumulative and synergistic\nimpacts. The application of the Precautionary Principle, embedded in the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union\nand reflected in the Marine Strategy Framework Directive and\nthe Maritime Spatial Planning Directive, is essential to safeguard marine biodiversity (European Parliament and Council,\n2023b). The findings highlight the urgent need for legislative\nreform and stronger regulatory frameworks to support a sustainable offshore wind strategy in the Mediterranean. Inadequate\nlegislation and limited research on socio-economic and environmental impacts currently impede progress. Protection of\nmaritime cultural heritage and biodiversity requires expanded\nprotected areas and enhanced regional cooperation. Furthermore, diversifying the REEs supply chain is critical to reducing EU dependence on China and can be advanced through\npartnerships and exploratory projects in northern European\ncountries such as Sweden, Finland, Germany, and Norway\n(Bobba et al., 2020).\nOffshore wind energy offers significant potential to contribute to decarbonisation and sustainable development goals. Yet,\nwithout a more holistic, evidence-based, and precautionary\napproach, the sector risks replicating environmental harms it\nseeks to mitigate. The Mediterranean’s energy future depends\nnot only on technological innovation but also on robust governance, environmental integrity, and collaborative regional\nmanagement. More rigorous ecological baselines and long-term\nstudies are urgently needed as offshore wind development\nexpands into deeper waters through floating technologies. Policy\nframeworks must maintain environmental integrity alongside\nenergy transition goals, particularly in biodiversity-rich or understudied marine regions. New studies on the manufacturing,\noperation, disposal, and recycling of wind turbines are needed,\nalongside international guidelines and pilot projects, to ensure\nsustainability and long-term viability. While offshore wind\ncan contribute to sustainable development goals, its risks must\nbe addressed through innovation, research, and a holistic\nregional approach. Otherwise, it may ultimately fall short of\nexpectations as the “New Eldorado”.\n\nEthical approval and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\n\nData availability\n\nNo data are associated with this article.
