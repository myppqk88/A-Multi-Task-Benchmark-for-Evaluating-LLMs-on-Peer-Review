Various assessments and scientific\nstudies have shown that the evidence base for decision-making is\nalready sufficient to justify rapid and large-scale transformative\naction (e.g. EEA, 2019; EEA, 2025; Kallis et al., 2025).\nHowever, this evidence has not yet translated into actionable change capable of slowing down or even eradicating\nunsustainable pressures on climate (IPCC, 2023), biodiversity\n(IPBES, 2024), or resource use (UNEP, 2024).\nIn other words, the great acceleration of environmental\npressures continues despite science-based calls for sustainability\ntransition (Steffen et al., 2015). This, in turn, has partly shaped priorities\nin research and innovation (R&I) activities in global, European\nand national levels. However, the impacts have been insubstantial. For example, the SDGs have influenced societal\ndevelopment primarily on a discursive level by repackaging\nexisting initiatives using the language of the SDGs, rather than\ninitiating transformative action (Biermann et al., 2022;\nLyytimäki et al., 2025; UN, 2025).\nAddressing sustainability transitions requires navigating a\npolycrisis of climate change, biodiversity and habitat loss\nand the impact of environmental pollution on human and\necosystem health as well as crises in the political, economic\nand social spheres, the ‘slowbalisation’ of the global economy\nand trade and growing societal fragmentation over values and\nidentities (EEA, 2023). Despite the\nincreasing knowledge base on environmental and sustainability\nissues, prospects for coherent and forward-looking evidencebased decision-making might be eroding in the 2020s. The\npractical challenges associated with this type of transdisciplinary research, particularly those involving non-scientific\nactors, are, however, pronounced (D’Amato et al., 2025). For\ninstance, in Italy, improved coordination of funding mechanisms alongside this dialogue was deemed essential. Similarly,\nin France as well as in Austria, fostering transdisciplinarity\nand the collaborative construction of research with diverse\nactors—including citizens, associations, firms, and local\nauthorities—was recognised as vital. This isn’t just\na matter of fairness; it is essential for ensuring that transition efforts are effective, acceptable and sustainable for all.\nAchieving this inclusivity necessitates specific practices and\npolicies designed to dismantle barriers to participation amongst\nunder-represented individuals, social groups, firms, sectors,\nand regions, while fostering the integration of sustainability\nresearch and entrepreneurial activities. Ultimately, the objective\nis to ensure equitable opportunities for all segments of society\nto engage with and benefit from environmental and sustainability R&I (Lähteenmäki-Smith et al., 2023; Planes-Satorra &\nPaunov, 2017). Collaborative co-creation and co-design SPPI\nprocesses exemplify this inclusive approach, involving diverse\nstakeholder groups in initiatives aligned with shared interests,\nsuch as those related to urban planning tasks (Lee et al., 2024) or\nin developing urban transformative capacity (Wolfram, 2016).\n\n\nInclusion has already been recognised as a critical dimension\nof R&I policy, contributing to greater equitability and sustainability by fostering the acceptance of policy missions\nand facilitating their implementation (UN, 2023). Effective\npolicy action necessitates a comprehensive understanding of\nits potential impacts across diverse stakeholder groups,\nensuring the involvement of all relevant parties throughout\nplanning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. It is\ncrucial to acknowledge that incorporating all stakeholders into\nthe decision-making process can present challenges and risks\n(Bogner & Dahlke, 2022). These include the possibility of\npremature assumptions of consensus, the potential for dominant actors to unduly influence outcomes, and the adoption of\noverly simplistic solutions that prove difficult to scale (Hermans\net al., 2011).\nTherefore, careful consideration must be given to involving relevant and representative stakeholder groups and managing the\nprocess in a structured and systematic manner (Firus et al.,\n2024). A balanced approach is needed—one that actively seeks\ndiverse voices while remaining alert against potential pitfalls\nand ensuring that outcomes remain grounded in robust evidence\nand a clear understanding of the complexities of the transition process. Fundamentally, inclusive SPPI represents a\nprocess of fostering trust, with the assurance that equitable\nsolutions addressing the divergent impacts of the transition\nprocess will be identified, even in a context of long-term,\nglobal environmental change.\nIn Box 1. we propose practical steps towards more inclusive\nSPPI, grounded in the principles of relational trust building,\nequitable participation and inclusiveness, and building from\nthe experiences from our European cases. Design\nthese forums with a deliberate focus on fostering mutual\nunderstanding and trust, embedding structured facilitation\nmethods, participatory narrative-building and transparent\ndecision-making rules to bridge divergent perspectives and\nprevent elite capture. These\nworkshops should explicitly address underlying power dynamics\nand systemic inequalities that contribute to environmental and\nsocial challenges. These factors contribute to difficulties in\nachieving coherent SPPI and underscore the need for improved\ncoordination among ongoing projects.\nRespondents from multiple countries observed that fragmented\norganizational structures and divergent administrative practices\nresult in inefficient resource allocation. Facilitating personnel\ntransfers between sectors could contribute to improved integration. Initiatives that actively convene researchers, businesses,\nand policymakers are essential. The establishment of boundary\norganizations or knowledge ecosystems to connect projects\nand overcome siloed approaches was consistently highlighted.\nUltimately, respondents stressed the necessity for enhanced\ncoordination of R&I initiatives to generate more systemic\nknowledge and drive transformative change. For example,\n\nSlovakia underscored the importance of cross-disciplinary\nresearch approaches. Findings and methodologies from one\nresearch area should be systematically applied to others.\n\nDifferent policy contexts for SPPI\nEnsuring that research outcomes are readily applicable and genuinely useful within specific local contexts was emphasised\nas a basic function for SPPI. Current capacities to utilise SPPI\ndiffer considerably. As highlighted in Flanders, SPPI addresses\na diverse spectrum of needs among various stakeholders, encompassing policy support, technological development, heightened\ncitizen awareness, societal acceptance, resources for demonstration and implementation, robust monitoring methodologies,\nand a deeper understanding of human behaviour. Conversely, in\nItaly, research outcomes remain underutilised, risking remaining purely theoretical. A significant challenge in Italy is the difficulty faced by the nation’s extensive network of small and\nmedium-sized enterprises (SMEs)—a defining feature of the\nproductive sector—in accessing R&I, hindering their progress\ntowards green, energy, digital, and technological transitions.\nSimilarly, in Austria the need to redefine the role of SMEs\nwithin R&I activities was highlighted.\nThe diversity of national contexts and policy systems influencing SPPI presents a significant obstacle to the development of\nrecommendations applicable across Europe. Overly centralised systems can impede effective engagement by failing to\naccount for local contexts and regional requirements, as observed\nin France. The federal structure of Germany was noted as a\nsource of complexity in coordinating diverse regional interests\nand ensuring equitable representation. Regional and linguistic\ndiversity complicates policy coordination and communication,\nas evidenced by the experience of the Basque Country. Bureaucratic processes can impede the translation of scientific research\ninto actionable policies, thereby affecting implementation,\nas highlighted by observations from Italy.\nThe diversity of policy contexts also presents unique national\napproaches for SPPI. For example, Wales possesses a distinct\nlegislative framework through its Well-being of Future Generations Act (2015) and Environment (Wales) Act (2016), the first\nof their kind to legally incorporate the UN Sustainable Development Goals. This framework mandates a joined-up approach\nacross the public sector and requires the Sustainable Management of Natural Resources, thereby embedding an integrated\nconsideration of environmental, social, and economic well-being\ninto all levels of policy and practice. This provides a strong,\nlegally-backed context for SPPI that differs significantly from\nother regions.\nThe significance of established knowledge producers—including\nuniversities, research institutions, governmental advisory bodies, and expert councils—was consistently highlighted. These\norganizations typically provide expert knowledge to inform\npolicy formulation. The influence of such bodies is contingent\n\n\n\nupon the prevailing policy environment. In Ireland, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Research Programme\ncoordinates national environmental research initiatives through\nthe National Environmental Research Coordination Group. Every\nthree years, the EPA conducts a comprehensive assessment\nto determine thematic research priorities. This process facilitates the long-term strategic development of SPPI by ensuring\nalignment between research and policy objectives.\nHowever, established knowledge infrastructures also present a\nrisk of exacerbating bureaucracy and administrative complexity,\npotentially hindering access to funding, project implementation, and effective communication. This risk was noted in\nrelation to both European-level funding and within countries\nlike Italy. Therefore, ensuring streamlined and accessible processes is vital, particularly for new entrants to SPPI, to foster\nbroad and effective participation.\n\nThe challenge of transformative transdisciplinarity\nAcross the surveyed countries, the need for more diverse and\ninteractive communication to foster transformative collaboration\n—research intrinsically linked to societal change—at local,\nnational, and international levels was frequently emphasized. In Switzerland, it was observed that funding mechanisms should be better aligned with transformative research\nby fostering unconventional forms of scientific collaboration,\nexperimentation, and outputs. However, the continued need for\nconventional, unidirectional science communication was also\nacknowledged. This underscores the necessity to utilize the\ndiverse tools of SPPI through the integration of complementary\napproaches.\nAddressing complex sustainability challenges undeniably\nrequires the inclusion of diverse fields of expertise (Godemann\n& Michelsen, 2011). However, effectively bringing these disciplines together necessitates significant effort in cultivating\nmutual understanding and establishing effective modes of collaboration between fields of natural sciences, the humanities\nand social sciences, each with its own inherent culture and\nmethodologies. Beyond interdisciplinarity, there is a growing\nneed for transdisciplinary collaboration, which explicitly\ninvolves non-scientific actors, such as citizens, policymakers, and\nprofessionals from the productive and creative sector, media\nand arts.\nTo facilitate this, emphasis was placed on creating supportive\nenvironments that encourage dialogue and collaboration. The\npractical challenges associated with transdisciplinary research,\nparticularly those involving non-scientific actors, are, however, pronounced (D’Amato et al., 2025). For instance, in\nItaly, improved coordination of funding mechanisms alongside\nthis dialogue was deemed essential. Similarly, in France and\nAustria, fostering transdisciplinarity and the collaborative construction of research with diverse actors—including citizens,\nassociations, firms, and local authorities—was recognised as\nvital. Furthering this, Wales is actively engaged with international forums such as the UNESCO-Bridges Coalition, which\nserves as an exemplar for foregrounding the humanities and\nsocial sciences in developing solutions to the climate and\n\nnature crises, reinforcing the need for truly transdisciplinary\napproaches.\n\nTowards more inclusive SPPI\n\nA recurring theme emerging from the European experiences\nwas the need for more broad-based and inclusive SPPI, facilitating a just transition towards sustainability. This isn’t just\na matter of fairness; it is essential for ensuring that transition\nefforts are effective, acceptable and sustainable for all. Achieving inclusivity requires specific practices and policies designed\nto dismantle barriers to participation amongst under-represented\nindividuals, social groups, firms, sectors, and regions, while\nfostering the integration of sustainability research and entrepreneurial activities. Ultimately, the objective is to ensure\nequitable opportunities for all segments of society to engage\nwith and benefit from environmental and sustainability R&I\n(Lähteenmäki-Smith et al., 2023; Planes-Satorra & Paunov,\n2017). Collaborative co-creation and co-design SPPI processes\nexemplify this inclusive approach, involving diverse stakeholder groups in initiatives aligned with shared interests, such\nas those related to urban planning tasks (Lee et al., 2024) or\nin developing urban transformative capacity (Wolfram, 2016).\nInclusion has already been recognised as a critical dimension\nof R&I policy, contributing to greater equitability and sustainability by fostering the acceptance of policy missions and facilitating their implementation (UN, 2023). Effective policy action\nnecessitates a comprehensive understanding of its potential\nimpacts across diverse stakeholder groups, ensuring the involvement of all relevant parties throughout planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. It is crucial to acknowledge\nthat incorporating all stakeholders into the decision-making\nprocess can present challenges and risks (Bogner & Dahlke,\n2022). These include the possibility of premature assumptions\nfor consensus, the risk of dominant actors unduly influencing outcomes, and the adoption of overly simplistic solutions\nthat prove difficult to scale (Hermans et al., 2011).\nTherefore, careful consideration must be given to involving relevant and representative stakeholder groups and managing the\nprocess in a structured and systematic manner (Firus et al., 2024).\nA balanced approach is needed—one that actively seeks diverse\nvoices while remaining alert against potential pitfalls and\nensuring that outcomes remain grounded in robust evidence\nand a clear understanding of the complexities of the transition\nprocess. Fundamentally, inclusive SPPI represents a process\nof fostering trust, with the assurance that equitable solutions\naddressing the divergent impacts of the transition process will be identified, even in a context of long-term, global\nenvironmental change.\nTo further assist in achieving this, Box 1. presents practical steps towards more inclusive SPPI, grounded in the principles of relational trust building, equitable participation and\ninclusiveness. These steps summarise key lessons from our\nempirical European cases and are designed to serve as an accessible and actionable blueprint adaptable to various communication contexts, with a focus on fostering lasting, impactful\nchange towards sustainability.\n\n\nBox 1. Practical steps towards inclusive SPPI supporting\nsustainability transition\n\nInstitutionalise SPPI platforms for trust-building\nEstablish dedicated, multi-stakeholder ‘Sustainability SPPI\nForums’ at national and sub-national levels. These Forums\nshould prioritise the representation of marginalized\ncommunities and individuals directly impacted by sustainability\ntransitions. Design these forums with a deliberate focus\non fostering mutual understanding and trust, embedding\nstructured facilitation methods, participatory narrative-building\nand transparent decision-making rules to bridge divergent\nperspectives and prevent elite capture. Implement independent\nregular monitoring and evaluation of participation and equity\noutcomes to ensure inclusivity and adapt Forum structures as\nneeded.\nReframe solutions to prioritise empowerment\nOrganise collaborative ‘Problem Definition Workshops’ co-led\nby scientists, policymakers, and community representatives.\nThese workshops should explicitly examine underlying\npower dynamics and systemic inequalities that contribute to\nenvironmental and social challenges. Frame solutions not as\ntechnical fixes or top-down regulations, but as collaboratively\ndesigned interventions that address root causes and empower\ncommunities to become active agents of change.\nIncrease societal relevance through localised expertise\nDevelop a ‘Contextualization Toolkit’ for SPPI, including\nadaptable communication strategies, facilitation techniques,\nand resource allocation models. This toolkit should be designed\nto guide practitioners in tailoring SPPI tools to specific cultural,\neconomic, and political contexts while ensuring alignment\nwith EU and global objectives. Invest in localised long-term,\nindependent knowledge brokers who possess the linguistic and\ncultural competency and legitimacy within their communities to\nfacilitate effective communication and build trust within diverse\ncommunities.\nAddress cross-systemic knowledge needs\nEstablish ‘SPPI Innovation Networks’ at the intersection of\nacademic institutions, government agencies, and civil society\norganizations. These networks should be staffed by individuals\ntrained in systems thinking, facilitation, and relational leadership\n– to identify leverage points for change, build cross-sectoral\npartnerships, and foster collaborative problem-solving. Mandate\ncross-sectoral rotations for staff within these networks and\nintroduce joint training on systems thinking and relational\nleadership to break down internal silos and build a shared\nculture of collaboration.\nAddress coordination and funding needs\nCreate a ‘SPPI Coordination Fund’ designed to provide\nlong-term financial and administrative support. This fund should\nprioritize actors and networks demonstrating commitment to\nequitable participation, adaptive governance, and knowledge\nsharing. Mandate regular peer-to-peer learning sessions\nwithin the network to promote best practices and strengthen\nrelationships. and ensure outputs are openly shared to foster\ncumulative knowledge and avoid isolated experiences.\nAddress actionable knowledge needs\nEstablish ‘Community Innovation Grants’ specifically targeted\nat supporting local businesses and community organizations\nto co-develop and test sustainability solutions. Provide free\ntechnical assistance and mentorship to ensure they can\nparticipate effectively in R&I initiatives and develop clear\nfeedback loops, so community insights demonstrably influence\nresearch and innovation agendas.\n\nSummary and recommendations\nSummary of key challenges of SPPI in Europe\n\nA transition towards sustainability demands a transnational\napproach, carefully balanced with local tailoring to account for\ndiverse contexts and development pathways both across and\nwithin nations. Successfully orchestrating this transition requires\ninclusive SPPI to foster a shared understanding of priorities,\ntheir regional and national implications, and the capacity to\ntranslate generated knowledge into actionable solutions within\nspecific local settings.\nOur empirical analysis identified several key challenges impacting the effectiveness of SPPI. A primary concern was the fragility and fragmentation of information utilization chains or\nknowledge-to-action pathways, often compounded by limited\nresources and capacities. A lack of coordination also restricts\nopportunities for cross-sectoral and cross-national collaboration.\nCommunication limitations within regulatory bodies were\nobserved, and formalized dialogue and co-creation processes\noffer significant potential to improve transparency and responsiveness. Reaching and meaningfully engaging a diverse stakeholder base remains a considerable hurdle, particularly given\nthe erosion of trust, increasing societal polarization, nationalism\nand populism. Moreover, scepticism surrounding scientific\nevidence, the proliferation of misinformation, and general disengagement from environmental and sustainability concerns pose\nsignificant threats. Overcoming these requires deliberate trustbuilding and consistent, far-sighted and insipiring communication\nstrategies.\nOur bottom-up method for identifying and assessing the challenges of SPPI showed that versatile tools are already in use,\nproviding valuable opportunities to learn what works under\nwhich circumstances. However, without a systemic approach to\nlearning and adapting these practices, European environmental\nand sustainability R&I risks remaining a fragmented cacophony of different voices rather than a coordinated engine for\nsocietal transformation. Strengthening SPPI in Europe necessitates a coordinated approach encompassing enhanced interdisciplinary and inclusive cross-sectoral collaboration, refined\nfunding mechanisms, targeted capacity building, and accessible\ncommunication channels—all of which are essential to ensuring meaningful engagement between science, society, policy and\npractice.\n\nRecommendations for strengthening the methods of\nSPPI\nTo overcome identified challenges and maximise the long-term\nsocietal impact of SPPI, we propose a multi-faceted approach\ntargeting policymakers, researchers, and funders. The use of\nspecific tools or methods of SPPI should be grounded on the\nfollowing general level principles:\n• \u0007Foster coordinated and endured collaboration: Policymakers should prioritize the creation and formal support\nof collaborative networks encompassing government\nagencies, academic institutions, and third-sector organizations. These networks should be explicitly designed\n\n\n\nto facilitate knowledge sharing and resource allocation,\nensuring their longevity despite policy changes.\n• \u0007Empower local stakeholders: Broad-based engagement\nand empowerment of local businesses and communities\nare vital for ensuring R&I directly addresses local needs\nand contributes to a just and sustainable transition. Targeted training and resource provision should facilitate\nmeaningful citizen participation. This requires accessible funding, sustained facilitation, involving all parties\nwith explicit inclusion of vulnerable or marginalized\ngroups from the outset.\n• \u0007Adapt to local contexts: Recognizing distinct cultural,\neconomic and political contexts, SPPI frameworks require\nadaptation to reflect local realities while retaining\nalignment with broader EU-level objectives and relevant\nglobal discourse. Guidance and toolkits should explicitly address how to navigate these tensions rather than\ndefaulting to a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach.\n• \u0007Secure dedicated SPPI funding and robust evaluation:\nCommit to multi-annual funding cycles, joint budgets\nfor research–policy–practice consortia, and independent\nevaluation that measures both scientific outputs and\nsocietal impacts. Long-term funding commitments and\nrobust evaluation methodologies are essential to ensure\nproject continuity and assess the wide-ranging societal\nimpacts of environmental and sustainability R&I. Joint\nbudgets for research consortia explicitly incorporating\ninteraction activities will ensure a consistent focus on\nSPPI outcomes. Attracting private sector involvement\nand investment on environmental and sustainability R&I\nmay alleviate financial burdens on public resources.\n• \u0007Incentivise collaborative research: Realign academic\nincentives to actively reward cross-disciplinary, collaborative, and problem-oriented research contributions. This\n\nshould include financial and societal support for collaborations with non-academic stakeholders from the\nproject’s inception. A particular emphasis should be on\ndeveloping mechanisms for small and medium sized\nenterprises related to environmental and sustainability\nR&I to participate and to test results on their own sites.\n• \u0007Build societal trust and ensure transparency: Establishing permanent structures to cultivate trust and connect\nresearch outcomes with practical application is critical.\nTransparency means not only disclosing results but also\ndisclosing interests and values. This requires targeted\ncapacity building across all stakeholder groups, underpinned by open and accessible communication and\ninteraction channels.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nThis study utilizes existing data that were originally collected\nwith verbal informed consent during interviews and workshops.\nAs the data had already been ethically approved and handled,\nformal ethical review and explicit consent for this secondary\nanalysis were not required.
