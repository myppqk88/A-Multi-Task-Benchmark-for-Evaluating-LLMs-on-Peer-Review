Encouraging self-regulation during\ninitial teacher education involves guiding future teachers to\nbecome aware of their progress and difficulties, which translates into promoting action research, collaboration in teaching\ncommunities, and ongoing professional development.\n\nStudent perception\nThe development of professional noticing requires the\ncoordination of didactic knowledge, sensitivity to diverse\nstrategies, and readiness to adapt to practice in real time.\nMathematics learning, as a form of social interaction, requires\nunderstanding not only what another person says, but also why\nand how they say it—in order to anticipate their intentions,\nreactions, or emotional state. This factor involves the teacher’s\nability to attribute mental states to students and to anticipate their\ncomprehension processes or potential difficulties. This ability\nis not merely empathetic, but deeply analytical and situated,\nrequiring the teacher to operate with complex internal representations of how their students learn. The effective activation\nof this competence—visible in teachers who “read between\nthe lines” of students’ responses—implies an identity commitment: teachers who see themselves as facilitators of thinking\ntend to develop a more attentive, patient, and diagnostic gaze\nSituational awareness and decision-making\nAnother key set of cognitive factors is the teacher’s ability\nto act with professional judgment in the complex environment\nof the mathematics classroom. It is well-documented that\nmathematical classroom discussions and debates contribute\nto learning progress, as shown in the works of Bishop (1972;\n2008), among others. However, such debates, which require\nreasoning, activate high cognitive demand conditions: the classroom is a dynamic environment, saturated with stimuli, where\nattention must be constantly distributed and updated. Hence, the\nimportance of situational awareness—the teacher’s ability to\nperceive, interpret, and anticipate what is happening in the\nclassroom in real time. This awareness is not a form of passive\nobservation; rather, it is an active process of meaning-making\nthat enables the teacher to respond with flexibility and relevance.\n\nInterdependence between teacher profile and\nidentity\n\nThe discussion presented in this document on teacher profiles\nand identities in mathematics education is structured by three\nkey elements: professional teacher knowledge, teacher identity,\nand cognitive factors involved in the development of\n“professional noticing.” This section outlines a model that\naddresses the interdependence between teacher profile and\nidentity (IPD model)\n\n\nIntegrative models\nRegarding the study and construction of integrative frameworks\nfor teacher profile (TP) and teacher identity (TI), studies such\nas Lutovac and Kaasila (2019) call for the inclusion of mixed\napproaches and emphasize the need for a model that articulates diverse theoretical and methodological perspectives.\nA pioneering model that directly integrates teacher identity\nand professional knowledge in mathematics education is that\nof Van Zoest and Bohl (2005), who developed a theoretical\nframework to study how secondary teachers learn to teach\nmathematics, simultaneously considering what they know\nand who they become as teachers. This framework combines three key constructs into an integrative model in which\nmathematics teacher identity is understood as the intersection of\ndidactic-mathematical knowledge and participation in professional\ncommunities:\n•\t\u0007Shulman’s (1986) heuristic on essential knowledge\nfor mathematics teaching (disciplinary, curricular, and\npedagogical knowledge expected of an effective\nteacher).\n•\t\u0007Wenger’s (2001) conception of learning as identity\ndevelopment through participation in communities of\npractice.\n•\t\u0007Cognitive notions that recognize teachers’ individual\nmental processes.\n\nIn their model, Van Zoest and Bohl introduce two major\ninterrelated components: the “self-in-mind” and the “self-incommunity.” The self-in-mind aspects refer to what the teacher\npossesses individually at the cognitive level: mathematical\ncontent knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, beliefs,\nattitudes, intentions, and personal commitments to teaching.\nThe self-in-community components recognize the social facet\nof teacher identity: how the teacher participates in professional\ncommunities (e.g., math departments, collaborative groups, professional networks), how they are perceived by others, how\nthey perceive others’ views of them, and their alignment with\nthe norms and values of the educational community.\nA central element of this model is that professional\ndevelopment occurs through the interaction of these aspects.\nTeachers build their identity by adopting roles and competencies within the community while simultaneously constructing\ninternal knowledge and beliefs. Van Zoest and Bohl emphasize\nthat teacher learning and knowledge exist on a continuum—from\nindividual to social—that includes all intermediate stages -see\nFigure 2, from (Sun, 2017)-. Within this framework, teacher\nlearning is understood as neither purely internal nor solely\nsocial; it is “not just an accumulation of skills and information,\nbut a process of identity development through social interactions.”\nEmbracing this idea implies recognizing that changing the way\none teaches mathematics involves both acquiring new knowledge\nand reconfiguring one’s teacher identity through interaction\n\nFigure 2. Mathematics Teacher Identity framework. From: Sun (2017).\n\n\nwith others. The model incorporates several dimensions in the\nconstruction of identity:\n•\t\u0007Mutuality of engagement, developed directly in\nthe classroom community with students or through\ninteractions with peers.\n•\t\u0007Accountability to an enterprise, reflecting adherence\nto the standards and norms of an institution, often\nenforced through internal evaluations or expectations.\n•\t\u0007Negotiability of a repertoire, involving participation\nin and co-ownership of the community’s shared\npractices, tools, and knowledge developed over time to\nachieve its goals.\nOne implication of this model is that a strong teacher identity tends to be associated with greater content and pedagogical\nknowledge, as well as more active participation in professional\ncommunities. For professional development, this framework\nsuggests that training should address both dimensions: providing\nnew knowledge and opportunities for practice, while also\nsupporting teachers in reconstructing their identity—including\nbeliefs, confidence, and sense of professional belonging. Thus,\nthe Van Zoest and Bohl model offers a holistic conceptual\nframework for studying the development of mathematics\nteachers by integrating the cognitive and social dimensions of\ntheir growth.\n\nFrom conceptual analysis to model construction\nThe previous sections have examined a range of theoretical\nframeworks related to teacher knowledge and identity in\nmathematics education. While these models offer valuable\ninsights, they tend to treat teacher profile and identity as distinct\ndomains, often resulting in parallel yet disconnected analyses.\nThis separation limits our ability to capture the full complexity\nof teacher development as a dynamic and situated process. To\naddress this, we move beyond the juxtaposition of conceptual\nmodels and propose an integrated framework: the Interdependent\nProfile-Identity (IPD) model.\nTo support the model’s design, a comparative synthesis\nof key frameworks was conducted, focusing on how each\n\nconceptualization addresses three fundamental dimensions:\n(a) the epistemic dimension (knowledge and cognition),\n(b) the relational dimension (self-perception and positioning),\nand (c) the contextual dimension (institutional and sociocultural influences). Table 1 summarizes this synthesis, highlighting\nconvergences and gaps among selected models.\nThis comparative analysis reveals an asymmetry: while some\nmodels (e.g., MTSK, MKT) provide robust accounts of\nprofessional knowledge, they neglect how this knowledge connects to the teacher’s sense of self or role within a community.\nConversely, identity-oriented frameworks (e.g., Wenger; Sfard\n& Prusak) focus on narrative, social, or relational aspects of\nteacher identity but lack an operationalization of professional\nknowledge structures.\nThe design of the IPD model thus emerges from the need to\nreconcile these dimensions. Its architecture is organized into\nthree interrelated subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Knowledge and Beliefs (Epistemic Core): comprising\nboth content-specific knowledge and pedagogical\nreasoning, informed by MTSK and enriched with\nnotions such as professional noticing and reflective judgment.\n•\t\u0007Identity Configuration (Relational Layer): incorporating narrative positioning, perceived agency, and\nemotional self-understanding, based on identity theory\nand discourse-based models.\n•\t\u0007Situated Membership (Contextual Interface): including institutional norms, community participation,\nrecognition processes, and social expectations, grounded\nin practice-based and sociocultural theories.\nThese subdomains are not conceived as separate layers but as\ninteracting spheres that co-construct the teacher’s professional\ndevelopment over time. Their interdependence supports the\nhypothesis that changes in one dimension (e.g., identity\nreconfiguration) will affect the others (e.g., uptake of professional\nknowledge or pedagogical stance).\n\nTable 1. Their study demonstrates that\nidentity construction processes do not occur in isolation, but\nrather emerge from a complex dialectic among personal,\nprofessional, academic, and disciplinary factors. In this view,\nmathematics teacher professional identity (IPD) is conceived\nas a dynamic construction that integrates family and social\nbackgrounds, academic trajectories, the influence of previous\nteachers, and—critically—the teacher’s relationship with mathematics and mathematics education as fields of knowledge.\nBezerra and colleagues conclude that IPD cannot be analyzed\nas a closed category or merely as a self-image but is deeply\nintertwined with the practices and knowledge that comprise the\nteacher’s profile.\nA framework aiming to recognize IPD interdependence must\nuphold the central thesis that the professional teacher profile\ncannot be defined exclusively by observable competencies or\nfunctional traits but must instead be understood as the outward\nprojection of an identity process in constant evolution. Likewise, teacher identity is shaped and redefined in response to\nthe profile the teacher expects to assume—or is expected (or\npressured) to assume—by the institution. Based on the sources\ndiscussed, an interdependent IPD model can be synthesized\naround the following categories:\n•\t\u0007The distinctive elements of the mathematics teacher’s professional profile, particularly those identified\nwithin the MTSK framework (Carrillo-Yañez et al.,\n2018).\n•\t\u0007The development of teacher identity as a situated\nprocess (Hodgen, 2011).\n•\t\u0007The cognitive aspects, especially those related to\nthe development of professional noticing (Jacobs\net al., 2010).\nThe construction of this model assumes that teacher identity is the product of situated practice. Studies such as those by\nChávez and Llinares (2012) indicate that teachers negotiate their\nidentity through daily classroom participation. These authors\noffer empirical support for the sociocultural claim that identity\nemerges from engagement in social practices—professional\npractice thus acts as both the setting and catalyst for identity\ndevelopment.\nAdditionally, the development of professional noticing is\ninherently interdependent, grounded in cognitive elements and\nconcrete aspects such as reflective capacity and student perception. In this regard, research presented at SEIEM, such as that of\n(Contreras et al., 2011), shows that preservice teachers, when\nreflecting on their past experiences as students and comparing\nthem with their current training, identify tensions and continuities that shape their identity, for example, rejecting traditional\n\nrote methods and aspiring to become more dynamic teachers.\nThese and other contributions add an important nuance to the\nsociocultural approach: while identity is socially constructed,\nself-reflection is the tool through which individuals assimilate and internalize social influences, giving coherence to their\nown professional biography. Consequently, the proposed model\n(see Figure 3) considers a broad set of cross-connections and\ninterdependencies.\n\nStructure of the model\nThe interdependent IPD model proposed here is organized\naround three main pillars: the aspects of the “self-in-mind,”\nthe “self-in-community,” and the mixed elements that mediate\nbetween them. This structure revisits and updates the theoretical\nframework of Van Zoest and Bohl (2005), who conceptualized\nteacher identity as the result of the interaction between\nindividual cognition and community participation. Building on\nthat foundation, categories from the MTSK model are integrated\nto characterize the teacher’s professional profile, and the construct of “professional noticing” is incorporated as a key axis to\nexamine the connection between practice and identity.\nThe professional component (teacher profile) of the model\nemploys the categories from the MTSK framework and its\ndevelopmental philosophy to distinguish two internal domains:\nthe “self-in-mind” and the “self-in-community.” It is composed\nof the following subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Self-efficacy (SE): defined as the teacher’s perception\nof their own ability to teach and their mastery of the\nprofessional knowledge and skills they believe they\npossess. It is thus a judgment about one’s own capacity.\n•\t\u0007Self-improvement (SI): reflects the individual’s commitment to continuous improvement, training and\nlifelong learning. It indicates the extent to which one\nadopts a growth mindset that enables Professional\nAgency.\n•\t\u0007Professional Agency (PA): this subdomain reflects\nthe extent to which the teacher feels responsible and\nautonomous in transforming their own practice. It\nrefers to the will, decision-making, and responsibility\nto act upon oneself (and one’s professional environment).\nWhile the concepts of self-efficacy and self-training are\nwell-established in mathematics education—and are included\nin validated questionnaires for characterizing teacher identity,\nsuch as the MTEBI (Enochs et al., 2000; Segarra & Julià,\n2022)—Professional Agency is defined in the literature\n(Gardee, 2019) as the teacher’s capacity and willingness to\nimplement transformative agency in the classroom. Such\ntransformative practice affects both the students’ mathematical\nidentity and the teacher’s own.\nMeanwhile, the aspects of situated identity are considered\nwithin the “self-in-community” component, which encompasses the social, contextual, and biographical factors that shape\n\n\nFigure 3. IPD framework.\n\nteacher identity. These include participation in educational\ncommunities, the teacher’s self-perception, and how they are\nperceived by others. This dimension recovers the notion of\nidentity as a situated and negotiated process built through\ninteraction (Hodgen, 2011), in which shared practices and\ndiscourses shape not only what the teacher does, but who\nthey are as a professional. It incorporates key elements in identity construction—educational background, school experiences,\nshared values, professional networks—as factors directly influencing identity formation. Thus, the “self-in-community” domain\nrepresents the relational, situated, and socially constructed\ndimension of professional identity. It includes three subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Negotiability of the Common Regime (NCR): refers\nto the teacher’s ability to interpret, question, appropriate, and modify—explicitly or implicitly—the\nnorms, practices, and shared meanings that shape their\nprofessional community.\n•\t\u0007Relational Recognition (RR): refers to the extent to\nwhich the teacher is recognized, valued, and legitimized\nby other members of the professional community\n(peers, students, families, institution) as an active,\ncompetent, and authorized participant. This recognition not only contributes to the sense of belonging but\ndirectly influences identity formation.\n\n•\t\u0007Accountability to an Organization (AO): refers to\nthe way in which the teacher assumes responsibility to\nexternal entities that regulate, assess, or supervise their\nprofessional practice. This subdomain encompasses\nboth formal obligations to comply with curricular\nstandards, evaluation policies, or institutional norms,\nand the ethical and professional disposition to respond\nto these expectations reflectively and critically.\nThe RR subdomain complements NCR and AO by introducing\nthe intersubjective dimension—how identity is also constructed\nexternally. It connects to the emotional component of recognition,\nas feeling valued by others reinforces one’s commitment to the\ncommunity. Moreover, RR has clear practical implications:\na teacher who does not perceive themselves as recognized or\nincluded tends to withdraw, reduce participation, or abandon\nprofessional leadership.\n\nInner dialogue and possibilities\nThe IPD model presents a robust and well-balanced conceptual\narchitecture, filled with internal connections worth highlighting.\nIn this regard, expectations form the central organizing element\nof the model’s “identity” dimension. Teachers’ expectations\nguide their judgments of self-efficacy (SE), their decision to\npursue professional learning (SI), and their willingness to act\n\n\n(PA). Simultaneously, external expectations shape the context\nin which the teacher is held accountable (AO), seeks recognition (RR), and negotiates meanings (NCR). The model thus\nrecognizes how teachers accept, reject, or reinterpret these\nexpectations—and the impact this has on their identity. By placing expectations at the core of the identity dimension—acting as\na sort of “semi-permeable membrane” between the “self-in-mind”\nand the “self-in-community” domains—the model allows for\nthe integration or filtering of social mandates in accordance\nwith the teacher’s values, knowledge, and aspirations.\nHowever, the model’s interconnections extend beyond\nthis, as every potential crossover within the “identity” dimension\ncan be explored. While not all intersections will be discussed\nhere, a few are worth noting:\n•\t\u0007SI–AO: The commitment to improvement may be\ndriven by external demands that the teacher transforms\ninto personal goals. Lifelong learning thus becomes an\nethical response to institutional and social demands for\nprofessional quality.\n•\t\u0007SE–RR: Perceived personal competence is reinforced\nor undermined by the recognition received from\nothers. An environment that validates a teacher’s\nknowledge and actions enhances self-efficacy; one that\ndiscredits them may erode it—even if the teacher has\nsolid training.\n•\t\u0007PA–NCR: Agency is externally manifested in the\nability to influence collective norms. A teacher\nexercising transformative agency tends to actively\nparticipate in the construction and renegotiation of the\nshared repertoire.\nAdditional interconnections can also be explored within\neach domain. In the “self-in-mind” domain, for instance, the\nfollowing relationships can be observed:\n•\t\u0007SE–SI: A strong self-efficacy judgment promotes a\ncommitment to lifelong learning by reinforcing the\nbelief that training efforts will yield results.\nConversely, commitment to professional development\nmay enhance self-efficacy by providing new tools,\nstrategies, and successful experiences.\n•\t\u0007SI–PA: Self-training is a key enabler of agency.\nTeachers who take responsibility for their own growth\nare better positioned to act autonomously, make\ntransformative decisions, and innovate in their practice.\n•\t\u0007SE–PA: Agency is mediated by perceptions of\ncompetence: a teacher who doubts their knowledge\nor skills is unlikely to act agentively. At the same\ntime, acting successfully reinforces self-efficacy in a\nvirtuous cycle.\nBeyond these internal mechanisms, the model offers several\npractical possibilities for analysis. One such use is identifying\nidentity tensions that arise when subdomains from different\n\nareas are misaligned or in conflict. For example, a teacher with\nstrong professional agency (PA) and commitment to learning (SI) may feel frustrated or alienated if their professional\nenvironment limits participation in renegotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) or fails to provide adequate relational\nrecognition (RR). This tension between self-image and contextual\nconditions may lead to identity redefinition, withdrawal, or active\nresistance, depending on how internal and social-professional\nexpectations are managed.\nSecondly, the model enables descriptions of identity development\nprocesses through the progressive strengthening of subdomains.\nFor instance, the trajectory from emerging self-efficacy to\nconsolidated professional agency could be understood as a\npath of identity maturation—one involving not just increased\nperceived competence but also deeper transformative engagement.\nLikewise, a teacher’s gradual integration into the shared\nrepertoire—from passive compliance to active participation—can\nbe seen as a process of professional community appropriation.\nFinally, the model provides a tool for mapping diverse identity\ntrajectories, understood as specific combinations of subdomains\nthat shape distinct identity profiles. For example, a teacher\nwith high relational recognition (RR) but low professional\nagency (PA) may adopt a more conformist identity, while one with\nstrong self-training (SI) and openness to negotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) may embody an innovative, transformative\nidentity. These trajectories are not fixed, but subject to\nchange—reinforcing the idea of identity as a situated and\ncontinuously evolving process.\n\nConclusions\n\nThis paper has explored existing literature with the aim of\ninvestigating and synthesizing the co-determination between the\nteacher profile and teacher identity in mathematics education,\nin order to integrate both within a common framework that\nbetter captures the complexity of teaching practice and promotes\nprofessional growth in a comprehensive manner. In this regard,\nthe paper discusses the transformation that the characterization of the teacher profile has undergone in recent decades:\nfrom early work focused on content knowledge and pedagogy\n(Carrillo-Yañez et al., 2018; Shulman, 1986) to more integrative approaches that simultaneously address cognitive, affective,\ncontextual, and social dimensions (Blömeke et al., 2020;\nLutovac & Kaasila, 2019). These studies are framed within an\ninternational research landscape that has also examined the\nrelationship between teacher profiles and other elements such\nas professional identity (Beijaard et al., 2004), instructional\nquality, assessment (Mandujano et al., 2025), and learning\nopportunities. However, a persistent question in the literature—\nwhich this paper seeks to address—remains: how can cognitive,\nprofessional, identity, and discursive dimensions of the teacher\nbe articulated within a single model?\nDrawing on a review of existing theoretical frameworks, this\npaper proposes a model that seeks to overcome the dichotomy\nbetween professional profile and identity, integrating them\nwithin a unified analytic matrix. This matrix aims to describe\n\n\nmathematics teachers’ training trajectories, understand their\nprofessional decisions and career paths, and most importantly,\ndesign teacher education programs that take into account not\nonly observable aspects of teaching performance, but also the\nsubjective and relational components that shape what it means to\nbe a teacher.\nThis perspective is especially relevant in contexts where\ntensions persist between disciplinary training and pedagogical\npreparation, and where professional recognition of university\nteaching has yet to be systematically linked to reflection on\nidentity. In this sense, the model proposed here is not merely a\ntheoretical framework, but also a tool to rethink teacher education\nprograms and quality assessment systems, aligned with a more\nhumanized, situated, and transformative vision of mathematics\neducation. The framework presented contributes to ongoing\ndiscussions about the complex interrelationship between identity and teacher profile, acknowledging the inherent limitations\nof constructs that are, by nature, constantly evolving. Thus, the\nmodel is intended to be more structural than merely descriptive,\nin order to facilitate its operationalization in training contexts.\nThe model presented in this article responds to a critical gap:\nit proposes a conceptual structure in which teacher profile\nand professional identity are not parallel dimensions, but mutually constitutive. In contrast to existing models that tend to\nanalyze “knowing how to teach” and “being a teacher” separately,\nthe interdependent proposal put forth here builds on the MTSK\nframework and expands it to show how professional knowledge\ninteracts with the construction of identity meanings—and how\nparticular identity configurations influence the appropriation\nand development of such knowledge.\nTaken as a whole, the model not only allows for the analysis of\nteacher identity as a structural construct, but also as a dynamic\n\nfield of possibilities, shaped by tensions, interactions, and shifts\nthat define the professional development of mathematics\nteachers.\nOn the other hand, while the IPD model offers a comprehensive\nframework for understanding teacher development, it is not\nwithout limitations. Its emphasis on reflective practice, identity\nnegotiation, and situated knowledge may render it less applicable in highly prescriptive educational systems where teacher\nautonomy is limited and professional roles are tightly standardized. In such contexts—often characterized by rigid curricula,\ncentralized accountability, or limited opportunities for teacher\nagency—the dynamic interplay between identity and knowledge\nmay be constrained, reducing the explanatory power of the\nmodel. Furthermore, the model assumes access to collaborative\nand dialogic professional environments, which may not be present\nin all institutional settings.\nFinally, it is worth noting that among the many challenges\nidentified, one of the most salient is the coexistence of\nmultiple profiles within the same teaching staff. This difficulty\nalso presents an opportunity to explore the unique potential\nand limitations of each profile (Giadas et al., 2024) and implies\nrecognizing that there is no single model of what it means to be\na teacher, but rather multiple ways of being one, each associated\nwith distinct professional identities.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\n\nData availability\n\nNo data are associated with this article.\n\nReferences\nAguilar-González A, Muñoz-Catalán C, Carrillo-Yáñez J, et al.: ¿Cómo establecer\nrelaciones entre conocimiento especializado y concepciones del\nprofesorado de matemáticas? This\nimplies that professional identity is constructed and reconstructed\nthroughout a teacher’s life, integrating both personal and\ncontextual experiences from teaching practice (Naranjo Crespo,\n2018). This section aims to reflect on and analyze the various\napproaches developed in this area.\n\nTeacher identity as a situated process\nIn the field of mathematics education, research has examined\nteacher professional identity using international theoretical\nframeworks adapted to local contexts. A widely used approach\nis the situated learning perspective, derived from the work\nof Lave and Wenger (1991). From this viewpoint, professional\nknowledge is not seen as an abstract entity possessed by the\nteacher, but rather as something constructed and expressed in social\npractice and intrinsically tied to the teacher’s identity. (Wenger,\n1998) notably argued that learning and identity are two\nsides of the same coin, stating that learning “is not simply\nabout acquiring skills and information, but about becoming a\ndifferent person.” In their original work, Lave and Wenger\n(1991) proposed that professional development occurs through\nparticipation in communities of practice, where peripheral\nparticipants gradually assume more central roles. In this process,\nthese peripheral individuals forge their identity as competent\nmembers of the group. Applied to mathematics education, this\nmeans that one learns to become a mathematics teacher not\nmerely by acquiring knowledge, but by participating in the\npractices, discourses, and values shared by the mathematics\nteaching community.\nIn mathematics education specifically, Hodgen (2011) proposed\na “situated theory of mathematical knowledge for teaching,”\narguing that teacher knowledge is inherently situated in\n\n\n\npractice and inseparably linked to identity as a participant in\nthose practices. In this approach, concepts such as the teacher’s\nprofessional narrative or classroom discourse participation\nbecome as relevant as declarative knowledge, as they reflect how\nthe teacher sees themselves (identity) enacting their professional\nknowledge in real contexts. More recently, mathematics education research has used the situated perspective to interpret\nteacher development. For example, Graven and Lerman (2014),\nin a study with in-service mathematics teachers, emphasized the\ncentral importance of “confidence” in identity construction. Their\nfindings show that as teachers gained mastery of new practices\nand content, their confidence increased, and their self-image\nevolved toward that of educators. This increase in confidence\nwas interpreted as an essential component in the construction of\nemerging professional identity, intimately linked to learning\nachieved in community.\nMoreover, variables such as experience and level of training\nshape opportunities for developing specific identities. A recent\nstudy on mathematics education assessment identified two\ndistinct teacher profiles based on the frequency with which\nthey implement different evaluative practices, revealing that\nfactors such as years of experience, participation in continuing\neducation, and school context—although with a small effect—\ndifferentiate between the profiles Mandujano et al. (2025).\nThis suggests that a teacher’s professional trajectory influences\nhow they conceptualize and enact their practice, reflecting distinct\nidentity stances toward assessment.\nIt may be concluded that these sociocultural approaches are\nuseful for studying how teachers negotiate their professional\nidentity when adopting pedagogical innovations, or how peer\nlearning communities function as spaces for knowledge construction and belonging. Available evidence shows that when\nmathematics teachers engage in reflective communities (such\nas lesson study groups, study collectives, or professional\nnetworks), processes of co-construction of knowledge and identity take place: teachers assimilate new didactic-mathematical\nknowledge while also gradually adopting the values, discourse\nstyles, and attitudes of the community—in short, they develop\ntheir identity as “good mathematics teachers” within that group.\nTheorists like Wenger describe this process by stating that identity “emerges from personal knowledge and its alignment\nthrough participation in the community.”\n\nProcesses of constructing teacher profile and identity\nStudies such as those by Bezerra De Melo et al. (2024) show\nthat educators of future mathematics teachers—since they are\nagents of professional socialization—have a direct impact on\nthe identity construction of their students. This influence is\nexerted explicitly through the discourse and practice of teacher\neducators, and also implicitly via what Ronca (2007), as cited\nin Bezerra De Melo et al. (2024), refers to as “internalized\nmodels.” Thus, the future mathematics teacher’s identity\nconstruction is also conditioned by the identity of their instructor.\nHowever, the construction of TP and TI does not conclude\nwith initial teacher training; it continues throughout the\n\nprofessional career. In Spain, institutions and professional networks offer courses, conferences, and working groups to update\nknowledge and share best practices. Nevertheless, structural\nobstacles persist (Piñero, 2020), as—despite the widespread\ndiscourse emphasizing the importance of innovation and teaching research—real conditions for engaging in such activities in daily school life are lacking, leaving ongoing training\nas an almost exclusively personal responsibility. Moreover,\nstudies like those by Zapatera Llinares and Callejo de la Vega\n(2018), conclude that mastery of mathematics is “mandatory but\nnot enough” for developing an adequate “professional noticing” of student learning. In other words, teacher identity in\nmathematics is configured through the integration of mathematical knowledge, didactic expertise, and competencies related to\nnoticing or interpreting students’ understanding. This reflects\nthe traditional tension between disciplinary and pedagogical\ntraining, while also introducing a third essential factor.\nSummarizing, mathematics teacher professional identity is\ncurrently understood as a situated, dynamic, and continuously\nevolving process, shaped by personal, social, and professional\nexperiences. Indeed, research in mathematics education\n(Contreras Parraguezet al., 2011) emphasizes that teachers\nbuild their identity in response to questions like “Who am I as\na teacher at this moment, and who do I want to become?”—\nredefining their role as they gain experience. These studies\naffirm that while mathematical mastery is essential, it is not\nsufficient for developing a professional perspective that is\nattentive to student learning.\n\nCognitive factors\n\nA model that aims to recognize interdependence must assume\nthat certain cognitive factors of the teacher profile (knowledge,\nbeliefs, reasoning skills, etc.) are closely linked to how the\nteacher perceives themselves in their professional role (identity).\nThis section presents and analyzes the main cognitive factors to\nbe considered in the model.\n\nBeliefs about mathematics and its teaching/learning\nBeliefs about mathematics, its teaching, and its learning constitute interpretive frameworks that guide practice (sometimes\neven stronger than formal knowledge). These beliefs act as filters\nthrough which teachers interpret classroom situations and define\ntheir role (Azcárate Goded, 2001). For instance, a teacher\nwho believes that “mathematics is only for a few” will tend to\ngenerate selective practices, while one who believes that all\nstudents can learn will be more inclined to adapt teaching to\nstudent diversity. In this regard, two clear categories can be\nestablished—based on NCTM principles: productive and\nunproductive beliefs (Leinwand et al., 2014).\nBeliefs about mathematics and its teaching/learning\nProfessional self-regulation refers to a teacher’s ability to reflect\non their own practice, identify areas for improvement, and\ntake formative actions toward continuous professional growth.\nIt encompasses metacognitive skills such as self-assessment,\nplanning for improvement, and managing emotions and motivations\nin teaching. In building professional identity, self-regulation\n\n\nplays a key role: teachers who see themselves as “lifelong\nlearners” adopt an evolving identity, open to change and\ndevelopment. Literature highlights the importance of reflection and inquiry into one’s own practice as drivers of identity\ndevelopment. Beijaard et al. (2004) for example, emphasize that\nreflection on teaching experiences and self-assessment as a\nprofessional are factors that strengthen teacher identity by\narticulating theory with practice and aligning actions with personal beliefs and values. Encouraging self-regulation during\ninitial teacher education involves guiding future teachers to\nbecome aware of their progress and difficulties, which translates into promoting action research, collaboration in teaching\ncommunities, and ongoing professional development.\n\nStudent perception\nThe development of professional noticing requires the\ncoordination of didactic knowledge, sensitivity to diverse\nstrategies, and readiness to adapt to practice in real time.\nMathematics learning, as a form of social interaction, requires\nunderstanding not only what another person says, but also why\nand how they say it—in order to anticipate their intentions,\nreactions, or emotional state. This factor involves the teacher’s\nability to attribute mental states to students and to anticipate their\ncomprehension processes or potential difficulties. This ability\nis not merely empathetic, but deeply analytical and situated,\nrequiring the teacher to operate with complex internal representations of how their students learn. The effective activation\nof this competence—visible in teachers who “read between the\nlines” of students’ responses—can be interpreted as involving\nan identity commitment, in the sense that teachers who position\nthemselves as facilitators of students’ thinking may be more\nlikely to cultivate patient, and diagnostic gaze\nSituational awareness and decision-making\nAnother key set of cognitive factors is the teacher’s ability\nto act with professional judgment in the complex environment\nof the mathematics classroom. It is well-documented that\nmathematical classroom discussions and debates contribute\nto learning progress, as shown in the works of Bishop (1972;\n2008), among others. However, such debates, which require\nreasoning, activate high cognitive demand conditions: the classroom is a dynamic environment, saturated with stimuli, where\nattention must be constantly distributed and updated. Hence, the\nimportance of situational awareness—the teacher’s ability to\nperceive, interpret, and anticipate what is happening in the\nclassroom in real time. This awareness is not a form of passive\nobservation; rather, it is an active process of meaning-making\nthat enables the teacher to respond with flexibility and relevance.\n\nInterdependence between teacher profile and\nidentity\n\nThe discussion presented in this document on teacher profiles\nand identities in mathematics education is structured by three\nkey elements: professional teacher knowledge, teacher identity,\nand cognitive factors involved in the development of\n“professional noticing.” This section outlines a model that\naddresses the interdependence between the mathematics’ teacher\n\nspecialized knowledge and identity (Professional Identity and\nKnowledge -PIK- model)\n\nIntegrative models\nRegarding the study and construction of integrative frameworks\nfor teacher profile (TP) and teacher identity (TI), studies such\nas Lutovac and Kaasila (2019) call for the inclusion of mixed\napproaches and emphasize the need for a model that articulates diverse theoretical and methodological perspectives.\nA pioneering model that directly integrates teacher identity\nand professional knowledge in mathematics education is that\nof Van Zoest and Bohl (2005), who developed a theoretical\nframework to study how secondary teachers learn to teach\nmathematics, simultaneously considering what they know\nand who they become as teachers. This framework combines three key constructs into an integrative model in which\nmathematics teacher identity is understood as the intersection of\ndidactic-mathematical knowledge and participation in professional\ncommunities:\n•\t\u0007Shulman’s (1986) heuristic on essential knowledge\nfor mathematics teaching (disciplinary, curricular, and\npedagogical knowledge expected of an effective\nteacher).\n•\t\u0007Wenger’s (2001) conception of learning as identity\ndevelopment through participation in communities of\npractice.\n•\t\u0007Cognitive notions that recognize teachers’ individual\nmental processes.\nIn their model, Van Zoest and Bohl introduce two major\ninterrelated components: the “self-in-mind” and the “self-incommunity.” The self-in-mind aspects refer to what the teacher\npossesses individually at the cognitive level: mathematical\ncontent knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, beliefs,\nattitudes, intentions, and personal commitments to teaching.\nThe self-in-community components recognize the social facet\nof teacher identity: how the teacher participates in professional\ncommunities (e.g., math departments, collaborative groups, professional networks), how they are perceived by others, how\nthey perceive others’ views of them, and their alignment with\nthe norms and values of the educational community.\nA central element of this model is that professional\ndevelopment occurs through the interaction of these aspects.\nTeachers build their identity by adopting roles and competencies within the community while simultaneously constructing\ninternal knowledge and beliefs. Van Zoest and Bohl emphasize\nthat teacher learning and knowledge exist on a continuum—from\nindividual to social—that includes all intermediate stages -see\nFigure 2, from (Sun, 2017)-. Within this framework, teacher\nlearning is understood as neither purely internal nor solely\nsocial; it is “not just an accumulation of skills and information,\nbut a process of identity development through social interactions.” Embracing this idea implies recognizing that changing\nthe way one teaches mathematics involves both acquiring new\n\n\n\nFigure 2. The model incorporates several dimensions\nin the construction of identity:\n•\t\u0007Mutuality of engagement, developed directly in\nthe classroom community with students or through\ninteractions with peers.\n•\t\u0007Accountability to an enterprise, reflecting adherence\nto the standards and norms of an institution, often\nenforced through internal evaluations or expectations.\n•\t\u0007Negotiability of a repertoire, involving participation\nin and co-ownership of the community’s shared\npractices, tools, and knowledge developed over time to\nachieve its goals.\nOne implication of this model is that a strong teacher identity tends to be associated with greater content and pedagogical\nknowledge, as well as more active participation in professional\ncommunities. For professional development, this framework\nsuggests that training should address both dimensions: providing\nnew knowledge and opportunities for practice, while also\nsupporting teachers in reconstructing their identity—including\nbeliefs, confidence, and sense of professional belonging. Thus,\nthe Van Zoest and Bohl model offers a holistic conceptual\nframework for studying the development of mathematics\n\nteachers by integrating the cognitive and social dimensions of\ntheir growth.\n\nFrom conceptual analysis to model construction\nThe previous sections have examined a range of theoretical\nframeworks related to teacher knowledge and identity in\nmathematics education. While these models offer valuable\ninsights, they tend to treat teacher profile and identity as distinct\ndomains, often resulting in parallel yet disconnected analyses.\nThis separation limits our ability to capture the full complexity\nof teacher development as a dynamic and situated process. The next\nsection therefore moves from conceptual comparison to model\nconstruction by making these couplings explicit in the IPD\nframework.\n\nRecent findings, such as those published by Bezerra De Melo\net al. (2024), support the need to adopt an interdependent\napproach to analyzing TP and TI, particularly within\nmathematics teacher education. Their study demonstrates that\nidentity construction processes do not occur in isolation, but\nrather emerge from a complex dialectic among personal,\nprofessional, academic, and disciplinary factors. In this view,\nmathematics teacher professional identity (PI) is conceived\nas a dynamic construction that integrates family and social\nbackgrounds, academic trajectories, the influence of previous\nteachers, and—critically—the teacher’s relationship with mathematics and mathematics education as fields of knowledge.\nBezerra and colleagues conclude that PI cannot be analyzed\nas a closed category or merely as a self-image but is deeply\nintertwined with the practices and knowledge that comprise the\nteacher’s profile.\nA framework aiming to recognize professional identity (PI)\nand professional knowledge (PK) interdependence must\nuphold the central thesis that the professional teacher profile\ncannot be defined exclusively by observable competencies or\nfunctional traits but must instead be understood as the outward\nprojection of an identity process in constant evolution. Likewise, teacher identity is shaped and redefined in response to\nthe profile the teacher expects to assume—or is expected (or\npressured) to assume—by the institution. Based on the sources\ndiscussed, an interdependent PIK model can be synthesized\naround the following categories:\n•\t\u0007The distinctive elements of the mathematics teacher’s professional profile, particularly those identified\nwithin the MTSK framework (Carrillo-Yañez et al.,\n2018).\n•\t\u0007The development of teacher identity as a situated\nprocess (Hodgen, 2011).\n•\t\u0007The cognitive aspects, especially those related to\nthe development of professional noticing (Jacobs\net al., 2010).\nThe construction of this model assumes that teacher identity is the product of situated practice. Studies such as those\nby Chávez and Llinares Ciscar (2012) indicate that teachers\nnegotiate their identity through daily classroom participation.\n\n\nThese authors offer empirical support for the sociocultural\nclaim that identity emerges from engagement in social practices—\nprofessional practice thus acts as both the setting and catalyst\nfor identity development.\nAdditionally, the development of professional noticing is\ninherently interdependent, grounded in cognitive elements and\nconcrete aspects such as reflective capacity and student perception. In this regard, research presented at SEIEM, such as that\nof (Contreras Parraguez et al., 2011), shows that preservice\nteachers, when reflecting on their past experiences as students and comparing them with their current training, identify\ntensions and continuities that shape their identity, for example, rejecting traditional rote methods and aspiring to become\nmore dynamic teachers. These and other contributions add an\nimportant nuance to the sociocultural approach: while identity\nis socially constructed, self-reflection is the tool through which\nindividuals assimilate and internalize social influences, giving\ncoherence to their own professional biography. Consequently,\nthe proposed model (see Figure 3) considers a broad set of\ncross-connections and interdependencies.\n\nStructure of the model\nThe interdependent PIK model proposed here is organized\naround three main pillars: the aspects of the “self-in-mind,”\nthe “self-in-community,” and the mixed elements that mediate\nbetween them. This structure revisits and updates the theoretical\n\nframework of Van Zoest and Bohl (2005), who conceptualized\nteacher identity as the result of the interaction between\nindividual cognition and community participation. Building on\nthat foundation, categories from the MTSK model are integrated\nto characterize the teacher’s professional profile, and the construct of “professional noticing” is incorporated as a key axis to\nexamine the connection between practice and identity.\nThe professional component (teacher profile) of the model\nemploys the categories from the MTSK framework and its\ndevelopmental philosophy to distinguish two internal domains:\nthe “self-in-mind” and the “self-in-community.” It is composed\nof the following subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Self-efficacy (SE): defined as the teacher’s perception\nof their own ability to teach and their mastery of the\nprofessional knowledge and skills they believe they\npossess. It is thus a judgment about one’s own\ncapacity.\n•\t\u0007Self-improvement (SI): reflects the individual’s commitment to continuous improvement, training and\nlifelong learning. It indicates the extent to which one\nadopts a growth mindset that enables Professional\nAgency.\n•\t\u0007Professional Agency (PA): this subdomain reflects\nthe extent to which the teacher feels responsible and\n\nFigure 3. PIK framework.\n\n\nautonomous in transforming their own practice. It\nrefers to the will, decision-making, and responsibility\nto act upon oneself (and one’s professional environment).\nWhile the concepts of self-efficacy and self-training are\nwell-established in mathematics education—and are included\nin validated questionnaires for characterizing teacher identity,\nsuch as the MTEBI (Enochs et al., 2000; Segarra & Julià,\n2022)—Professional Agency is defined in the literature\n(Gardee, 2019) as the teacher’s capacity and willingness to\nimplement transformative agency in the classroom. Such\ntransformative practice affects both the students’ mathematical\nidentity and the teacher’s own.\nMeanwhile, the aspects of situated identity are considered\nwithin the “self-in-community” component, which encompasses the social, contextual, and biographical factors that shape\nteacher identity. These include participation in educational\ncommunities, the teacher’s self-perception, and how they are\nperceived by others. This dimension recovers the notion of\nidentity as a situated and negotiated process built through\ninteraction (Hodgen, 2011), in which shared practices and\ndiscourses shape not only what the teacher does, but who\nthey are as a professional. It incorporates key elements in identity construction—educational background, school experiences,\nshared values, professional networks—as factors directly influencing identity formation. Thus, the “self-in-community” domain\nrepresents the relational, situated, and socially constructed\ndimension of professional identity. It includes three subdomains:\n•\t\u0007Negotiability of the Common Regime (NCR): refers\nto the teacher’s ability to interpret, question, appropriate, and modify—explicitly or implicitly—the\nnorms, practices, and shared meanings that shape their\nprofessional community.\n•\t\u0007Relational Recognition (RR): refers to the extent to\nwhich the teacher is recognized, valued, and legitimized\nby other members of the professional community\n(peers, students, families, institution) as an active,\ncompetent, and authorized participant. This recognition not only contributes to the sense of belonging but\ndirectly influences identity formation.\n•\t\u0007Accountability to an Organization (AO): refers to\nthe way in which the teacher assumes responsibility to\nexternal entities that regulate, assess, or supervise their\nprofessional practice. This subdomain encompasses\nboth formal obligations to comply with curricular\nstandards, evaluation policies, or institutional norms,\nand the ethical and professional disposition to respond\nto these expectations reflectively and critically.\nThe RR subdomain complements NCR and AO by introducing\nthe intersubjective dimension—how identity is also constructed\nexternally. It connects to the emotional component of recognition,\n\nas feeling valued by others reinforces one’s commitment to the\ncommunity. Moreover, RR has clear practical implications:\na teacher who does not perceive themselves as recognized or\nincluded tends to withdraw, reduce participation, or abandon\nprofessional leadership.\n\nEpistemic status of the PIK model\nThe relational links proposed within the PIK model should be\nread as conceptual propositions derived from the comparative\nsynthesis, not as empirically established causal claims. They\nare intended to be testable hypotheses that can guide future\nempirical work (e.g., design-based studies in teacher education,\nlongitudinal identity analyses, or mixed-methods profiling),\nclarifying what kinds of covariation and mediation the\nliterature makes plausible when knowledge and identity are\ntreated as mutually constitutive.\nInner dialogue and possibilities\nThe PIK model presents a robust and well-balanced conceptual\narchitecture, filled with internal connections worth highlighting.\nIn this regard, expectations form the central organizing element\nof the model’s “identity” dimension. Teachers’ expectations\nguide their judgments of self-efficacy (SE), their decision to\npursue professional learning (SI), and their willingness to act\n(PA). Simultaneously, external expectations shape the context\nin which the teacher is held accountable (AO), seeks recognition (RR), and negotiates meanings (NCR). The model thus\nrecognizes how teachers accept, reject, or reinterpret these\nexpectations—and the impact this has on their identity. By placing expectations at the core of the identity dimension—acting as\na sort of “semi-permeable membrane” between the “self-in-mind”\nand the “self-in-community” domains—the model allows for\nthe integration or filtering of social mandates in accordance\nwith the teacher’s values, knowledge, and aspirations.\nHowever, the model’s interconnections extend beyond\nthis, as every potential crossover within the “identity” dimension\ncan be explored. While not all intersections will be discussed\nhere, a few are worth noting:\n•\t\u0007SI–AO: The commitment to improvement may be\ndriven by external demands that the teacher transforms\ninto personal goals. Lifelong learning thus becomes an\nethical response to institutional and social demands for\nprofessional quality.\n•\t\u0007SE–RR: Perceived personal competence is reinforced\nor undermined by the recognition received from\nothers. An environment that validates a teacher’s\nknowledge and actions enhances self-efficacy; one that\ndiscredits them may erode it—even if the teacher has\nsolid training.\n•\t\u0007PA–NCR: Agency is externally manifested in the\nability to influence collective norms. A teacher\nexercising transformative agency tends to actively\nparticipate in the construction and renegotiation of the\nshared repertoire.\n\n\n\nAdditional interconnections can also be explored within\neach domain. In the “self-in-mind” domain, for instance, the\nfollowing relationships can be observed:\n•\t\u0007SE–SI: A strong self-efficacy judgment promotes a\ncommitment to lifelong learning by reinforcing the\nbelief that training efforts will yield results.\nConversely, commitment to professional development\nmay enhance self-efficacy by providing new tools,\nstrategies, and successful experiences.\n•\t\u0007SI–PA: Self-training is a key enabler of agency.\nTeachers who take responsibility for their own growth\nare better positioned to act autonomously, make\ntransformative decisions, and innovate in their practice.\n•\t\u0007SE–PA: Agency is mediated by perceptions of\ncompetence: a teacher who doubts their knowledge\nor skills is unlikely to act agentively. At the same\ntime, acting successfully reinforces self-efficacy in a\nvirtuous cycle.\nBeyond these internal mechanisms, the model offers several\npractical possibilities for analysis. One such use is identifying\nidentity tensions that arise when subdomains from different\nareas are misaligned or in conflict. For example, a teacher with\nstrong professional agency (PA) and commitment to learning (SI) may feel frustrated or alienated if their professional\nenvironment limits participation in renegotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) or fails to provide adequate relational\nrecognition (RR). This tension between self-image and contextual\nconditions may lead to identity redefinition, withdrawal, or active\nresistance, depending on how internal and social-professional\nexpectations are managed.\nSecondly, the model enables descriptions of identity development\nprocesses through the progressive strengthening of subdomains.\nFor instance, the trajectory from emerging self-efficacy to\nconsolidated professional agency could be understood as a\npath of identity maturation—one involving not just increased\nperceived competence but also deeper transformative engagement.\nLikewise, a teacher’s gradual integration into the shared\nrepertoire—from passive compliance to active participation—can\nbe seen as a process of professional community appropriation.\nFinally, the model provides a tool for mapping diverse identity\ntrajectories, understood as specific combinations of subdomains\nthat shape distinct identity profiles. For example, a teacher\nwith high relational recognition (RR) but low professional\nagency (PA) may adopt a more conformist identity, while one with\nstrong self-training (SI) and openness to negotiating the shared\nrepertoire (NCR) may embody an innovative, transformative\nidentity. These trajectories are not fixed, but subject to\nchange—reinforcing the idea of identity as a situated and\ncontinuously evolving process.\n\nConclusions\n\nThis paper has explored existing literature with the aim of\ninvestigating and synthesizing the co-determination between the\nteacher profile and teacher identity in mathematics education,\n\nin order to integrate both within a common framework that\nbetter captures the complexity of teaching practice and promotes\nprofessional growth in a comprehensive manner. In this regard,\nthe paper discusses the transformation that the characterization of the teacher profile has undergone in recent decades:\nfrom early work focused on content knowledge and pedagogy\n(Carrillo-Yañez et al., 2018; Shulman, 1986) to more integrative approaches that simultaneously address cognitive, affective,\ncontextual, and social dimensions (Blömeke et al., 2020;\nLutovac & Kaasila, 2019). These studies are framed within an\ninternational research landscape that has also examined the\nrelationship between teacher profiles and other elements such\nas professional identity (Beijaard et al., 2004), instructional\nquality, assessment (Mandujano et al., 2025), and learning\nopportunities. However, a persistent question in the literature—\nwhich this paper seeks to address—remains: how can cognitive,\nprofessional, identity, and discursive dimensions of the teacher\nbe articulated within a single model?\nDrawing on a review of existing theoretical frameworks, this\npaper proposes a model that seeks to overcome the dichotomy\nbetween professional profile and identity, integrating them\nwithin a unified analytic matrix. This matrix aims to describe\nmathematics teachers’ training trajectories, understand their\nprofessional decisions and career paths, and most importantly,\ndesign teacher education programs that take into account not\nonly observable aspects of teaching performance, but also the\nsubjective and relational components that shape what it means to\nbe a teacher.\nThis perspective is especially relevant in contexts where\ntensions persist between disciplinary training and pedagogical\npreparation, and where professional recognition of university\nteaching has yet to be systematically linked to reflection on\nidentity. In this sense, the model proposed here is not merely a\ntheoretical framework, but also a tool to rethink teacher education\nprograms and quality assessment systems, aligned with a more\nhumanized, situated, and transformative vision of mathematics\neducation. The framework presented contributes to ongoing\ndiscussions about the complex interrelationship between identity and teacher profile, acknowledging the inherent limitations\nof constructs that are, by nature, constantly evolving. Thus, the\nmodel is intended to be more structural than merely descriptive, in order to facilitate its operationalization in training\ncontexts.\nThe model presented in this article responds to a critical gap:\nit proposes a conceptual structure in which teacher profile\nand professional identity are not parallel dimensions, but\nmutually constitutive. In contrast to existing models that tend\nto analyze “knowing how to teach” and “being a teacher” separately, the interdependent proposal put forth here builds on the\nMTSK framework and expands it to show how professional\nknowledge interacts with the construction of identity meanings—and how particular identity configurations influence the\nappropriation and development of such knowledge.\nTaken as a whole, the model not only allows for the analysis of\nteacher identity as a structural construct, but also as a dynamic\nfield of possibilities, shaped by tensions, interactions, and shifts\n\n\nthat define the professional development of mathematics\nteachers.\nOn the other hand, while the PIK model offers a comprehensive\nframework for understanding teacher development, it is not\nwithout limitations. Its emphasis on reflective practice, identity\nnegotiation, and situated knowledge may render it less applicable in highly prescriptive educational systems where teacher\nautonomy is limited and professional roles are tightly standardized. In such contexts—often characterized by rigid curricula,\ncentralized accountability, or limited opportunities for teacher\nagency—the dynamic interplay between identity and knowledge\nmay be constrained, reducing the explanatory power of the\nmodel. Furthermore, the model assumes access to collaborative\nand dialogic professional environments, which may not be present\nin all institutional settings.\n\nFinally, it is worth noting that among the many challenges\nidentified, one of the most salient is the coexistence of\nmultiple profiles within the same teaching staff.
