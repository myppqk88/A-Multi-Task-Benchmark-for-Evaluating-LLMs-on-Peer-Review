      "text": "Abstract\nRationality cannot be a form of intelligence or any other cognitive\nfaculty such as memory, language faculty, mathematical skill, and so\non. To establish this thesis, this paper uses a distinction from set\ntheory, namely, the distinction between “binary relators” and “binary\noperators”. Rationality acts as a binary relator in the sense that it is an\noptimization method, while cognitive ability acts as a binary operator\nin the sense that it is a transformational function. Rationality as an\noptimization method (binary relator) does not differ from the neoDarwinian notion of natural selection: either method seeks to find the\nbest choice or fit trait given the constraints. A cognitive faculty as a\ntransformational function (binary operator) simply translates inputs\ninto outputs. The distinction between rationality and cognitive\nfunctions has wide-ranging implications regarding the theory of\nevolution.\n\n\nIntroduction\nThe literature in the biological sciences generally shies away from the concept of rational choice. When the literature\nalludes to rational choice, it considers it as a cognitive faculty similar to memory, intelligence, linguistic ability,\nmathematical skill, and so on. As such, rationality is the subject of natural selection akin to these cognitive faculties.\nEven some economists, whose discipline canonizes rationality as the central pillar in explaining choice, concur and treat\nrationality as a cognitive faculty and, hence, the product of natural selection (e.g., Robson, 2001; Robson & Samuelson,\n2010).\nThis paper questions this literature. Rationality cannot be another cognitive faculty. It is rather, similar to natural\nselection, an optimization method:\nCore Thesis: Rationality radically differs from cognitive abilities such as memory, intelligence, language faculty,\nmathematical skill, and so on. Rationality is rather an optimization method of determining the best choice or fit\ntrait.\nThis core thesis stands irrespective of the many detractors and critics of rational choice theory in the social and behavioral\nsciences.1\nThis paper advances its thesis via a distinction borrowed from set theory (Rai et al., 2012). The distinction is between\n“binary relators” and “binary operators”. Rationality acts as a binary relator, i.e., as an optimization method. In contrast,\nany cognitive faculty acts as a binary operator, i.e., as a transformational function. Rationality as an optimization method\n(binary relator) does not differ from the neo-Darwinian notion of natural selection: either method defines the best choice\nor fit trait given the constraints. A cognitive faculty as a transformational function (binary operator) simply translates\ninputs into outputs.\nThe distinction between rationality and cognitive faculties has great ramifications regarding the theory of evolution.\nPrimarily, the distinction entails that rationality cannot be the subject of evolutionary change. It is rather a constant\nfeature of any organism, i.e., simply what makes an entity a living entity. This implication can be called the “Organismus\neconomicus hypothesis”. It is in contradistinction of the view that organisms do not make decisions, but rather behave\naccording to rules selected by natural selection. This view can be called the “Organismus automaton hypothesis”, which\nis at the core of neo-Darwinism (see Khalil & Marciano, 2010).\nThis paper commences with a sharp distinction between the two hypotheses via the distinction between the\noptimization method, which specifies the best outcome, and the transformation function, which translates inputs to\noutputs. Then it finds that the neo-Darwinian approach, as a result of treating rationality as a trait selected by the\noptimization method, commits a self-reference paradox, akin to the Russell paradox (Russell, 1956). This finding\nquestions the core of the neo-Darwnian approach a questioning that is usually avoided out of the specter of metaphysics.\nThis paper registers that there is no basis of such specter and, hence, we should welcome the Organismus economicus\nhypothesis.\nOptimization method contra transformation function\nThe Organismus automaton hypothesis\nThe Organismus automaton hypothesis entails the treatment of rationality as a trait, specifically as a cognitive faculty.\nIt is based on the fundamental thesis that the organism operates according to rules, where such rules are the products,\nultimately, of natural selection. Such rules are the operational function of faculties such as memory, intelligence, and\nsupposedly rationality. In this picture, the organism follows rules in the final analysis as an automaton.\nThis paper ignores sophisticated versions of the The Organismus automaton hypothesis (e.g., Godfrey-Smith,\n1996, 2009). This allows us to focus, see Figure 1, on the basic minimal schemata of the optimization structure of the\nOrganismus automaton:\n\n1\n\nThis core thesis—viz., rational choice is a method of selecting among options--is widely accepted (e.g., Becker, 1978; Gilboa, 2012;\nKhalil, 2023). However, this should not entail that rationality is an exhaustive description of all human behavior. Behavior may involve\nimagination, creativity, aspiration, or what economists recognize as “entrepreneurship” that goes beyond mechanical rules of optimization\n(see Khalil, 1997, 2010). Further, behavior involves biases that arise from failed heuristics (see Thaler & Sunstein, 2009; Kahneman, 2011;\nKhalil, 2022; Khalil & Amin, 2022). However, these issues need not undermine the rationality concept—but may in fact open opportunities to\nexpand it.\n\n\nFigure 1. The structure of the Organismus automaton.\n\nwhere E = a set of fitness ends such as the flight speed of zebra (too much flight speed weakens other abilities and too little\nundermines the survival fitness of the organism); xitj = the particular trait (t) and the instinctual behavior requiring certain\nset of goods (x) (different organisms are characterized by different combinations of t and x); TFk = the transformational\nfunction of level or type k that transforms inputs onto outputs; and OM = the optimization method that ensures that the\noptimum E (Ei*) is selected or chosen.\nIt is crucial to distinguish among three entities in the above schemata: i) the trait-as-input that is an element of the input set\non the left-hand side; ii) the trait-as-technology that acts as the binary operator of level k (TFk) between the input set and\nthe output set; iii) the optimization method (OM) that acts as binary relator.\nTo use a distinction from set theory, OM is the “binary relator” while TFk is the “binary operator” (see Rai et al., 2012).\nThe binary relator that acts as OM performs two roles. The first role works within each set, as it ranks the different outputs\nin a consistent fashion, that is, it specifies which output is better than the other while abstracting from the constraints. For\nexample, it can rank different speeds of flight without considering the constraints. The OM also ranks correspondingly the\ndifferent inputs. However, what is selected as the optimum cannot be determined without specifying the environmental\nconstraints. This highlights the second role, once the constraints are specified, the OM relates the input with the output\nsets in a certain way that shows what is the appropriate input that produces the optimum output (Ei*).\nIn contrast, the binary operator (TFk) transforms inputs into output according to k-level trait-as-technology. As such, TFk\nis neither the optimization method (OM) nor the trait-as-input (xitj) that make up the left-hand input set. TFk is merely the\ntechnical listing of outcomes that the TFk can produce from the given set of inputs.\nThe symmetry of the two hypotheses\nThe structure of the Organismus automaton hypothesis is almost identical to the structure of Organismus oeconomicus\nhypothesis. The input in the former is the population of individuals where each individual is characterized by an input set,\nwhereas each gives rise to a different end, called a phenotype. The input in the latter is the bundle of goods, whereas each\ngives origin to a different end, called utility.\nThe Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis, based on standard rational choice, also consists of the binary relator assuring\nus the production of the optimum, i.e., the employment of OM, and the binary operator that expresses the transformational\nfunction, say, at k-level, i.e., the employment of TFk. Again here, the binary relator (OM) has two roles. As for the first\nrole, the theory of rationality assures us the ends, that is, the utility values, are consistent and, correspondingly, that the\ninputs are consistently ranked. Consistency depends on many axioms, the most important are two: transitivity and\ncompleteness (see Gilboa, 2012; Khalil, 2023). As for the second role, once constraints are determined, the binary relator\nidentifies the optimum end, that is, the optimum utility, and its corresponding set of inputs.\nAs for the binary operator at k-level (TFk), there is a set of technologies, institutions, and beliefs transforming each set of\ninputs into output. As in the case of the Organismus automaton hypothesis, the binary operator merely lists the technical\naspect of what is the outcome of each set of inputs if the given TFk is employed in their transformation. It neither assures\nany internal consistency nor determines the optimum outcome.\nWhen the optimum is disturbed\nOnce an optimum is selected by nature or by choice, the optimum is stable as long as shocks disturb neither the set of\ninputs nor the set of environmental constraints. The stable decision equilibrium, not to be confused with market\n\n\nequilibrium, can be upset if at least one of the following two conditions changes: (i) a mutation of traits-as-inputs (t) or\ngoods (x) takes place; or (ii) a change in the set of environmental constraints.\nThe set of environmental constraints is implicit in the above schemata. Namely, the binary relator can only select the\noptimum end considering such set. Let us see the consequence of the change of such set of environmental constraints\n(C) from Ci to Cc. Nature should select or the individual should choose, as Figure 2 shows, a new set of inputs, xctd*,\ncorresponding to the new optimum end, E*c:\n\nFigure 2. The selection of the optimum end.\n\nHowever, nature as well as the individual may face hurdles, mainly arising from transitional cost (Marciano & Khalil,\n2012; Khalil, 2013), as it tries to adjust. Hence, there can be an adjustment period or, worse, a lock-in arrangement where\nthe status quo dominates. Natural selection or rational choice may opt for the status quo—i.e., the old end, Ei. In this case,\nnatural selection or rational choice (OM) would still select the old inputs, xitj. If nothing happens, Ei will continue forever\nas the “second best” actual outcome, where the OM is denoted as second-best (OMSB). In contrast, E*c continues to be the\nideal or “first-best”, where the OM is denoted as first-best (OMFB). However, what matters, the first-best (E*c) is\nsuboptimal while the second best (Ei) is optimal—given the transitional cost that secures the lock-in arrangement.\nThis gap between E*c (first-best) and Ei (second-best) may shed light on the controversy between the “hard” and “soft”\nneo-Darwinian approaches. For the “hard” approach (for example, Dawkins, 1989; Dennett, 1984), OM should surely\ngenerate the E*j. For the “soft” approach (for example, Gould & Lewontin, 1979), the binary relator is rather OMSB,\ngenerating the seemingly suboptimal outcome Ei. However, as Marciano and Khalil (2012) explain, Ei is actually\noptimal, if we (as we should) take into account the lock-in arrangements, such as the body-plan of the organism or the\ntechnological/institutional/legal make up of the community. E*c is optimal in the “ideal” sense, in a world that forcefully\nignores transitional cost as if we live in arrangement-free world.\nIn the given optimization problem above, the binary operator at k-level (TF) cannot be the subject of the optimization\nmethod (OM). However, what guarantees that the binary operator is fit or efficient? In the above representation, there is no\nguarantee. It is taken as a given and, hence, cannot be assessed at this level of selection or choice. It can, however, be the\nsubject of selection or choice when it is the object of rational choice or evolutionary selection. This could be the case, as\nFigure 3 demonstrates, if the binary operator is acting at a deeper level, say at k+1 level:\n\nFigure 3. The Binary Operator at k+1 Level.\n\n\n\nThe binary operator, which consists of technology, rules/instructions, or body-plans, involves a hierarchy. It can be at\nk-level or deeper such as k+1 level. We do not find such a hierarchy with respect to the binary relator (OM) specifying the\noptimum.\nWhile the elements of the input set, and corollary the output set, are different, the binary relator (OM) does not change,\nperforming the same function irrespective of the level of hierarchy of the binary operator, i.e., whether it is TFk or TFk+1.\nIn the above schemata, OM selects or chooses xiTFk* as the optimum input that corresponds to the optimum end, E*k.\nWhile the binary operator (TFk) cannot play the role of natural selection (binary relator), it is the product of natural\nselection at a deeper level, at k+1 level. However, when the binary relator functions, i.e., as OM, it must take the binary\noperator, whether TFk or TFk+1, as given. Such a limited optimization function should not be taken, à la “soft” neoDarwinians or à la critics of standard economists (see Khalil, 2013), as a shortcoming that undermines or repudiates the\nnotion of optimization per se. To wit, the raison d'être of optimization is the existence of limits taken as given. So, the\nterm “limited optimization” is pleonasm.\nThere are other nuances, which this paper ignores, of the common structure that underpins the Organismus automaton\nand Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis. What matters for this paper, the structure of either hypothesis is the same\nand can handle different kinds of complications and qualifications at secondary and tertiary approximations. What is most\nimportant in this proposed structure is that the trait-as-technology, i.e., the binary operator, functions as a transformation\nfunction that varies according to the level of the hierarchy. In contrast, the binary relator (OM) remains constant,\ni.e., irrespective of the level of hierarchy.\nThe conflation of the binary operator and the binary relator sheds another insight regarding the controversy between\n“soft” and “hard” neo-Darwinians--as well as between “soft” and “hard” neoclassical economists. This cannot be detailed\nhere. What concerns this paper is the seperation of rationality or any OM from the object of optimization. As shown next,\nsuch separation has one important ramification, rationality cannot be a trait.\nCould rationality ever be a trait?\nThe incoherence problem\nThe proposed distinction between OM, which acts as binary relator, and transformational function (TFk), which functions\nas binary operator, entails the incoherence of what can be called “Rationality-qua-Trait Thesis”.\nIt should be immediately clear that OM (the binary relator) can be either the standard rational choice or, equivalently, the\nneo-Darwinian natural selection. In contrast, TFk (the binary operator) is the given technology or cognitive faculty, such\nas intelligence, memory, and other so on. OM cannot be TFk, as OM is not about transforming inputs to output in the\ntechnical sense.\nMore importantly, rational choice or OM cannot be trait-as-input, that is, an element of the left-hand inputs set. OM\n(binary relator) cannot be a member of the set—when its function is about making sure that the elements are consistently\nranked. To treat OM as a member of itself leads to self-contradiction, i.e., incoherence.\nAs shown above, TFk—such as intelligence, memory, or other cognitive faculties—can be an element of the left-hand\ninputs set—but then the transformation is taking place at a deeper level. There is a hierarchy of technology or body-plan.\nThe transformation function (TF) cannot apply to OM, as OM cannot become a member of the left-hand inputs set. There\nis no hierarchy of rationality as the case with TF. What applies to TF cannot apply to OM. And if we conceive OM as a\nmember of the inputs set, this leads to incoherence à la Russell Paradox.\nThe Russell Paradox\nThe Russell Paradox (Russell, 1956) emerges when one conceives a set as a member of itself. To treat rational choice as a\nmember of the set that is the object of rational choice is an example of the Russell Paradox.\nOne helpful illustration of the Russell Paradox is the well-known Cretan liar paradox and, better, the Barber Paradox\n(Irvine & Deutsch, 2021). The only barber in a village has a sign posted in his shop that states, “I cut the hair of everyone in\nthe village that does not cut his own hair.” But who cuts the hair of the barber? If he cuts his own hair, then the barber\ncannot cut it. And if he does not cut his own hair, then the barber cuts it. Such a self-contradiction arises from treating the\nset, which is the barber’s statement, as a member of itself. Such treatment entails that the barber is an element of the set of\npeople who do not cut their own hair, on one hand, and the barber is the set itself as the one who cuts the hair of such\npeople, on the other.\n\n\n\nThe Russell Paradox arises from what philosophers recognize as the problem of self-reference. In the case of the\nRationality-qua-Trait Thesis, it treats rationality both as OM as well as an element of the set that is the subject of OM. This\nself-reference is best illustrated by some of the drawings of M.C. Escher (see Hofstadter, 1980). The first role of the binary\nrelator (OM) is to make sure that the output is consistently ranked and, corollary, the corresponding inputs are also\nconsistently ranked. Such an organization of inputs cannot also include OM: how could the OM calls for the substitution\nof one input in favor of another when the OM itself is one such input?\nWho’s afraid of the Organismus Oeconomicus hypothesis?\nThe implication of the above analysis is that rationality cannot be selected or chosen. Rationality cannot be a trait such as\nmusical skill, intelligence, or any cognitive faculty. As an OM, it is analytically akin to natural selection.\nIt is obvious that some species, such as Homo sapiens, behave rationally. A difficulty arises when a researcher draws a line\nbetween such species and supposedly species deemed to be non-rational. Such a line, most obviously, leads to the creation\nof a fictitious dichotomy that juxtaposes “rational species” and “non-rational species.” This dichotomy is fictitious as it\nsupposes a natural set that includes species such as bacteria and, say, lower primates as having a common trait that sets\nthem apart from another set that includes only upper primates. To avoid such fictious sets, researchers need to examine\nwhether rationality is a trait in the first place. The Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis certainly avoids such pittfalls, as it\nascribes rationality to all living entities (Khalil, 2023).\nHowever, if we treat rationality as OM, and hence, cannot be seen as a trait, this gives rise to questions about the status of\nrationality with respect to living entities, if not to questions that conjure the specter of metaphysics—the specter of\nconceiving rationality as existing separately from living entities.\nBiologists need not fear the Organismus oeconomicus hypothesis for at least five reasons.\n1. The rationality concept, at least as used by economists (e.g., Becker, 1978; Gilboa, 2012; Khalil, 2023), amounts\nto a rule or a method that does not specify any particular content—not to mention free will, imagination, creative\naction, or ambition. Rational choice means organisms generally make the best decision possible given the\nenvironmental constraints and their technological capacities that include cognitive faculties, physiological\ntraits, technologies, and institutions.\n2. Biologists and psychologists usually restrict rationality to mental or cognitive processes (e.g., Chater et al.,\n2018; Felin et al., 2017). They conclude that organisms with limited cognitive processes, and especially plants\nand other brainless organisms, cannot be rational. However, once one understands rationality properly, one\ncannot conflate it with cognitive faculties (see Khalil, 2010).\n3. Biologists (e.g., Gardner, 2009) are afraid of what philosophers call “reification”—that is, the thinking of an\nidea such as rationality and supposing it is true, i.e., choices made by organisms express rational choice. This\nraises the specter of metaphysics (see Winn, 1939)—the belief in an “uncaused cause” (Dennett, 1995). For\ninstance, the supposed reification of rationality may conjure up uncaused cause and, hence, uphold creationism,\nthat is, the idea there must be an external agent (e.g., a god) who created rationality. However, the specter of\nreification need not arise if one can trace rationality to a defining feature of being a living being. But this task is\noutside the scope of this paper.\n4. Biologists who use the foraging theory pioneered by Eric Charnov (e.g., 1976; see Stephens & Krebs, 1986) or\nwho employ the tools of “bioeconomics” (e.g., Ghiselin, 1974; see also Tullock, 1971, 1994; Khalil &\nMarciano, 2010) already use cost-benefit calculations, that is, rational choice. A few biologists have recently\ntaken the first step to recognizing openly the relevance of rationality to the understanding of behavior or\norganisms (e.g., Vermeij, 2004; Hurley & Nudds, 2006).\n5. Biologists implicitly employ the rational choice approach when they explain behavior as elastic, that is, as\nresponsive to changes in environmental constraints. They call such elasticity “phenotypic plasticity” (Gould,\n1997, 2002; Stanley, 1979; Matsuda, 1987; West-Eberhard, 1989; Raff, 1996; Müller & Newman, 2003; Hall\net al., 2004; DeWitt & Scheiner, 2004). They are making great strides in showing how even invertebrate\norganisms make decisions in light of uncertainty (e.g., Oberhauser & Czaczkes, 2018). They are documenting\nhow animals generally make decisions in reaction to the so-called “contrast effect” (e.g., McNamara et al.,\n2013).\n\n\n\nRegarding phenotypic plasticity, biologists need to recognize explicitly and openly the fact that it amounts to rational\nchoice. As shown elsewhere (Khalil, 2009, 2010), rational choice theory is simply the proposition that the organism’s\nchoice changes in light of changes in environmental constraints or, in short, incentives. With each choice, the organism\nmaximizes the benefit (fitness) given the cost or, what is the same thing, minimizes the cost given the benefit.\nConclusions\n1. There is an incongruent gap in the natural sciences. On one hand, the sciences that focus on the study of nonhuman organisms do not largely give weight to rationality. On the other hand, the sciences, especially\neconomics, that focus on the study of human organisms pay great attention to rationality.\n2. The sciences that focus on non-human organisms do not need to appeal to rationality, as they largely appeal to\nnatural selection as the optimization method. Still, they have to explain rationality if they are natural sciences,\ni.e., sciences that cannot exclude humans from nature. Such sciences, if pushed, tend to explain rationality as a\ntrait—i.e., similar to intelligence and other cognitive faculties. However, such explanation leads to the selfreference problem, a variety of incoherence à la Russell paradox: how could rationality that is an optimization\nmethod be a trait, i.e., the subject of natural selection that is an optimization method?\n3. We can easily avoid the Russell paradox regarding rational choice or any optimization method if we employ a\ndistinction from set theory, namely, the difference between “binary relators” and “binary operators”.\n4. Rationality cannot be a trait given that it is a binary relator that allows us to identify the optimum. In contrast,\ncognitive faculties are traits, as they are binary operators that transform inputs into outputs.\n5. Once we distinguish between binary relators and binary operators, we can conceive rationality as an OM (binary\nrelator) that cannot be a trait (binary operator). Such a limited optimization function should not be taken, à la\n“soft” neo-Darwinians or à la the critics of standard economists (see Khalil, 2013a), as a shortcoming that undermines or\nrepudiates the notion of optimization per se. To wit, the raison d’être of optimization is the existence of limits taken as\ngiven. Therefore, the term “limited optimization” is pleonasm.\nThere are other nuances, which this paper ignores, of the common structure that underpins the Organismus automaton\nand Organismus economicus hypothesis. Importantly, the structure of each hypothesis is the same and can handle\ndifferent kinds of complications and qualifications at secondary and tertiary approximations. Most importantly, in this\nproposed structure, the trait-as-technology, i.e., the binary operator, functions as a transformation function that varies\naccording to the level of the hierarchy. In contrast, the binary relator (OM) remains constant, i.e., irrespective of the level\nof hierarchy.\nAre binary operators and binary relators opposite of each other?\nWhile binary operators (cognitive capacity or technology) and binary relators (rational choice) are different, this does not\nmean that they are opposite of each other. While this paper argues that both binary linkages are analytically different, they\nare functionally linked. Indeed, it is impossible for the decision maker to make rational decisions—i.e., undertake binary\nrelator actions—without relying on cognitive capacities, traits, physiological organs, or transformative technologies,\ni.e., binary operators.\nFor instance, Varian (1992) commences his influential graduate-level economics textbook with the binary operator.\nHe dedicates Chapter 1 to discussing the capability of a firm—as expressed by the available technology, machines,\nbuildings, space, and workers—to transform inputs into outputs. Varian then proceeds in Chapter 2 to discuss how the\nfirm uses such capability, i.e., the binary operator, in different ways depending on the environment and other constraints.\nThe firm’s objective is the maximization of profit, which prescribes a choice that amounts to a binary relator (rationality).\nThe fact that the binary relator (rational choice) relies on a binary operator (technology or capacity) should not lead us to\nconflate the two. Rationality cannot be both a binary relator and a binary operator. As a binary relator, rationality involves\n\n\na binary operator. However, the opposite is not the case: Once a technology or a capacity (binary operator) is set up, its\noperation does not involve a binary relator (rationality).\nCan rationality ever be a trait?\nThe incoherence problem\nThe proposed distinction between the OM, which acts as a binary relator, and the transformational function (TFk), which\nfunctions as a binary operator, entails the incoherence of what can be called “Rationality-qua-Trait Thesis”.\nOM (the binary relator) can be either the standard rational choice or, equivalently, the neo-Darwinian natural selection.\nIn contrast, TFk (the binary operator) is the given technology or cognitive faculty, such as intelligence and memory.\nOM cannot be TFk, as OM is not about transforming inputs to outputs in the technical sense.\nMore importantly, rational choice or OM cannot be trait-as-input, that is, an element of the left-hand input set. The OM\n(binary relator) cannot be a member of the set—when its function is about ensuring that the elements are consistently\nranked. Treating the OM as a member of itself leads to self-contradiction, i.e., incoherence.\nAs shown above, TFk—such as intelligence, memory, or other cognitive faculties—can be an element of the left-hand\ninput set—but then the transformation takes place at a deeper level. There is a hierarchy of technology or body plans. The\ntransformation function (TF) cannot apply to the OM, as the OM cannot become a member of the left-hand input set.\nThere is no hierarchy of rationality, as in the case with TF. What applies to TF cannot apply to OM. If we conceive of the\nOM as a member of the input set, this leads to a self-reference paradox à la Russell Paradox.\nThe Russell Paradox\nIf we accept two theses, neo-Darwinian theory involves a serious self-reference paradox. The first thesis is that a standard\nsense of rationality exists. The second thesis is that such rationality is a trait. Therefore, standard rationality is a trait that\nchanges and evolves depending on the optimization mechanism, i.e., the natural selection process. However, given the\nargument above, rationality is an optimization mechanism. Thus, it would be a contradiction, i.e., a self-reference\nparadox, to state that an optimization process selects the optimization process. If this conclusion is tenable, it is akin to the\nRussell Paradox (Russell, 1956).\nTo be clear, the Russell Paradox is not a proof of the argument of this paper. That is, the Russell Paradox does not\nsubstantiate this paper’s conclusion. Specifically, once we accept the two theses mentioned above, the neo-Darwinian\napproach invites the self-reference paradox. This paper employs only the Russell Paradox to illustrate the claimed selfreference paradox.\nOne helpful example of the Russell Paradox is the well-known Cretan liar paradox and, better, the Barber Paradox (Irvine\n& Deutsch, 2021). The only barber in a village has a sign posted in his shop that states, “I cut the hair of everyone in the\nvillage that does not cut his own hair.” However, who cuts the hair of the barber? If he cuts his own hair, then the barber\ncannot cut it. If he does not cut his own hair, then the barber cuts it. Such self-contradiction arises from treating the set,\nwhich is the barber’s statement, as a member of itself. Such treatment entails that the barber is an element of the set of\npeople who do not cut their own hair, on the one hand, and the barber is the set itself as the one who cuts the hair of such\npeople, on the other.\nThe Russell Paradox arises from what philosophers recognize as the problem of self-reference. In the case of the\nRationality-qua-Trait Thesis, it treats rationality both as an OM and as an element of the set that is the subject of the\nOM. This self-reference is best illustrated by some of the drawings of M.C. Escher (see Hofstadter, 1980). The first role of\nthe binary relator (OM) is to ensure that the output is consistently ranked and, corollary, that the corresponding inputs are\nalso consistently ranked. Such an organization of inputs also cannot include OMs: how could the OM call for the\nsubstitution of one input in favor of another when the OM itself is one such input?\nWho’s afraid of the Organismus economicus hypothesis?\nThe implication of the above analysis is that rationality cannot be selected or chosen. Rationality cannot be a trait such as\nmusical skill, intelligence, or any cognitive faculty. As an OM, it is analytically akin to natural selection.\nIt is obvious that some species, such as Homo sapiens, behave rationally. A difficulty arises when a researcher draws a line\nbetween such species and supposedly species deemed to be non-rational. This line, most obviously, leads to the creation\nof a fictitious dichotomy that juxtaposes “rational species” and “non-rational species.” This dichotomy is fictitious, as it\nsupposes a natural set that includes species such as bacteria and, say, lower primates as having a common trait that sets\n\n\nthem apart from another set that includes only upper primates. To avoid such fictitious sets, researchers need to first\nexamine whether rationality is a trait. The Organismus economicus hypothesis certainly avoids such pitfalls, as it ascribes\nrationality to all living entities (Khalil, 2025b).\nHowever, if we treat rationality as OM and hence cannot be seen as a trait, this gives rise to questions about the status of\nrationality with respect to living entities, if not to questions that conjure the specter of metaphysics—the specter of\nconceiving rationality as existing separately from living entities.\nBiologists need not fear the Organismus economicus hypothesis for at least five reasons.\n1. The rationality concept, at least as used by economists (e.g., Becker, 1978; Gilboa, 2012; Khalil, 2025a),\namounts to a rule or a method that does not specify any particular content—not to mention free will, imagination,\ncreative action, or ambition. Rational choice means that organisms generally make the best decision possible\ngiven the environmental constraints and their technological capacities, which include cognitive faculties,\nphysiological traits, technologies, and institutions.\n2. Biologists and psychologists usually restrict rationality to mental or cognitive processes (e.g., Chater et al.,\n2018; Felin et al., 2017). They concluded that organisms with limited cognitive processes, especially plants and\nother brainless organisms, cannot be rational. However, once one understands rationality properly, one cannot\nconflate it with cognitive faculties (see Khalil, 2010).\n3. Biologists (e.g., Gardner, 2009) are afraid of what philosophers call “reification”—that is, the thinking of an\nidea such as rationality and suppose that it is true, i.e., choices made by organisms express rational choice.\nThis raises the specter of metaphysics (see Winn, 1939)—the belief in an “uncaused cause” (Dennett, 1995).\nFor example, the supposed reification of rationality may conjure up uncaused cause and, hence, uphold\ncreationism, that is, the idea that there must be an external agent (e.g., a god) who creates rationality. However,\nthe specter of reification need not arise if one can trace rationality to a defining feature of being a living being.\nHowever, this task is outside the scope of this paper.\n4. Biologists who use the foraging theory pioneered by Eric Charnov (e.g., 1976; see Stephens & Krebs, 1986) or\nwho employ the tools of “bioeconomics” (e.g., Ghiselin, 1974; see also Tullock, 1971, 1994; Khalil &\nMarciano, 2010) already use cost–benefit calculations, that is, rational choice. A few biologists have recently\ntaken the first step in recognizing openly the relevance of rationality to the understanding of behavior or\norganisms (e.g., Vermeij, 2004; Hurley & Nudds, 2006).\n5. New models of brain computation highlight that Shannon-like computations, i.e., information inputs to produce\nsignal outputs, capture only one mode of neural computation. For example, Nicolelis’ (2020) theory registers\nthat neural computation processes consists of two modes. The first is Shannon-like computation, which, similar\nto engineers, treats information as communication—corresponding to a binary operator (cognitive capacity or\ntechnology). The second is Gödelian/Boolean-like computation, which assembles networks of cells to organize\nstructures to solve problems or make decisions—corresponding to a binary relator. While the two modes differ,\nNicolelis and collaborators find that they intermingle as they are complementary (see also Lebedev & Nicolelis,\n2017; Pais-Vieira et al., 2015). This dual brain computation theory substantiates the proposed binary operator/\nrelator distinction. More importantly, the two modes of computation are not limited to human brains. Insofar as\nnon-human organisms are also motivated to evaluate and make decisions, their neural processes must also\nencompass the Gödelian/Boolean-like computation mode.\n6. Many neuroscientists model the brain as an optimization mechanism (OM) that must formulate models (beliefs)\nabout the world in the face of uncertainty. This effort, generally called the “Bayesian brain hypothesis”, assumes\nthat the brain uses new information à la Bayes’ rule—namely, to update its priors (ex ante beliefs) to formulate\nmore accurate posteriors (ex post beliefs) (e.g., Knill & Pouget, 2004; Rao & Ballard, 1999). Friston (2010)\ngeneralizes such OM, calling it “the free-energy principle”, highlighting that the organism wants to minimize\nthe surprise, i.e., the gap between what to expect on the basis of ex ante available information and what it actually\nfinds ex post, i.e., after executing the pertinent action. The minimization of surprise amounts to learning and\nadopting more accurate beliefs. The higher the accuracy of the belief is, the higher the probability of successful\naction. These Bayesian brain models are about the rationality of belief formation, which somewhat differs from\nmodels regarding the rationality of action, the focus of this paper. Nevertheless, they are models of rationality\nthat do not portray rationality as a cognitive mechanism but rather are propelled by the desire of the organism\n\n\nto possess accurate beliefs or minimize free energy. Such models of rationality are generalizable to any\norganism—i.e., not restricted to upper primates or, worse, to humans.\n7. Biologists implicitly employ the rational choice approach when they explain behavior as elastic, that is,\nas responsive to changes in environmental constraints. They call such elasticity “phenotypic plasticity”\n(Gould, 1977, 2002; Stanley, 1979; Matsuda, 1987; West-Eberhard, 1989; Raff, 1996; Müller & Newman,\n2003; Hall et al., 2004; DeWitt & Scheiner, 2004). They are making great strides in showing how even\ninvertebrate organisms make decisions in light of uncertainty (e.g., Oberhauser & Czaczkes, 2018). They\ndocument how animals generally make decisions in reaction to the so-called “contrast effect” (e.g., McNamara\net al., 2013).\nRegarding phenotypic plasticity, that is, this paper calls upon evolutionary biologists, irrespective of their diverse\napproaches and schools (see Khalil, 1993), to recognize what they already are doing. When they study phenotypic\nplasticity, they explain plasticity as the outcome of an organism’s response to changes in the environment. This is\nprecisely the definition of rational choice. As shown elsewhere (Khalil, 2009, 2010), standard rational choice theory is\nsimply the proposition that the organism’s choice changes in light of changes in environmental constraints, i.e., the set of\nincentives. With each choice, the organism maximizes its benefit (i.e., fitness) given the set of constraints or, similarly,\nminimizes the cost of a given benefit.\nConclusions\n1. There is an incongruent gap in the natural sciences. On the one hand, the sciences that focus on the study of\nnon-human organisms do not largely give weight to rationality (i.e., a standard sense of rationality). On the other\nhand, the sciences, especially economics, that focus on the study of human organisms pay great attention to\nrationality.\n2. The sciences that focus on non-human organisms do not need to appeal to rationality, as they largely appeal to\nnatural selection as the optimization method. Nevertheless, they have to explain rationality if they are natural\nsciences, i.e., sciences that cannot exclude humans from nature. Such sciences, if pushed, tend to explain\nrationality as a trait—i.e., similar to intelligence and other cognitive faculties. However, such an explanation\nleads to the self-reference paradox: how could rationality, which is an optimization method, be a trait, i.e., the\nsubject of natural selection, which is an optimization method?\n3. We can easily avoid the self-reference paradox regarding rational choice or any optimization method if we\nemploy a distinction from set theory, namely, the difference between “binary relators” and “binary operators”.\n4. Rationality cannot be a trait given that it is a binary relator that allows us to identify the optimum. Hence,\nit cannot be the subject of evolution. There is no need to appeal to another optimization mechanism (OM) to\nexplain its origin.\n5. The cognitive faculties are traits, as they are binary operators that transform the set of inputs into outputs. Hence,\ncognitive faculties can be the subject of evolution.\nData availability\nNo data are associated with this article.\nReferences\nBaron J: Thinking and Deciding. 5th ed.
