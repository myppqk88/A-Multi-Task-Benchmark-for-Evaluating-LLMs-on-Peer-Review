Growing body of research has indicated that\nbody image concerns along with exercise motivation and social\ncomparison are on the rise among young adults. The present study\naimed to examine the sex difference in body image, exercise\nmotivation and social comparison among people who use Instagram\nin the age group 20-30. A total of 212 participants (men=106,\nwomen=106) aged 20-30 years, who are users of Instagram\ncompleted Body Self Image Questionnaire Short Form as a measure of\nBody image, Exercise Motivation Inventory – 2 as a measure of\nExercise Motivation and Instagram as a Tool for Social Comparison as\na measure of Social Comparison. Results showed that a significant\ndifference in body image exist across gender with body image issues\nhigher among females and significant difference in exercise\nmotivation across gender with exercise motivation higher among\nmales. No sex differences were seen in social comparison. It was\nconcluded that body image concerns are higher among females and\nthe drive for exercise is higher among males who used Instagram. It\nwas found that body image concerns were higher among people who\nexercised regularly as well as among those who followed fitness\nrelated pages on Instagram as compared to those who did not. These\nresults provide an insight into the sex differences between the\nvariables and future directions can be aimed at conducting an indepth analysis using body image, exercise motivation and social\ncomparison.\n\n\nIntroduction\nIn this era of developing technology, many studies have been focusing on how social media and social networking\nsites (SNSs) relate to body image issues. Research indicates that peer contacts, the prevalence of sharing pictures, and\nthe availability of cell phone automation all contribute to the possibility that people who use SNSs may internalize the\n“skinny” prototype and self-embody (Lewallen, Behm-Morawitz, 2016). The role of exercise in understanding\nbody image distortions are undeniable in the contemporary world (Littrell, 2017). The harmful possible side effects\nof engagements in physical activities to enhance one’s body image has been intensified by the SNSs, especially via\napplications like Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat etc. Research has consistently shown that celebrities, fashion influencers, peer and media effects are particularly important in influencing social comparison among both men and women.\nThus, the statistics imply that social comparisons of physical appearance and the internalization of notions of beauty\nremain more prominent in people who actively use social networking (Lewallen, Behm-Morawitz, 2016; Brown &\nTiggemann, 2016).\nSocial media use refers to use of various kinds of artificial intelligence, such as social networking platforms and personal\nwebsites or online columns, through which users build online communities to share knowledge, concepts, beliefs,\nexclusive and intimate messages, and other contents such as photos & videos (Cohen, Newton-John & Slater, 2017).\nAccording to recent data, around 59% world’s population use social media, which in number approximates to 4.76 billion\nand about 137 million people have created online profiles/accounts within the last 12 months with an average daily time of\n2h and 31 m spent in these social networking websites (Kemp S. Digital, 2023: Global Overview Report [Internet], 2023)\nThe various motives for engaging in social media includes entertainment (25.51%),personal utility (12.34%), information\nseeking (7.53%), convenience (6.21%) and altruism (4.90%) (Al-Menayes, 2015). Recent studies have looked at how\nsocial media and social networking sites (SNS) are connected to body image issues (Eckler, Kalyango & Paasch, 2017).\nInstagram is a distinctive social network application which allows users to connect with friends and post photos and\nvideos (Panjrath & Tiwari, 2021). As of October 2021, the number of Instagram users have boosted up to 1.38 billion.\nWhile 70% of Instagram users are under 35 years, the proportion of users over 35years has grown every year since 2018,\nsimilar to Facebook (Statista Research Department, 2022). The terms “body image disturbance,” “body dissatisfaction,”\nor “body image concern” are frequently used in the literature to describe an unfavorable, displeased impression of one’s\nbody (Fardouly et al., 2015). In Western nations, both women and men accept the “thin ideal” conception of women\nportrayed by media and society. Women oftentimes feel a gap between their existing real bodies and the elusive ideal\nfemale body, which eventually paves the way for body dissatisfaction (Mills, Musto, Williams, & Tiggemann, 2018).\nHowever, in the past few decades, the concept of ‘perfect’ male body has gained popularity. According to research, media\nrepresentations of the ideal man have evolved through time and are now highly exaggerated and promoted to men through\npublications and television (Singh, Parsekar, & Bhumika, 2016).\nExercise motivation refers to individuals’ reasons for exercising (participation motives) in determining long-term\nadherence to regular physical activity (Markland & Hardy, 1993). Exercise motivation is primarily predicted by the\nperception of one’s physical appearance as being favourable or unfavourable. One of the specific reasons people exercise\nis to preserve or improve their desired physical appearance. Weight management, looks, and body dissatisfaction have all\nrepeatedly ranked highly as factors that motivate people to exercise, demonstrating the connection between body image\nand exercise participation (Halder & Mondal, 2020). Even though studies on body image with respect to social media\nusage have primarily focused on women, it has been discovered that male millennials are increasingly using SNSs such as\nInstagram and fitness hashtags to communicate and gain knowledge about fit bodies, raising the likelihood of body image\nconcerns.\nLean and toned bodies are frequently portrayed in media, and diet and exercise are frequently promoted for their aesthetic\nrather than health benefits (Holland & Tiggemann, 2016). This evidence points to the fact that people are inclined to\ndesire to change the dimensions of their bodies resulting from the media and societal influence to keep up with the\nidealized convention of a thin and toned body for women and a lean and muscular physique for men, with a desire for the\nrewards for looking good, and the health benefits of maintaining the prescribed body weight (Gültzow, Guidry &\nSchneider, 2020).\nAnother crucial element that has been identified to be a key component in understanding body image is social\ncomparison. According to Festinger’s social comparison theory (1954), people frequently compare their lives and\nselves to those of others based on the information they learn about others (Hobza et al., 2007). Social comparison comes\nin two main forms. Comparison to those we believe to be less fortunate than ourselves in some way, or downward social\ncomparison, tends to improve happiness and self-worth. Upward social comparison or comparing oneself to someone we\nbelieve to be socially superior to oneself, typically results in a bad mood and can jeopardise one’s ability to evaluate\n\n\noneself. When there are differences between oneself and the comparison standard, people are motivated to modify\nthemselves in order to resemble the comparison standard more closely, which serves to promote the self (Vogel, Rose &\nOkdie, 2015; Yang, 2016). There is evidence that various SNS information, including user profiles, “likes,” and\ncomments, can cause social comparison (de Vries et al., 2018).\nCurrent study\nThe media and popular culture endorse a thin beauty standard for women, which women and men in Western cultures\nsimply accept. However, in the past few decades, the concept of ‘perfect’ male body has gained popularity. Although this preference\ntowards bodily stimuli is well-documented in female populations, research on it in male populations is very scarce\n(Daniel Talbot & Daniella Saleme, 2022). Additionally, there are very few published studies on the role\nof social comparison in body image of males, because of which Sex differences in social comparison have not been\ndeeply analyzed. These are few among the studies that mostly focused on women and their body image\ndisturbances. So, we believe a comparative study is required to throw light on how these variables operate in women as\nwell as in men. The second one consisted of the three questionnaires used in the study. The first Google\nform was circulated through various online platforms such as Whatsapp, Instagram and Gmail. After a period of 15 days,\na reminder was sent to the participants regarding the same. Participants who met the inclusion criteria of the study that is\nbelonging to the age group 20-30 and using an Instagram account, participants who identified themselves as male or\nfemale and who gave consent to the study were forwarded the second Google form consisting of the questionnaires. Respondents indicate, by circling the\nappropriate number, whether or not each item is true for them personally or would be true for them personally if they did\nexercise. With 5 being the lowest possible score and 255 being the maximum score, higher\nthe score, greater the exercise motivation. The current study advances our understanding\nof how Instagram use affects both men and women in the age range 20-30 when it comes to body image, exercise\nmotivation, and social comparison. It is significant because it broadens the study of mass media to include Instagram, a\nmedium for social media with a strong visual component. Due to the majority of studies’ attention being directed towards\nwomen, there is relatively little information available on the influence of social networking sites on men’s body image.\nThe perfect male figure, however, has also received attention over the past few decades. As evidenced by images in\nmagazines or action figures, the idealized concept of a more slender, muscular, and V-shaped male body has begun to gain\nattraction among Westerners (Voges et al., 2019). As a result, men, these days are also forced to work towards building an\nideal body that is challenging to achieve. We chose to focus on Instagram in particular because a large body of studies on\nsocial media and body image has been focused on social networking sites like Facebook, Twitter etc. Since Instagram is\none of the most widely utilized social media platforms (SNS) in use today, it is crucial to pay more attention to its\ninfluence. The first hypothesis was that there were no Sex differences in body image, Secondly, it was hypothesized that\nthere were no Sex differences in exercise motivation. Finally, it was hypothesized that there were no Sex differences in\nsocial comparison in males and females Instagram users in the age group 20-30.\nThe first major finding of the study was that levels of body image concerns were higher among females, which was\nsupported by a vast array of previous literature. Previous research findings suggested that ideal form of women has\nevolved through time, as women are frequently shown to be underweight in magazines and advertisements (Luff & Gray,\n2009). Multiple research projects have indicated that there is no doubt that a constant encounter with idealized body types\nhas harmful effects, low self-esteem, and body dissatisfaction, even though studies are hesitant to make that assertion\n(Bessenoff, 2006). Women are constantly met with the need to “control” their body weight below the normal, expected,\nhealthy range as an attempt to fit into the society’s portrayal of idealized women’s body. This has been demonstrated by\nprevious research that explored the relationship between social media and women’s body image, which revealed that, preexposure to social media photographs of fashion models increased women’s’ drive for thinness, which was correlated\nwith post-exposure low mood and dissatisfaction with their body image (Drames, 2016). These findings imply that\nregardless of the setting or individual depicted, exposure to “thin” ideal images that are frequently encountered, either\nthrough newspapers, magazines, social media, or peers may have a comparable detrimental impact on women’s body\nsatisfaction. Future studies might specifically investigate this proposition.\nThe finding that body image concerns were higher among women strongly supported another major finding of the study,\nwhich showed that a significant difference exists in body image across gender; with higher body image concerns among\nfemales. This finding was supported by numerous previous research which indicated that women oftentimes feel a\ngap between their existing real bodies and the elusive - ideal female body, which eventually paves the way for body\ndissatisfaction (Mills et al., 2018). Compared to boys, girls are more conscious about the ways in which their body weight\ninfluences their appearance, beginning right from childhood (Shriver et al., 2013b). In addition, women are inclined to\nhave decreased self-esteem when they feel that their weight is above the idealized weight norms, whereas men tend to\nexperience this only when they feel that they are obese (Shriver et al., 2013a). Congruent to this finding, it was identified\nthat girls give importance to beauty and aesthetics and place less emphasis on the functioning capacity of their bodies than\ndid boys (Abbott & Barber, 2010). More so than the idealized male masculinity, which is represented by more varied\nmedia depictions, the norms for the female body ideal appear to be more well defined (Boute et al., 2011).\nThe second major finding was that the levels of exercise motivation was found to be higher among males. This was seen in\nthe data from the present study which indicated that men reported on an average tending to spend more time on exercise\nand other physical activities than women. The socio-demographic data also revealed that as compared to women, men are\nmore inclined to spend their time in the gym (30%). The dimensions of the Exercise Motivation Inventory used in this\nstudy indicated that men on an average were motivated to exercise more as compared to women due to many reasons,\nincluding the need to gain Strength & Endurance, Ill-health Avoidance, Revitalization, Challenge, Enjoyment, Stress\nManagement. These findings have been supported by previous research findings suggesting gender disparities in exercise\ninvolvement (Bauman et al., 2009). In traditional eastern cultures, where women typically take on the role of carers for the\nfamily and have less time for exercise pointing that, gender roles may have an impact on regular exercise habit (Craft,\nCarroll & Lustyk, 2014). Thus, even though women have body image issues due to social media exposure, they might be\nless indulgent in exercise behaviours due to these reasons (Chen et al., 2011). Additionally, these findings have also been\nstrengthened by previous literature exploring the effect of social media on exercise behaviours in men (Rote et al., 2015).\n\n\nThe success of these platforms has been linked to a number of factors, consisting of the sense of belonging fostered by\nphysical activity-based group chats, which increased accountability for daily exercise, social media-provided encouragement and praise, motivation gained from knowing other members’ progress on exercise behaviours, and motivation\ngained from viewing posts regarding the advantages of exercising (Chen et al., 2011).\nThis result provides strong evidence for another major finding from the present study which revealed that a significant\ndifference in exercise motivation across gender with higher exercise motivation in males in the present study. Previous\nresearch which focused on studying gender disparities in the motivations for exercising supports this finding (Markland &\nTobin, 2010). It is likely that men and women may feel distinct advantages from exercise as a result of their activity\nattributions given these gender disparities in reasons for exercising. Men in general are more likely to attribute exercise to\nsocial and competitive factors, while women are more likely to attribute exercise to appearance-related factors, such as to\nlose or maintain weight. Furthermore, it was observed that rather than exercise itself, women’s reasons for exercising\npredicted their quality of life. Engaging in physical activity with the goal of getting fit or reducing weight, for instance,\nwas linked to poor quality of life, whereas engaging in physical activity to uplift mood or enhance health was linked to\nan increased quality of life (Craft et al., 2014). Previous research also indicates that, men and women “enjoy” exercise\ndifferently. This was visible in the mental health benefits derived from physical activity. Men who engaged in vigorous\nphysical activity reported lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms as well as other visible signs of mental stress\n(Rote et al., 2015). Men may experience less harm to their mental health as a result of their higher levels and more intense\nphysical activity (Craft, Carroll & Lustyk, 2014) It was determined that men benefit more from intensive exercise than\nwomen do from lighter exercise (Asztalos et al., 2010). Additionally, these findings have also been strengthened by\nprevious literature exploring the effect of social media on exercise behaviours in men. The success of these platforms\n(Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat and other online platforms) has been attributed to a number of factors, including the\nsense of belonging fostered by physical activity-based group chats, which increased accountability for daily exercise,\nsocial media-provided encouragement and praise, motivation gained from knowing other members’ progress on exercise\nbehaviours, and motivation gained from viewing posts regarding the advantages of exercising (Jones, 2001; Bessenoff,\n2006).\nWith respect to social comparison, the results revealed that the levels of comparison were slightly higher among women\nas compared to men. This is congruent with a past quantitative data analysis research, which suggested that internalization\nof a “thin” ideal is facilitated by a strong inclination for evaluative comparisons with appealing targets (Morrison, Kalin &\nMorrison, 2004; Schutz, Paxton & Wertheim, 2002) and mediates the impact of the media on body dissatisfaction in\nwomen (Van den Berg et al., 2002). Studies have shown that women usually make comparison of their body with\nattractiveness, whereas men tend to compare their body in terms of build. Because women are frequent targets of ideal\nbody advertisements and commercials in general media as well as social media, these results are not surprising. But this\ndoesn’t deny the fact that men too are confronted with unrealistic comparisons aided by media. In fact, a qualitative study\nindicated that male participants reported that they felt forced by the social networking sites to appear more masculine,\nparticularly on the upper body (Ridgeway & Tylka, 2005). Thus, the levels of social comparison being more or less\nsimilar in males and females indicated that both sexes are confronted with self-evaluation with regards to media portrayals\nof ideal men and women, which might also be a predictor of body image issues in this population. This calls for a more\nin-depth investigation in future to better understand the mediating effect of social comparison on body image in both men\nand women.\nThe finding that there were no Sex differences in social comparison supported the above finding and was incongruent\nwith previous research. Literature has consistently shown that even though men and women frequently coexist in the\nsame social environments, there is ample proof that even the identical social situations can be interpreted and experienced\ndifferently by men and women (Grabe, Ward et al. 2008). Women were shown to be increasingly prone to view\nthemselves from an external point of view and participate in a self-critical comparison process regarding their bodies\nbecause of the cultural preoccupation on ideal female body as an aesthetic object relative to the male body (Franzoi et al.,\n2012). In contrast, men were shown to be more optimistic and less judgmental of themselves when assessing their bodies\nthan women (Miller & Ross, 1975). However, present study could not find any difference in social comparison across\ngender. This points to the assumption that there could be other factors such as the sample size, sociodemographic\ncharacteristics that might have played a role in producing these results.\nImplications\nThe present study contributes to the literature on social media and body image, exercise motivation and social\ncomparison, considering the importance of increased use of SNS such as Instagram over the recent years. This study\nrevealed that body image concerns were higher among women. This finding suggests that women should be educated\nabout the harmful effects of body image distortions and resulting lowered self-esteem and body dissatisfaction.\n\n\nThe finding that drive for exercise was higher among males provides a foundation for understanding the motivators for\nmale exercise behaviours including the genetic, psychological, social and cultural factors. This information can aid in\nplanning interventions for mitigating the potential negative effects of exercise behaviours such as exercise addiction,\nhowever more in depth research is needed.\nStrengths, limitations and future research\nIt was a strength of the current study that it collected a sample that gave disparate information about the participants’\nInstagram activity including the duration of use, celebrities followed, fitness related pages followed, and frequency of\nsharing posts/videos related to fitness which gave a clearer picture of how these factors could be a contributing factor to\nthe Sex differences in the variables studied.\nDespite these strengths, there were a few limitations that ought to be mentioned and which may help to direct future study.\nFirst, the study’s use of a purposive sampling method poses a significant restriction. The representativeness of Indian\nInstagram users may still be overestimated even if the sample comprises of 212 respondents chosen from various online\nplatforms. The study findings can be generalized to populations in the similar age range and similar sociocultural milieu.\nHowever, it is recommended that future studies can incorporate a larger sample size. Another limitation was that although\na significant difference in body image and exercise motivation was observed across gender, the huge community of\nSNS users with over 1.3 billion users on Instagram indicates that these discoveries are relevant only at a population level.\nThe study’s design constituted still another drawback. Its cross-sectional design prevents us from understanding the\ncausal connection between the variables. Finally, it is impossible to completely rule out the possibility of residual\nconfounding such as simultaneous use of other SNS platforms and social desirability bias in self-reported data. There was\nno supplemental information collected from other potential sources, such as relatives or peers.\nGiven these limitations, future research can focus on exploring individuals with a larger range of ages, given that older\nand middle-aged people engage in social media, although in a smaller amount than younger people. All future studies\nshould be proactive to recruit culturally diverse samples as often as possible to be able to draw conclusions about the\npotentially differential effects as well as to make comparisons across individuals from diverse backgrounds. Future\nresearch can also delve deeper into the sociodemographic factors explored in the study to better understand how it can\nplay a role in Sex difference in these variables with large samples. Also, experimental and longitudinal studies are\nrecommended in future studies, that may examine the cause-effect relationships among variables. In future studies,\nadditional data from other possible sources such as family members, peers, or significant others in the environment may\nbe collected to measure.\nConclusions\nBody image, exercise motivation, and social comparison are interrelated concepts that shape the way individuals perceive\nthemselves and others. According to this study, women were more likely than men to have body image concerns. This\nfinding suggests that women need to be made aware of the risks associated with distorted body images, low self-esteem,\nand body dissatisfaction. The discovery that males were more motivated to exercise than females lays the groundwork for\nunderstanding the genetic, psychological, social, and cultural motivations for male exercise behaviours. Planning\ninterventions to lessen the possible negative effects of exercise behaviours, such as exercise addiction, can be made\neasier with the help of this information. It is critical to identify who is at risk of acquiring dangerous exercise practises as a\nresult of a change in their body image for both prevention and treatment. It\nwas found that body image concerns were higher among people who\nexercised regularly as well as among those who followed fitness\nrelated pages on Instagram as compared to those who did not. The current study advances our understanding\nof how Instagram use affects both men and women in the age range 20-30 when it comes to body image, exercise\nmotivation, and social comparison. It is significant because it broadens the study of mass media to include Instagram, a\nmedium for social media with a strong visual component. Due to the majority of studies’ attention being directed towards\nwomen, there is relatively little information available on the influence of social networking sites on men’s body image.\nThe perfect male figure, however, has also received attention over the past few decades. As evidenced by images in\nmagazines or action figures, the idealized concept of a more slender, muscular, and V-shaped male body has begun to gain\nattraction among Westerners (Voges et al., 2019). As a result, men, these days are also forced to work towards building an\nideal body that is challenging to achieve. We chose to focus on Instagram in particular because a large body of studies on\nsocial media and body image has been focused on social networking sites like Facebook, Twitter etc. Since Instagram is\none of the most widely utilized social media platforms (SNS) in use today, it is crucial to pay more attention to its\ninfluence. The first hypothesis was that there were no Sex differences in body image, Secondly, it was hypothesized that\nthere were no Sex differences in exercise motivation. Finally, it was hypothesized that there were no Sex differences in\nsocial comparison in males and females Instagram users in the age group 20-30.\nThe first major finding of the study was that levels of body image concerns were higher among females, which was\nsupported by a vast array of previous literature. Previous research findings suggested that ideal form of women has\nevolved through time, as women are frequently shown to be underweight in magazines and advertisements (Luff & Gray,\n2009). Multiple research projects have indicated that there is no doubt that a constant encounter with idealized body types\nhas harmful effects, low self-esteem, and body dissatisfaction, even though studies are hesitant to make that assertion\n\n\n(Bessenoff, 2006). Women are constantly met with the need to “control” their body weight below the normal, expected,\nhealthy range as an attempt to fit into the society’s portrayal of idealized women’s body. This has been demonstrated by\nprevious research that explored the relationship between social media and women’s body image, which revealed that, preexposure to social media photographs of fashion models increased women’s’ drive for thinness, which was correlated\nwith post-exposure low mood and dissatisfaction with their body image (Drames, 2016). These findings imply that\nregardless of the setting or individual depicted, exposure to “thin” ideal images that are frequently encountered, either\nthrough newspapers, magazines, social media, or peers may have a comparable detrimental impact on women’s body\nsatisfaction. Future studies might specifically investigate this proposition.\nThe finding that body image concerns were higher among women strongly supported another major finding of the study,\nwhich showed that a significant difference exists in body image across gender; with higher body image concerns among\nfemales. This finding was supported by numerous previous research which indicated that women oftentimes feel a\ngap between their existing real bodies and the elusive - ideal female body, which eventually paves the way for body\ndissatisfaction (Mills et al., 2018). Compared to boys, girls are more conscious about the ways in which their body weight\ninfluences their appearance, beginning right from childhood (Shriver et al., 2013b). In addition, women are inclined to\nhave decreased self-esteem when they feel that their weight is above the idealized weight norms, whereas men tend to\nexperience this only when they feel that they are obese (Shriver et al., 2013a). Congruent to this finding, it was identified\nthat girls give importance to beauty and aesthetics and place less emphasis on the functioning capacity of their bodies than\ndid boys (Abbott & Barber, 2010). More so than the idealized male masculinity, which is represented by more varied\nmedia depictions, the norms for the female body ideal appear to be more well defined (Boute et al., 2011).\nThe second major finding was that the levels of exercise motivation was found to be higher among males. This was seen in\nthe data from the present study which indicated that men reported on an average tending to spend more time on exercise\nand other physical activities than women. The socio-demographic data also revealed that as compared to women, men are\nmore inclined to spend their time in the gym (30%). The dimensions of the Exercise Motivation Inventory used in this\nstudy indicated that men on an average were motivated to exercise more as compared to women due to many reasons,\nincluding the need to gain Strength & Endurance, Ill-health Avoidance, Revitalization, Challenge, Enjoyment, Stress\nManagement. These findings have been supported by previous research findings suggesting gender disparities in exercise\ninvolvement (Bauman et al., 2009). In traditional eastern cultures, where women typically take on the role of carers for the\nfamily and have less time for exercise pointing that, gender roles may have an impact on regular exercise habit (Craft,\nCarroll & Lustyk, 2014). Thus, even though women have body image issues due to social media exposure, they might be\nless indulgent in exercise behaviours due to these reasons (Chen et al., 2011). Additionally, these findings have also been\nstrengthened by previous literature exploring the effect of social media on exercise behaviours in men (Rote et al., 2015).\nThe success of these platforms has been linked to a number of factors, consisting of the sense of belonging fostered by\nphysical activity-based group chats, which increased accountability for daily exercise, social media-provided encouragement and praise, motivation gained from knowing other members’ progress on exercise behaviours, and motivation\ngained from viewing posts regarding the advantages of exercising (Chen et al., 2011).\nThis result provides strong evidence for another major finding from the present study which revealed that a significant\ndifference in exercise motivation across gender with higher exercise motivation in males in the present study. Previous\nresearch which focused on studying gender disparities in the motivations for exercising supports this finding (Markland &\nTobin, 2010). It is likely that men and women may feel distinct advantages from exercise as a result of their activity\nattributions given these gender disparities in reasons for exercising. Men in general are more likely to attribute exercise to\nsocial and competitive factors, while women are more likely to attribute exercise to appearance-related factors, such as to\nlose or maintain weight. Furthermore, it was observed that rather than exercise itself, women’s reasons for exercising\npredicted their quality of life. Engaging in physical activity with the goal of getting fit or reducing weight, for instance,\nwas linked to poor quality of life, whereas engaging in physical activity to uplift mood or enhance health was linked to\nan increased quality of life (Craft et al., 2014). Previous research also indicates that, men and women “enjoy” exercise\ndifferently. This was visible in the mental health benefits derived from physical activity. Men who engaged in vigorous\nphysical activity reported lower levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms as well as other visible signs of mental stress\n(Rote et al., 2015). Men may experience less harm to their mental health as a result of their higher levels and more intense\nphysical activity (Craft, Carroll & Lustyk, 2014) It was determined that men benefit more from intensive exercise than\nwomen do from lighter exercise (Asztalos et al., 2010). Additionally, these findings have also been strengthened by\nprevious literature exploring the effect of social media on exercise behaviours in men. The success of these platforms\n(Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat and other online platforms) has been attributed to a number of factors, including the\nsense of belonging fostered by physical activity-based group chats, which increased accountability for daily exercise,\nsocial media-provided encouragement and praise, motivation gained from knowing other members’ progress on exercise\nbehaviours, and motivation gained from viewing posts regarding the advantages of exercising (Jones, 2001; Bessenoff,\n2006).\n\n\nWith respect to social comparison, the results revealed that the levels of comparison were slightly higher among women\nas compared to men. This is congruent with a past quantitative data analysis research, which suggested that internalization\nof a “thin” ideal is facilitated by a strong inclination for evaluative comparisons with appealing targets (Morrison, Kalin &\nMorrison, 2004; Schutz, Paxton & Wertheim, 2002) and mediates the impact of the media on body dissatisfaction in\nwomen (Van den Berg et al., 2002). Studies have shown that women usually make comparison of their body with\nattractiveness, whereas men tend to compare their body in terms of build. Because women are frequent targets of ideal\nbody advertisements and commercials in general media as well as social media, these results are not surprising. But this\ndoesn’t deny the fact that men too are confronted with unrealistic comparisons aided by media. In fact, a qualitative study\nindicated that male participants reported that they felt forced by the social networking sites to appear more masculine,\nparticularly on the upper body (Ridgeway & Tylka, 2005). Thus, the levels of social comparison being more or less\nsimilar in males and females indicated that both sexes are confronted with self-evaluation with regards to media portrayals\nof ideal men and women, which might also be a predictor of body image issues in this population. This calls for a more\nin-depth investigation in future to better understand the mediating effect of social comparison on body image in both men\nand women.\nThe finding that there were no Sex differences in social comparison supported the above finding and was incongruent\nwith previous research. Literature has consistently shown that even though men and women frequently coexist in the\nsame social environments, there is ample proof that even the identical social situations can be interpreted and experienced\ndifferently by men and women (Grabe, Ward et al. 2008). Women were shown to be increasingly prone to view\nthemselves from an external point of view and participate in a self-critical comparison process regarding their bodies\nbecause of the cultural preoccupation on ideal female body as an aesthetic object relative to the male body (Franzoi et al.,\n2012). In contrast, men were shown to be more optimistic and less judgmental of themselves when assessing their bodies\nthan women (Miller & Ross, 1975). However, present study could not find any difference in social comparison across\ngender. This points to the assumption that there could be other factors such as the sample size, sociodemographic\ncharacteristics that might have played a role in producing these results.\nTheoretical implications\nAccording to Self-Determination Theory (SDT), motivation is divided into intrinsic and extrinsic. According to SDT,\nintrinsic motivation generally leads to more sustained involvement in behaviours such as exercise. There is strong\nevidence, supporting SDT's usefulness to understanding exercise motivation according to Teixeira et al. (2012). They\nconducted a systematic study that found strong evidence supporting SDT's usefulness to understanding exercise\nmotivation. Key findings show that autonomous types of motivation, whether driven by intrinsic motivation (enjoyment,\npersonal challenge) or recognized regulation (valuing health benefits), are consistently associated with higher levels\nof physical activity. This review particularly focuses on sex differences in exercise motivation. According to the\nreview, increased exercise motivation in men is primarily driven by intrinsic attributes such as strength and endurance,\nemphasizing the necessity of improving intrinsic motivation in exercise promotion tactics. On the other hand, for women,\nbody dissatisfaction appears to be a strong motivation for exercise. In women, exercise motivation is frequently linked\nto extrinsic reasons such as attractiveness objectives. This emphasizes the need for gender-specific approaches to\nencouraging exercise. The current study implication also emphasizes on considering sex difference for encouraging the\nexercise. For example, addressing body image issues and fostering a supportive environment that shifts the focus away\nfrom beauty and toward overall well-being may be beneficial for women. On the other hand, strategies that increase\nintrinsic motivations, such enjoyment of oneself and skill development, may be more beneficial for men.\nImplications\nThe present study contributes to the literature on social media and body image, exercise motivation and social\ncomparison, considering the importance of increased use of SNS such as Instagram over the recent years. This study\nrevealed that body image concerns were higher among women. This finding suggests that women should be educated\nabout the harmful effects of body image distortions and resulting lowered self-esteem and body dissatisfaction.\nThe finding that drive for exercise was higher among males provides a foundation for understanding the motivators\nfor male exercise behaviours including the genetic, psychological, social and cultural factors. We need to enhance the\nintrinsic motivational factors in men who exercise regularly to the extent that they get to experience a sense of autonomy,\ncompetence and relatedness (in line with the self-determination theory) by seeing the difference for themselves, rather\nthan driven by external standards of body building and fitness portrayed in social media. With respect to women’s body\nimage issues, we need to delve into the sociocultural factors including parenting styles, academic settings, and other\npossible contexts where they could have internalized these unrealistic standards about their own body. It all comes to a\nsingle inference that the more you keep comparing yourself, the more these cognitive distortions gets deeply ingrained in\nyour schema. So, the key is to break the chain very early in the critical developmental stages which can be accomplished\nthrough parental psychoeducation, showing the harsh reality of the media and strengthening healthy relationships.\n\n\n\nBody image interventions: Targeted interventions for women are essential to mitigate the negative impact of social media\non body satisfaction. Programs should focus on enhancing media literacy, promoting body positivity, and encouraging\ncritical thinking about media messages. This information can aid in planning interventions for mitigating the potential\nnegative effects of exercise behaviours such as exercise addiction, however more in-depth research is needed.\nStrengths, limitations and future research\nIt was a strength of the current study that it collected a sample that gave disparate information about the participants’\nInstagram activity including the duration of use, celebrities followed, fitness related pages followed, and frequency of\nsharing posts/videos related to fitness which gave a clearer picture of how these factors could be a contributing factor to\nthe Sex differences in the variables studied.\nDespite these strengths, there were a few limitations that ought to be mentioned and which may help to direct future study.\nFirst, the study’s use of a purposive sampling method poses a significant restriction. The representativeness of Indian\nInstagram users may still be overestimated even if the sample comprises of 212 respondents chosen from various online\nplatforms. The study findings can be generalized to populations in the similar age range and similar sociocultural milieu.\nHowever, it is recommended that future studies can incorporate a larger sample size. Another limitation was that although\na significant difference in body image and exercise motivation was observed across gender, the huge community of\nSNS users with over 1.3 billion users on Instagram indicates that these discoveries are relevant only at a population level.\nThe study’s design constituted still another drawback. Its cross-sectional design prevents us from understanding the\ncausal connection between the variables. Finally, it is impossible to completely rule out the possibility of residual\nconfounding such as simultaneous use of other SNS platforms and social desirability bias in self-reported data. There was\nno supplemental information collected from other potential sources, such as relatives or peers.\nGiven these limitations, future research can focus on exploring individuals with a larger range of ages, given that older\nand middle-aged people engage in social media, although in a smaller amount than younger people. All future studies\nshould be proactive to recruit culturally diverse samples as often as possible to be able to draw conclusions about the\npotentially differential effects as well as to make comparisons across individuals from diverse backgrounds. Future\nresearch can also delve deeper into the sociodemographic factors explored in the study to better understand how it can\nplay a role in Sex difference in these variables with large samples. Also, experimental and longitudinal studies are\nrecommended in future studies, that may examine the cause-effect relationships among variables. In future studies,\nadditional data from other possible sources such as family members, peers, or significant others in the environment may\nbe collected to measure.\nConclusions\nBody image, exercise motivation, and social comparison are interrelated concepts that shape the way individuals perceive\nthemselves and others. According to this study, women were more likely than men to have body image concerns. This\nfinding suggests that women need to be made aware of the risks associated with distorted body images, low self-esteem,\nand body dissatisfaction. The discovery that males were more motivated to exercise than females lays the groundwork for\nunderstanding the genetic, psychological, social, and cultural motivations for male exercise behaviours. Planning\ninterventions to lessen the possible negative effects of exercise behaviours, such as exercise addiction, can be made\neasier with the help of this information. It is critical to identify who is at risk of acquiring dangerous exercise practises as a\nresult of a change in their body image for both prevention and treatment.
