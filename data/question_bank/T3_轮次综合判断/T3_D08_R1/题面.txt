The European\nUnion’s response, embodied in the European sustainability competence framework (GreenComp) (Bianchi et al., 2022), represents a significant policy intervention aimed at systematically\ndeveloping citizens’ capabilities to navigate and address complex socio-ecological challenges. However, the translation\nof this ambitious framework into concrete educational practice remains problematic, revealing a critical gap between policy\naspiration and pedagogical implementation.\nThis research is guided by a central methodological challenge: How can the GreenComp framework be operationalized\ninto a set of valid, assessable learning outcomes that are fit\nfor purpose within a European-level recognition system?\n\nEU competence frameworks for lifelong learning\nThe European Commission’s Joint Research Centre has developed a family of reference frameworks (LifeComp, EntreComp,\nDigComp, and GreenComp) to establish a shared understanding of key competences for citizens across formal, non-formal,\nand informal learning contexts. These “competency models”\nserve to describe learning outcomes and thus provide frameworks for operationalizing educational goals (Klieme et al.,\n2004). However, they vary significantly in their level of detail\nand implementation guidance.\nLifeComp, the European framework for “personal, social and\nlearning to learn” competences, consists of nine competencies\norganized in three areas (Personal, Social, Learning to Learn). It\nis explicitly conceptual and non-prescriptive, intended to\ninform curriculum and learning-activity design, but does not\ninclude a progression model or a formal catalogue of learning\noutcomes.\nEntreComp, the Entrepreneurship Competence Framework,\narticulates 15 competences in three interrelated areas (Ideas &\nOpportunities, Resources, Into Action), mapped along an 8-level\nprogression model, and provides a comprehensive list of 442\nlearning outcomes to guide curriculum design and assessment\n(Bacigalupo et al., 2016).\nDigComp, the Digital Competence Framework for Citizens,\ndefines 21 digital competences across five areas, described at\neight proficiency levels, and is being extended via the “DigComp:\nLearning outcomes” project to develop intended learning outcomes and supporting materials for diverse application contexts.\nThe development of learning outcomes for DigComp may serve\nas the basis for the European Digital Skills Certificate (EDSC),\nconceptualized as a quality label for digital skills certification\nacross Europe.\nGreenComp, the European Sustainability Competence Framework, identifies 12 sustainability competences organized into\nfour areas (Embodying Sustainability Values, Embracing\n\nComplexity, Envisioning Sustainable Futures, Acting for\nSustainability), each described by a competency descriptor\nplus Knowledge–Skills–Attitudes (KSAs). While the framework’s background document references “behavioral learning outcomes” (Scalabrino, 2022), and the main framework\ndocument acknowledges the need to incorporate learning outcomes into sustainability pedagogies (Bianchi et al., 2022),\nneither establishes formal, assessable learning outcomes nor a\nprogression model, resulting in an “implementation vacuum”.\n\nThe GreenComp framework – Focus of this study\nEntreComp and DigComp already define or are in the process\nof defining detailed learning outcomes, and LifeComp remains\nat a conceptual level without specific outcome descriptors.\nIn this study, the authors propose a set of measurable learning\noutcomes for GreenComp. In doing so, it aims to fill the current gap in operationalizing the sustainability competences of\nGreenComp, transforming its descriptive KSAs into assessable\nstatements that educators and trainers can use to guide\ncurriculum development, implementation, and evaluation.\nCurrent state of GreenComp assessment\nThe landscape of GreenComp assessment initiatives reveals\na complex tapestry of approaches (Javorka et al., 2024), each\ngrappling with the fundamental challenge of operationalizing\nsustainability competencies into measurable outcomes. These\ndiverse efforts, while demonstrating considerable innovation and\ncommitment, collectively illuminate both the possibilities and\npersistent limitations in translating the framework’s ambitious\nvision into practical assessment tools.\nTraditional knowledge-focused assessment approaches dominate the current landscape, exemplified by TASKTM by Sulitest,\nwhich achieves comprehensive coverage of knowledge elements\n(100% alignment) while demonstrating more limited capacity\nin assessing skills (70% alignment) and attitudes (30% alignment) (Sulitest, 2023). This imbalance, far from being merely\ntechnical, reflects deeper epistemological assumptions about\nwhat constitutes legitimate and measurable learning in sustainability education. CertiSkillTM similarly privileges cognitive dimensions through its certification exam based on 30 multiple-choice\nquestions aligned with UNI/Pdr 109.2:2021 standards, while\nthe Engineers4Europe project’s Green Skills micro-credential,\ndespite its innovative platform delivery and broad disciplinary\nappeal, ultimately relies on three knowledge-based quizzes\nto assess competencies in corporate sustainability and ESG.\nAttempts to address the skills dimension of GreenComp have\nproduced varied approaches with differing levels of rigor. The\nMy Personality Skills® framework by Fundacja represents a\nprivate sector initiative offering the MY PS GreenComp\nCertificate, with examinations spanning all four GreenComp\nareas. However, the proprietary nature of this assessment raises\nquestions about transparency and alignment with the open, collaborative spirit of the European competence frameworks. More\ninnovative approaches emerge in the self-assessment domain,\nwhere BCN+B’s Be Ready For Now employs a 48-question\nreflective instrument examining sustainability habits and\n\n\nknowledge, though its self-reporting methodology limits its\nutility for formal competence recognition.\nThe assessment of attitudes and values, arguably the most\ntransformative dimension of sustainability education, remains\nparticularly underdeveloped. The Advancing Sustainability Education’s Sustainability Competence Mindset Map (SCMM) represents one of the few initiatives explicitly capturing attitudinal\ndimensions of sustainability competence capacity building. The\nSCMM is a formative self-reflective assessment based on the\nkey sustainability competence frameworks (Brundiers et al.,\n2021; Redman & Weik, 2021) and has been validated across\ndisciplines in higher education (Annelin & Boström, 2023;\nAnnelin & Boström, 2024a; Annelin, 2025). It is a facilitating tool that supports teacher course planning and curriculum\ndesign, where teachers follow the progression of the affective\nintrapersonal attitudes of students over different periods of time\n(a course, a semester, a year, a program). Teachers can communicate the learning experience with the students (Annelin,\n2025) by using visual aids, reflective work, and complementary assessment techniques. The outcome of the SCMM assessment is automated via online tools integrated with data software\nto produce visual and comparable diagrams of the class progression. The tool is applied in the understanding that sustainability\ncompetence capacity building is a nonlinear, life-long learning\nprocess. This nonlinear relation is captured in the visual diagrams presented. The SCMM is used to complement the integration of knowledge and skills assessment necessary for a\ntraditional holistic competence assessment.\nEducational technology has offered novel pathways for engagement, though not necessarily for rigorous assessment. The\nGameComp–Green Edugames Erasmus+ project exemplifies\nthis trend through its gamified approach, featuring 36 selfreflective questions that award badges and points, creating\nmotivational affordances while formal competence certification\ncalls for established validity and reliability standards.\nProviding a more methodologically robust but more narrowly\nfocused example, the EduCITY project uses mobile augmented\nreality games (MARGs) to assess sustainability competencies\nwithin smart learning city environments. This research-driven\ninitiative demonstrates how a deep and specific assessment\ncan be operationalized, but it also highlights the challenge of\ncomprehensive coverage. Its assessment model, as presented,\nexplicitly targets only one of the four GreenComp competence\nareas: ‘Embodying Sustainability Values’. Within this specific\narea, the project employs a rigorous mixed-methods approach,\ntriangulating data from a validated GreenComp-based questionnaire with automated gameplay performance logs. While its\nscope is partial, the EduCITY model provides a structured and\nevidence-based method for evaluating competencies fostered\nthrough experiential, technology-enhanced learning, a level\nof detail not present in initiatives that claim broader coverage\nbased on self-reflection or participation alone (Marques et al.,\n2025).\nThe ASSESS project developed a more pedagogically sophisticated approach, creating rubrics for Green Competencies\n\nassessment across multiple levels (primary, secondary, higher\neducation, and business contexts) with an accompanying mobile\napplication. Their framework emphasizes dialogic assessment through co-creation of rubrics between teachers and students, peer and self-assessment capabilities, and collaborative\ndiscussion of evaluation criteria (Sousa et al., 2024). While\nthis dialogic approach is powerful for fostering student agency\nand formative development, its reliance on context-specific,\nco-created criteria stands in direct tension with the goals of a\nstandardized recognition framework. The method’s pedagogical\nstrength in the classroom, therefore, presents a significant challenge for issuing portable credentials, whose currency depends\non a high degree of comparability and inter-institutional trust.\nThe emergence of Open Badge initiatives represents a particularly promising yet problematic development in GreenComp\noperationalization. The GoGreen - Youth Navigator project’s\nGreen Badges system, hosted on the Cities of Learning platform and announced for pilot testing in January 2025, allows\nactivity organizers to tag skills and competencies while enabling participants to earn badges and share learning experiences.\nHowever, the absence of defined learning outcomes or systematic assessment criteria reduces these badges to participation certificates rather than competence credentials. The\nsame platform hosts the Climate for All project’s “Climate for\nAll Facilitator Training”, where a 5-hour course includes a\nGreenComp Reflection badge recognizing “experiential understanding” gained through game-based learning, a formulation\nthat sidesteps the challenge of defining what such understanding\nentails or how it might be evidenced beyond self-report.\nMore substantial efforts at badge-based recognition emerge\nin formal educational contexts. The University of Florence’s\n“Education Towards Sustainable Futures” badge, launched in\nJune 2023 as part of The European University for Well-Being\n[EUniWell] Erasmus+ project, recognizes completion of a\nhybrid course for future teachers comprising five days of training, self-learning activities, and evaluation. While this initiative\ndemonstrates closer alignment with formal educational quality assurance through its explicit connection to the GreenComp\nmethodological framework, it remains fundamentally a credential\nof participation rather than assessed competence.\nThis proliferation of initiatives collectively reveals both the\nwidespread recognition of GreenComp’s importance and the\npersistent conceptual and methodological challenges in its\nimplementation. Each project’s idiosyncratic interpretation of\nwhat constitutes evidence of sustainability competencies has created a fragmented landscape where innovation flourishes but\ncoherence and comparability remain elusive. The absence of\nshared assessment criteria, quality assurance mechanisms, or\ninteroperability frameworks means that a learner’s sustainability\ncompetencies may be recognized entirely differently, or not at\nall, across institutional and national boundaries. This patchwork approach, while reflecting healthy experimentation, ultimately undermines GreenComp’s vision of providing a common\nEuropean framework for sustainability competencies, highlighting the urgent need for more systematic approaches to\nlearning outcome definition and assessment methodology.\n\n\nThe need for learning outcomes in GreenComp\nLearning outcomes are essential for operationalizing competence frameworks, as they translate competencies into concrete,\nobservable, and assessable statements of what learners should\nknow and be able to do. The development of clear learning\noutcomes for GreenComp provides concrete targets for curriculum design and assessment in formal education, while guiding\nthe development of non-formal learning experiences and workplace training. They enable the validation and recognition of\ninformal learning through frameworks like Open Badges\nand support the evaluation of educational interventions for\nsustainability competence development.\nYet the challenge of developing learning outcomes for GreenComp extends beyond these technical considerations of measurement and validation. Sustainability competencies embody what\nWals (2015) terms “gestalt competencies”, i.e., holistic capabilities that emerge from the integration of knowledge, values,\nand action in ways that resist decomposition into discrete elements. This conceptualization reveals a fundamental paradox at\nthe heart of our endeavor: educational systems require specificity\nfor assessment and credentialing, while sustainability challenges demand integrated, systemic responses that transcend\ndisciplinary and methodological boundaries.\nThe “wicked” nature of sustainability problems compounds\nthis challenge. Characterized by complexity, uncertainty, value\nconflicts, and systemic interdependencies, these problems\nresist reduction to discrete, measurable outcomes. UNESCO’s\nframework for Education for Sustainable Development\n(UNESCO, 2019) emphasizes the need to develop three interconnected dimensions: cognitive (knowledge and understanding)\n(Spaccatini et al., 2023), socio-emotional (values and attitudes) (Lozano et al., 2022), and behavioral dimension (often\nreferred to as conative, encompassing goal-directed actions\nand capabilities) (Presseau et al., 2019). This tripartite model\nsuggests that traditional approaches to learning outcome development, which typically privilege cognitive dimensions as\nmore readily assessable, may be fundamentally insufficient for\ncapturing the transformative potential of sustainability education.\nFurthermore, sustainability education carries an explicitly normative dimension that distinguishes it from other competence\nframeworks in the European portfolio. While DigComp can\nmaintain relative neutrality regarding digital tool use, treating\ntechnology as a means rather than an end, and EntreComp can\nembrace diverse entrepreneurial values within a broadly capitalist framework, GreenComp necessarily embeds particular values about human-nature relationships, intergenerational justice,\nand systemic transformation. This normative dimension creates profound tensions when developing learning outcomes\nthat must be both prescriptive enough to guide learning and\nopen enough to accommodate diverse cultural and ideological\ncontexts.\nThe gestalt nature of sustainability competencies thus presents\na double bind: decompose them too much and they lose\ntheir transformative essence, becoming mere technical skills\ndivorced from the values and systemic thinking that give them\n\nmeaning; leave them too holistic and they become impossible to assess, reducing credentials to vague attestations of exposure rather than evidence of capability. This tension between the\nholistic nature of sustainability competencies and the analytical\nrequirements of educational systems represents not merely a\ntechnical challenge to be solved, but a fundamental feature of\nsustainability education that must be navigated thoughtfully.\nOur approach to developing learning outcomes for GreenComp\nmust therefore acknowledge and work within these tensions\nrather than attempting to resolve them prematurely. The outcomes must be specific enough to guide educational practice and\nenable recognition, while remaining open enough to accommodate the emergent, contextual, and transformative nature of\nsustainability learning. They must honor the gestalt quality of\nsustainability competencies while providing sufficient structure\nfor implementation across diverse educational contexts. Most\ncritically, they must maintain the normative vision of sustainability transformation while respecting cultural diversity and\nlocal knowledge systems.\n\nThe OpenPass4Climate context\nThe OpenPass4Climate project (2022-1-FR01-KA220-HED000089354) provides the institutional context for this learning outcome development, with its focus on creating an open\nrecognition alliance system for sustainability competencies.\nThe project aims to tackle climate change by implementing innovative teaching and learning methods, with one of its\nkey objectives being the development of Open Badges for recognition of non-formal and informal learning related to sustainability competencies, ultimately leading to certification of\ncompetencies through Europass. This technological and institutional infrastructure shapes the requirements for learning\noutcomes, which must be granular enough for badge-based recognition while comprehensive enough to represent meaningful\ncompetency development. This presents a central methodological\nchallenge: leveraging the accessibility of micro-credentials\nwithout succumbing to the very reductionism that a holistic\nframework like GreenComp seeks to avoid. The methodology described below was designed explicitly to navigate this\ntension.\n\nMethodology\n\nThe development of learning outcomes for GreenComp\nrequired a methodological approach that could navigate multiple tensions: between theoretical comprehensiveness and\npractical applicability, between standardization and contextual\nflexibility, and between expert knowledge and stakeholder accessibility. A systematic, iterative approach was chosen, comprising four interconnected phases: initial co-design, first expert\nvalidation, coverage analysis, and second expert validation. This\nmulti-phase approach allowed for progressive refinement while\nmaintaining alignment with both the GreenComp framework\nand CEDEFOP guidelines for learning outcome development.\n\nPhase 1: Initial co-design process\nThe co-design phase represented a deliberate departure from\nexpert-driven approaches to learning outcome development.\nRather than beginning with technical specifications derived\n\n\nfrom learning theory, we initiated a collaborative process involving stakeholders from the OpenPass4Climate consortium\n(CSCI Novara, NOVA University, UniLaSalle, and Valladolid\nUniversity), including educators from formal higher education\ncontexts, trainers from non-formal education settings, and\nlearners representing diverse disciplinary backgrounds. This\nheterogeneous composition was essential for ensuring that the\nresulting learning outcomes would be meaningful across the\nvaried contexts in which GreenComp might be implemented.\nThe co-design process unfolded through a series of structured\nworkshops conducted over three months. Each workshop focused\non a specific GreenComp competence area, with participants\nworking in interdisciplinary groups to translate abstract KSA\nstatements into concrete learning outcomes.\nTo structure the translation process, we employed Bloom’s\nrevised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) as a heuristic device rather than a rigid framework. Participants were\nencouraged to consider how each competence might manifest at\ndifferent cognitive levels, from basic comprehension to creative synthesis. However, we found that many sustainability\ncompetencies resisted easy categorization within Bloom’s\nframework, particularly those involving affective dimensions or\nsystems thinking. This led to iterative refinement of outcomes\nthat attempted to capture the holistic nature of competencies\nwhile maintaining sufficient specificity for assessment purposes.\nThe initial co-design phase produced 36 learning outcomes,\nthree for each of the 12 GreenComp competencies. These were\nformulated according to the Centre Européen pour le Développement de la Formation Professionnelle (CEDEFOP) guidelines for defining, writing and applying learning outcomes\n(CEDEFOP, 2022), ensuring they were observable (describing a\nbehavior or product that can be observed), measurable (assessable using specific criteria), and student-centered (focusing on\nwhat the learner will be able to do). Each learning outcome was\nformulated to integrate relevant knowledge, skills, and attitudes\nfrom the GreenComp framework, translating the abstract KSAs\ninto concrete statements of what learners should demonstrate.\n\nPhase 2: First expert validation round\nThe expert validation phase employed a modified Delphi\napproach designed to balance quality assurance with practical\nconstraints, such as the project timeline and expert availability. Our expert panel comprised 12 specialists selected based on\ndemonstrated expertise in sustainability education, familiarity with European competence frameworks (evidenced through\npublications or project participation), and representation of\ndiverse educational contexts (formal, non-formal, and informal\nsettings).\nThe institutional diversity of the panel was carefully cultivated, with experts representing universities, vocational education institutions, and NGOs focused on sustainability education.\nThis diversity proved crucial during the validation process, as\nexperts brought culturally and contextually specific insights\nthat challenged the universalist assumptions embedded in our\ninitial formulations. The panel included representatives from\n\nvarious EU sustainability projects (such as TWIN-IN, ASSESS,\nEduC3, GrowLearnOut, GreenScent, GreenAtYou, and Entrepreneurship4All) and authors of GreenComp-related publications\nand initiatives.\nThe validation instrument consisted of a structured evaluation form for each learning outcome, incorporating both binary\nvalidity judgments and qualitative feedback. Experts evaluated\neach outcome against four criteria:\n–\n\n\u0007Alignment: Clear relationship to the target GreenComp\ncompetence\n\n–\n\n\u0007Measurability: Use of clear, assessable verbs and\nspecific performance indicators\n\n–\n\n\u0007Student-centricity: Focus on learner capabilities rather\nthan teaching activities\n\n–\n\n\u0007Observability: Potential for demonstration through\nobservable behaviors or products\n\nFor each criterion, experts indicated whether the learning outcome was valid or not valid, accompanied by open-ended\ncomments for improvement suggestions. A learning outcome\nwas considered to require revision if any expert marked it as\nnot valid on any criterion or if substantial concerns were raised\nin the qualitative feedback. The validation was conducted\nasynchronously over four weeks, allowing experts sufficient\ntime for thoughtful evaluation.\nBinary validation data from expert ratings were analyzed to\nidentify outcomes requiring revision (those marked as not\nvalid by any expert on any criterion). Learning outcomes were\ncategorized as: (1) approved without changes if all experts\nmarked them as valid across all criteria, or (2) requiring revision\nif one or more experts identified validity concerns. Qualitative\nfeedback was analyzed thematically to identify recurring concerns and suggestions for improvement, with particular attention\nto cases where experts disagreed on validity judgments.\n\nPhase 3: Coverage analysis\nFollowing the first validation round, we also conducted a systematic coverage analysis to assess how comprehensively the\nlearning outcomes addressed all aspects of the GreenComp\nframework. This imbalance reflects not merely technical limitations but deeper\nassumptions about what constitutes legitimate educational\nassessment. The measurability of factual knowledge through\ntraditional testing formats creates path dependencies that\nmarginalize the transformative dimensions of sustainability\neducation.\nThis limitation is critical because, as Mezirow (1981) argues,\na core aim of education for sustainability must be to transform\nlearners’ perspectives, helping them reconsider and change\nproblematic frames of reference while reshaping fundamental\nattitudes. Our contribution responds directly to this transformative imperative. Unlike initiatives that primarily focus on knowledge assessment, the developed learning outcomes address\nall three dimensions of learning (cognitive, social-emotional,\nand behavioral) across all 12 GreenComp competencies. In\ncontrast to self-assessment tools, the learning outcomes are\ndesigned to be observable through specific behaviors or products and measurable using defined criteria. Rather than treating knowledge, skills, and attitudes as separate elements, the\nlearning outcomes integrate these components into holistic\nstatements of what learners should be able to demonstrate.\n\nThe philosophical grounding of our approach also distinguishes\nit from technocratic assessment initiatives. By explicitly incorporating learning outcomes that challenge anthropocentric\nworldviews and require engagement with environmental ethics, we acknowledge that sustainability education cannot be\nvalue-neutral. This normative dimension, while complicating assessment, is essential for the transformative potential of\nsustainability education.\n\nChallenges and limitations\nThe development of learning outcomes for GreenComp revealed\nseveral persistent challenges that reflect deeper tensions in\nsustainability education. The most fundamental challenge\nconcerns the tension between the holistic, transformative nature\nof sustainability competencies and the analytical requirements\nof learning outcome specifications. The learning outcomes are\npresented as discrete units, but sustainability competencies, with\ntheir emphasis on systems thinking, value transformation, and\ncollective action, resist decomposition into discrete, observable\nbehaviors.\nCultural and contextual variability presents another significant challenge. While our expert panel represented diverse\nEuropean contexts, the learning outcomes inevitably embed\ncertain cultural assumptions about sustainability, education, and\nassessment. What constitutes “supporting fairness” or “promoting\nnature” varies significantly across cultural contexts, and our\nformulations, despite attempts at inclusivity, reflect predominantly Western European perspectives. Future research should\ntherefore focus on validating and adapting these learning\noutcomes in non-European contexts, engaging with diverse\n\n\ncultural knowledge systems to enrich and decolonize the\nframework.\nThe assessment challenge remains particularly acute. While we\nhave specified observable and measurable dimensions for each\nlearning outcome, the actual assessment of complex competencies like “challenging anthropocentric perspectives” requires\nsophisticated approaches that go beyond traditional testing. Portfolio assessment, peer evaluation, and community-based projects\noffer possibilities, but these require resources and expertise\nthat may not be available in all educational contexts.\n\nImplications for Practice\nThe implementation of these learning outcomes in educational\npractice requires consideration of context, resources, and pedagogical approaches. Educators implementing these outcomes\nshould view them not as rigid prescriptions but as flexible frameworks that can be adapted to local contexts while\nmaintaining alignment with GreenComp’s core intentions.\nDigital badge systems offer several affordances for competency recognition. Badges provide granular documentation of\nspecific competencies achieved (Hickey & Willis, 2017). They\ncan increase learner motivation through visible progress markers and social recognition (Abramovich et al., 2013). However,\nimplementation approaches vary significantly in their effectiveness. Some badge systems risk fragmenting holistic competencies into disconnected micro-credentials, potentially undermining\nthe integrated nature of sustainability education (Lockley\net al., 2016). This fragmentation echoes a broader challenge in\nsustainability education: the tendency toward isolated learning\nexperiences that fail to connect across disciplines, contexts, and\nreal-world applications (Christie et al., 2013; Sipos et al., 2008).\nStudents consistently report frustration with compartmentalized sustainability courses that don’t translate into integrated\nunderstanding or action (Hopkinson & James, 2010). More\neffective badge implementations recognize ‘constellations’\nof learning outcomes, requiring learners to demonstrate competence across cognitive, behavioral, and affective domains. For\ninstance, a badge might require combining a systems analysis\nwith a collective action initiative before a credential is awarded.\nThis approach better honors the holistic ‘gestalt’ nature of sustainability competence. However, the labor market reception of such\ncredentials remains mixed, with some employers valuing the specific competency documentation while others remain unfamiliar\nwith badge-based credentials (Carey & Stefaniak, 2018; Raish &\nRimland, 2016).\nProfessional development for educators represents perhaps\nthe most critical factor for the successful implementation of\nthese learning outcomes. The competencies assume educators who can facilitate complex learning processes, assess transformative capabilities, and navigate the inherently value-laden\nterrain of sustainability education (Annelin & Boström, 2024b).\nThis extends far beyond technical training in assessment methods. Educators require deep engagement with sustainability\nconcepts, systems thinking pedagogies, and transformative\nlearning approaches (Burns, 2015; Sterling, 2024). They need\n\nsupport in developing facilitation skills for managing potentially contentious discussions about values, justice, and systemic\nchange. Furthermore, educators themselves must engage in\nthe same transformative learning processes they seek to facilitate, examining their own assumptions about human-nature\nrelationships and sustainability transitions (Moore, 2005).\nWithout substantial investment in such comprehensive professional development, even the most carefully designed learning outcomes risk being implemented in ways that perpetuate\ntransmissive rather than transformative education.\n\nFuture directions\nThe development of these learning outcomes for GreenComp\nrepresents a beginning rather than an endpoint. Future work\nshould focus on several critical areas to ensure their effective\nand equitable implementation: (1) extensive field-testing across\ndiverse educational contexts, (2) development of robust assessment frameworks and tools, (3) creation of comprehensive\nimplementation guides and supporting materials, (4) establishment of progression pathways for competency development over\ntime, and (5) integration with existing curriculum frameworks\nand qualification systems.\nA primary step is to conduct extensive field testing in diverse\neducational settings. This is essential not only to validate the\npractical utility of the learning outcomes but also to inform the\ndevelopment of guidance for their contextual adaptation. To\nensure the framework’s long-term relevance as sustainability challenges evolve, it would be prudent to establish a review\ncycle to periodically reassess its effectiveness.\nThe development of robust assessment frameworks is another\npriority. This includes creating tools and rubrics that can\ncapture the complexity of sustainability competencies while\nremaining practical for educators. Crucially, this work should\ninclude examples of low-resource assessment methods to ensure\napplicability across varied economic and institutional contexts.\nTo support educators, the creation of comprehensive implementation guides is vital. Such guides could be complemented by\nintegrated learning modules that address multiple competencies\nsimultaneously and supporting materials that address prerequisite knowledge requirements. Building on this, the creation of\nprogression pathways showing how learners can develop competencies over time would address a significant gap and would\nhelp educators design coherent learning sequences. The European\nUnion’s response, embodied in the European sustainability competence framework (GreenComp) (Bianchi et al., 2022), represents a significant policy intervention aimed at systematically\ndeveloping citizens’ capabilities to navigate and address complex socio-ecological challenges. However, the translation\nof this ambitious framework into concrete educational practice remains problematic, revealing a critical gap between policy\naspiration and pedagogical implementation.\nThis research is guided by a central methodological challenge: How can the GreenComp framework be operationalized\ninto a set of valid, assessable learning outcomes that are fit\nfor purpose within a European-level recognition system?\n\nEU competence frameworks for lifelong learning\nThe European Commission’s Joint Research Centre has developed a family of reference frameworks (LifeComp, EntreComp,\nDigComp, and GreenComp) to establish a shared understanding of key competences for citizens across formal, non-formal,\nand informal learning contexts. These “competency models”\nserve to describe learning outcomes and thus provide frameworks for operationalizing educational goals (Klieme et al.,\n2004). However, they vary significantly in their level of detail\nand implementation guidance.\n\nLifeComp, the European framework for “personal, social and\nlearning to learn” competences, consists of nine competencies\norganized in three areas (Personal, Social, Learning to Learn). It\nis explicitly conceptual and non-prescriptive, intended to\ninform curriculum and learning-activity design, but does not\ninclude a progression model or a formal catalogue of learning\noutcomes.\nEntreComp, the Entrepreneurship Competence Framework,\narticulates 15 competences in three interrelated areas (Ideas &\nOpportunities, Resources, Into Action), mapped along an 8-level\nprogression model, and provides a comprehensive list of 442\nlearning outcomes to guide curriculum design and assessment\n(Bacigalupo et al., 2016).\nDigComp, the Digital Competence Framework for Citizens,\ndefines 21 digital competences across five areas, described at\neight proficiency levels, and is being extended via the “DigComp:\nLearning outcomes” project to develop intended learning outcomes and supporting materials for diverse application contexts.\nThe development of learning outcomes for DigComp may serve\nas the basis for the European Digital Skills Certificate (EDSC),\nconceptualized as a quality label for digital skills certification\nacross Europe.\nGreenComp, the European Sustainability Competence Framework, identifies 12 sustainability competences organized into four\nareas (Embodying Sustainability Values, Embracing Complexity, Envisioning Sustainable Futures, Acting for Sustainability),\neach described by a competency descriptor plus Knowledge–\nSkills–Attitudes (KSAs). While the framework’s background\ndocument references “behavioral learning outcomes” (Scalabrino,\n2022), and the main framework document acknowledges the\nneed to incorporate learning outcomes into sustainability pedagogies (Bianchi et al., 2022), neither establishes formal, assessable learning outcomes nor a progression model, resulting\nin an “implementation vacuum”.\n\nThe GreenComp framework – Focus of this study\nEntreComp and DigComp already define or are in the process\nof defining detailed learning outcomes, and LifeComp remains\nat a conceptual level without specific outcome descriptors.\nIn this study, the authors propose a set of measurable learning\noutcomes for GreenComp. In doing so, it aims to fill the current gap in operationalizing the sustainability competences of\nGreenComp, transforming its descriptive KSAs into assessable\nstatements that educators and trainers can use to guide\ncurriculum development, implementation, and evaluation.\nCurrent state of GreenComp assessment\nThe landscape of GreenComp assessment initiatives reveals\na complex tapestry of approaches (Javorka et al., 2024), each\ngrappling with the fundamental challenge of operationalizing\nsustainability competencies into measurable outcomes. These\ndiverse efforts, while demonstrating considerable innovation and\ncommitment, collectively illuminate both the possibilities and\npersistent limitations in translating the framework’s ambitious\nvision into practical assessment tools.\n\n\nTraditional knowledge-focused assessment approaches dominate the current landscape, exemplified by TASKTM by Sulitest,\nwhich achieves comprehensive coverage of knowledge elements\n(100% alignment) while demonstrating more limited capacity\nin assessing skills (70% alignment) and attitudes (30% alignment) (Sulitest, 2023). The absence of\nshared assessment criteria, quality assurance mechanisms, or\ninteroperability frameworks means that a learner’s sustainability\ncompetencies may be recognized entirely differently, or not at\nall, across institutional and national boundaries. This patchwork approach, while reflecting healthy experimentation, ultimately undermines GreenComp’s vision of providing a common\nEuropean framework for sustainability competencies, highlighting the urgent need for more systematic approaches to\nlearning outcome definition and assessment methodology.\n\nThe need for learning outcomes in GreenComp\nLearning outcomes are essential for operationalizing competence frameworks, as they translate competencies into concrete,\nobservable, and assessable statements of what learners should\nknow and be able to do. The development of clear learning\noutcomes for GreenComp provides concrete targets for curriculum design and assessment in formal education, while guiding\nthe development of non-formal learning experiences and workplace training. They enable the validation and recognition of\ninformal learning through frameworks like Open Badges\nand support the evaluation of educational interventions for\nsustainability competence development.\nYet the challenge of developing learning outcomes for GreenComp extends beyond these technical considerations of measurement and validation. Sustainability competencies embody what\nWals (2015) terms “gestalt competencies”, i.e., holistic capabilities that emerge from the integration of knowledge, values,\nand action in ways that resist decomposition into discrete elements. This conceptualization reveals a fundamental paradox at\nthe heart of our endeavor: educational systems require specificity\nfor assessment and credentialing, while sustainability challenges demand integrated, systemic responses that transcend\ndisciplinary and methodological boundaries.\nThe “wicked” nature of sustainability problems compounds this\nchallenge. Characterized by complexity, uncertainty, value conflicts, and systemic interdependencies, these problems resist\nreduction to discrete, measurable outcomes. UNESCO’s framework for Education for Sustainable Development (UNESCO,\n2019) emphasizes the need to develop three interconnected\ndimensions: cognitive (knowledge and understanding) (Spaccatini\net al., 2023), socio-emotional (values and attitudes) (Lozano\net al., 2022), and behavioral dimension (often referred to\n\nas conative, encompassing goal-directed actions and capabilities) (Presseau et al., 2019). This tripartite model\nsuggests that traditional approaches to learning outcome development, which typically privilege cognitive dimensions as\nmore readily assessable, may be fundamentally insufficient for\ncapturing the transformative potential of sustainability education.\nFurthermore, sustainability education carries an explicitly normative dimension that distinguishes it from other competence\nframeworks in the European portfolio. While DigComp can\nmaintain relative neutrality regarding digital tool use, treating\ntechnology as a means rather than an end, and EntreComp can\nembrace diverse entrepreneurial values within a broadly capitalist framework, GreenComp necessarily embeds particular values about human-nature relationships, intergenerational justice,\nand systemic transformation. This normative dimension creates profound tensions when developing learning outcomes\nthat must be both prescriptive enough to guide learning and\nopen enough to accommodate diverse cultural and ideological\ncontexts.\nThe gestalt nature of sustainability competencies thus presents\na double bind: decompose them too much and they lose\ntheir transformative essence, becoming mere technical skills\ndivorced from the values and systemic thinking that give them\nmeaning; leave them too holistic and they become impossible to assess, reducing credentials to vague attestations of exposure rather than evidence of capability. This tension between the\nholistic nature of sustainability competencies and the analytical\nrequirements of educational systems represents not merely a\ntechnical challenge to be solved, but a fundamental feature of\nsustainability education that must be navigated thoughtfully.\nOur approach to developing learning outcomes for GreenComp\nmust therefore acknowledge and work within these tensions\nrather than attempting to resolve them prematurely. The outcomes must be specific enough to guide educational practice and\nenable recognition, while remaining open enough to accommodate the emergent, contextual, and transformative nature of\nsustainability learning. They must honor the gestalt quality of\nsustainability competencies while providing sufficient structure\nfor implementation across diverse educational contexts. Most\ncritically, they must maintain the normative vision of sustainability transformation while respecting cultural diversity and\nlocal knowledge systems.\n\nThe OpenPass4Climate context\nThe OpenPass4Climate project (2022-1-FR01-KA220-HED000089354) provides the institutional context for this learning outcome development, with its focus on creating an open\nrecognition alliance system for sustainability competencies.\nThe project aims to tackle climate change by implementing innovative teaching and learning methods, with one of its\nkey objectives being the development of Open Badges for recognition of non-formal and informal learning related to sustainability competencies, ultimately leading to certification of\ncompetencies through Europass. This technological and institutional infrastructure shapes the requirements for learning\noutcomes, which must be granular enough for badge-based recognition while comprehensive enough to represent meaningful\n\n\ncompetency development. This presents a central methodological\nchallenge: leveraging the accessibility of micro-credentials\nwithout succumbing to the very reductionism that a holistic\nframework like GreenComp seeks to avoid. The methodology described below was designed explicitly to navigate this\ntension.\n\nMethodology\n\nThe development of learning outcomes for GreenComp\nrequired a methodological approach that could navigate multiple tensions: between theoretical comprehensiveness and\npractical applicability, between standardization and contextual\nflexibility, and between expert knowledge and stakeholder accessibility. A systematic, iterative approach was chosen, comprising four interconnected phases: initial co-design, first expert\nvalidation, coverage analysis, and second expert validation. This\nmulti-phase approach allowed for progressive refinement while\nmaintaining alignment with both the GreenComp framework\nand CEDEFOP guidelines for learning outcome development.\n\nPhase 1: Initial co-design process\nThe co-design phase represented a deliberate departure from\nexpert-driven approaches to learning outcome development.\nRather than beginning with technical specifications derived from\nlearning theory, we initiated a collaborative process across the\nfour OpenPass4Climate consortium institutions. Each institution assembled carefully balanced stakeholder groups: (1)\nat least three educators from formal education (university\nfaculty at UniLaSalle, NOVA, and University of Valladolid;\nvocational trainers at CSCI Novara); (2) at least three practitioners from non-formal settings, recruited from corporate\nsustainability professionals engaged in the project’s ‘Work\nPackage 5 - Impact evaluation, enlargement & policy recommendations’); and (3) at least five learners representing different educational levels, such as vocational and A-level students\n(CSCI Novara), Bachelor’s and Master’s students (UniLaSalle,\nNOVA), and doctoral candidates (University of Valladolid).\nLearner participants were volunteers selected via a convenience\nsampling approach from those involved in the prior surveys\nand focus groups of the project’s ‘Work Package 3 - Nature\n& assessment of engagement’ (Martín-Ramos et al., 2025).\nBy bringing together these diverse perspectives (spanning\neducational levels, professional contexts, and implementation\nsettings), we ensured the resulting learning outcomes would\nbe meaningful across the full spectrum of contexts where\nGreenComp might be applied.\nThe co-design process unfolded through a series of structured workshops conducted over three months. Each institution\nhosted four workshops (one per GreenComp competence\narea) online via Microsoft Teams and Miro collaborative\nplatform, conducted in local languages without time constraints to facilitate nuanced discussion. Participants worked in\ninterdisciplinary groups to translate abstract KSA statements\ninto concrete learning outcomes.\nTo structure the KSA translation process, we employed Bloom’s\nrevised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) as a heuristic device rather than a rigid framework. Participants were\n\nencouraged to consider how each competence might manifest at different cognitive levels, from basic comprehension\nto creative synthesis. However, we found that many sustainability competencies resisted easy categorization within Bloom’s\nframework, particularly those involving affective dimensions\nor systems thinking. This led to iterative refinement of outcomes that attempted to capture the holistic nature of competencies while maintaining sufficient specificity for assessment\npurposes.\nThe learning outcomes were formulated according to the Centre Européen pour le Développement de la Formation Professionnelle (CEDEFOP) guidelines for defining, writing and\napplying learning outcomes (CEDEFOP, 2022), ensuring they\nwere observable (describing a behavior or product that can\nbe observed), measurable (assessable using specific criteria),\nand student-centered (focusing on what the learner will be\nable to do). Each outcome was designed to integrate relevant knowledge, skills, and attitudes from the GreenComp\nframework, translating the abstract KSAs into concrete\nstatements of what learners should demonstrate.\nFollowing the institutional workshops, a cross-consortium\nsynthesis process was undertaken by project staff (without\nparticipation from learners or non-formal trainers) to consolidate the proposals. Translation of outputs from the local\nlanguages into English was validated by bilingual consortium\nmembers to preserve conceptual accuracy. Similar outcomes\nwere merged, and internal Delphi surveys were used to rank\nand select the top three learning outcomes for each of the 12\ncompetencies. The initial co-design phase produced 36\nlearning outcomes, three for each of the 12 GreenComp\ncompetencies.\n\nPhase 2: First expert validation round\nThe expert validation phase employed a modified Delphi\napproach designed to balance quality assurance with practical\nconstraints, such as the project timeline and expert availability.\nOur expert panel comprised 12 specialists (8 women, 4 men)\nselected based on demonstrated expertise in sustainability\neducation and familiarity with European competence\nframeworks. Selection criteria included authorship of GreenComp-related publications or reports, active engagement\nin the GreenComp Community, and participation in EU\nsustainability education projects such as TWIN-IN, ASSESS,\nEduC3, GrowLearnOut, GreenScent, GreenAtYou, and Entrepreneurship4All. Selection\nof the latter option triggered a text field for detailed feedback\nand improvement suggestions. Our review of existing approaches reveals a\nfragmented landscape characterized by partial coverage, methodological limitations, and implicit assumptions about the\nnature of sustainability competencies.\nThe dominant pattern across existing initiatives is the privileging of cognitive dimensions over affective and behavioral ones.\nTASK™ by Sulitest, despite its comprehensive alignment with\nGreenComp’s knowledge elements (100% coverage), achieves\nonly 70% coverage of skills and 30% of attitudes. This imbalance reflects not merely technical limitations but deeper\nassumptions about what constitutes legitimate educational\nassessment. The measurability of factual knowledge through\ntraditional testing formats creates path dependencies that\nmarginalize the transformative dimensions of sustainability\neducation.\nThis limitation is critical because, as Mezirow (1981) argues,\na core aim of education for sustainability must be to transform\nlearners’ perspectives, helping them reconsider and change\nproblematic frames of reference while reshaping fundamental\nattitudes. Our contribution responds directly to this transformative imperative. Unlike initiatives that primarily focus on knowledge assessment, the developed learning outcomes address\nall three dimensions of learning (cognitive, social-emotional,\nand behavioral) across all 12 GreenComp competencies. In\ncontrast to self-assessment tools, the learning outcomes are\ndesigned to be observable through specific behaviors or products and measurable using defined criteria. Rather than treating knowledge, skills, and attitudes as separate elements, the\nlearning outcomes integrate these components into holistic\nstatements of what learners should be able to demonstrate.\nThe philosophical grounding of our approach also distinguishes\nit from technocratic assessment initiatives. By explicitly incorporating learning outcomes that challenge anthropocentric\nworldviews and require engagement with environmental\nethics, we acknowledge that sustainability education cannot be\nvalue-neutral. The most fundamental challenge\nconcerns the tension between the holistic, transformative nature\nof sustainability competencies and the analytical requirements\nof learning outcome specifications. The learning outcomes are\npresented as discrete units, but sustainability competencies, with\n\ntheir emphasis on systems thinking, value transformation, and\ncollective action, resist decomposition into discrete, observable\nbehaviors.\nCultural and contextual variability presents another significant challenge. While our expert panel represented diverse\nEuropean contexts, the learning outcomes inevitably embed\ncertain cultural assumptions about sustainability, education, and\nassessment. What constitutes “supporting fairness” or “promoting\nnature” varies significantly across cultural contexts, and our formulations, despite attempts at inclusivity, reflect predominantly\n\n\nWestern European perspectives. Future research should therefore focus on validating and adapting these learning outcomes\nin non-European contexts, engaging with diverse cultural\nknowledge systems to enrich and decolonize the framework.\nThe assessment challenge remains particularly acute. While we\nhave specified observable and measurable dimensions for each\nlearning outcome, the actual assessment of complex competencies like “challenging anthropocentric perspectives” requires\nsophisticated approaches that go beyond traditional testing. Portfolio assessment, peer evaluation, and community-based projects\noffer possibilities, but these require resources and expertise\nthat may not be available in all educational contexts.\n\nImplications for practice\nThe implementation of these learning outcomes in educational\npractice requires consideration of context, resources, and pedagogical approaches. Educators implementing these outcomes\nshould view them not as rigid prescriptions but as flexible frameworks that can be adapted to local contexts while\nmaintaining alignment with GreenComp’s core intentions.\nDigital badge systems offer several affordances for competency recognition. Badges provide granular documentation of\nspecific competencies achieved (Hickey & Willis, 2017). They\ncan increase learner motivation through visible progress markers and social recognition (Abramovich et al., 2013). However,\nimplementation approaches vary significantly in their effectiveness. Some badge systems risk fragmenting holistic competencies into disconnected micro-credentials, potentially undermining\nthe integrated nature of sustainability education (Lockley\net al., 2016). This fragmentation echoes a broader challenge in\nsustainability education: the tendency toward isolated learning\nexperiences that fail to connect across disciplines, contexts, and\nreal-world applications (Christie et al., 2013; Sipos et al., 2008).\nStudents consistently report frustration with compartmentalized sustainability courses that don’t translate into integrated\nunderstanding or action (Hopkinson & James, 2010). More\neffective badge implementations recognize ‘constellations’\nof learning outcomes, requiring learners to demonstrate competence across cognitive, behavioral, and affective domains. For\ninstance, a badge might require combining a systems analysis\nwith a collective action initiative before a credential is awarded.\nThis approach better honors the holistic ‘gestalt’ nature of sustainability competence. However, the labor market reception of such\ncredentials remains mixed, with some employers valuing the specific competency documentation while others remain unfamiliar\nwith badge-based credentials (Carey & Stefaniak, 2018; Raish &\nRimland, 2016).\nProfessional development for educators represents perhaps\nthe most critical factor for the successful implementation of\nthese learning outcomes. The competencies assume educators who can facilitate complex learning processes, assess transformative capabilities, and navigate the inherently value-laden\nterrain of sustainability education (Annelin & Boström, 2024b).\nThis extends far beyond technical training in assessment methods.\nEducators require deep engagement with sustainability concepts,\n\nsystems thinking pedagogies, and transformative learning\napproaches (Burns, 2015; Sterling, 2024). They need support\nin developing facilitation skills for managing potentially contentious discussions about values, justice, and systemic\nchange. Furthermore, educators themselves must engage in\nthe same transformative learning processes they seek to facilitate, examining their own assumptions about human-nature\nrelationships and sustainability transitions (Moore, 2005).\nWithout substantial investment in such comprehensive professional development, even the most carefully designed learning outcomes risk being implemented in ways that perpetuate\ntransmissive rather than transformative education.\n\nFuture directions\nThe development of these learning outcomes for GreenComp\nrepresents a beginning rather than an endpoint. Future work\nshould focus on several critical areas to ensure their effective\nand equitable implementation: (1) extensive field-testing across\ndiverse educational contexts, (2) development of robust assessment frameworks and tools, (3) creation of comprehensive\nimplementation guides and supporting materials, (4) establishment of progression pathways for competency development over\ntime, and (5) integration with existing curriculum frameworks\nand qualification systems.\nA primary step is to conduct extensive field testing in diverse\neducational settings. This is essential not only to validate the\npractical utility of the learning outcomes but also to inform the\ndevelopment of guidance for their contextual adaptation. To\nensure the framework’s long-term relevance as sustainability challenges evolve, it would be prudent to establish a review\ncycle to periodically reassess its effectiveness.\nThe development of robust assessment frameworks is another\npriority. This includes creating tools and rubrics that can\ncapture the complexity of sustainability competencies while\nremaining practical for educators. Crucially, this work should\ninclude examples of low-resource assessment methods to ensure\napplicability across varied economic and institutional contexts.\nTo support educators, the creation of comprehensive implementation guides is vital. Such guides could be complemented by\nintegrated learning modules that address multiple competencies\nsimultaneously and supporting materials that address prerequisite knowledge requirements. Building on this, the creation of\nprogression pathways showing how learners can develop competencies over time would address a significant gap and would\nhelp educators design coherent learning sequences. While the\ncurrent framework avoids specifying rigid levels to maintain\nflexibility, future iterations could consider creating differentiated versions for different educational levels (e.g., primary,\nsecondary, vocational, higher education) to enhance usability.\nFinally, integration with existing curriculum frameworks and\nqualification systems presents both an opportunity and a challenge. The learning outcomes must be translated into language\nand structures that align with national and regional education\nsystems while maintaining their transformative potential. This\n\n\nrequires ongoing dialogue between European-level framework\ndevelopment and national implementation efforts.\n\nConclusions\n\nThis paper has presented the methodology and results of developing learning outcomes for the European sustainability competence framework (GreenComp) as part of the OpenPass4Climate\nproject. The development process, involving co-design among\neducational stakeholders, validation by an international panel\nof experts, and comprehensive coverage analysis, has produced 40 learning outcomes that operationalize GreenComp’s\ncompetencies while addressing critical gaps in the original\nframework.\nThe resulting learning outcomes represent more than technical specifications for assessment. They embody a vision of\nsustainability education that integrates cognitive understanding, emotional engagement, and behavioral transformation. By\nexplicitly incorporating philosophical dimensions, challenging anthropocentric assumptions, and connecting individual\nand collective action, they point toward a more transformative\napproach to sustainability education than purely skills-based\nframeworks allow.\nThe tensions revealed in this process (between standardization\nand contextualization, between measurability and transformation, between individual and systemic change) are not merely\ntechnical challenges to be resolved but fundamental features of\nsustainability education. The learning outcomes we have developed attempt to hold these tensions productively rather than\nresolve them prematurely.\nAs the climate crisis accelerates and planetary boundaries\nstrain, the urgency of sustainability education intensifies. Our\nlearning outcomes provide infrastructure for this educational\ntransformation, but infrastructure alone cannot create change.\nThe transformation requires educators willing to challenge disciplinary boundaries, institutions ready to restructure assessment paradigms, and societies prepared to question fundamental\nvalues. The learning outcomes are tools for this work, but the\nwork itself remains ahead.\nThe ultimate test of our framework will not be its technical\nelegance or theoretical sophistication but its capacity to\n\ncatalyze meaningful change. Do learners who develop these\ncompetencies act differently in the world? Do educational\nprograms using these outcomes produce graduates capable of\nsystemic thinking and collective action? Does the framework\ncontribute to the broader transformation toward sustainability?\nThese questions cannot be answered through methodology but\nonly through practice, experimentation, and ongoing reflection.\nIn offering these learning outcomes to the educational community,\nwe invite not just implementation but co-evolution. The framework should grow, adapt, and transform through use. It should\nincorporate perspectives excluded from our initial process. It\nshould respond to emerging sustainability challenges and educational innovations. Most fundamentally, it should serve not as an\nendpoint but as a catalyst for the ongoing work of creating\neducational systems adequate to our planetary moment.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\n\nData availability statement\n\nUVaDOC Documentary Repository of the University of Valladolid: Operationalizing the European sustainability competence\nframework: Development and validation of learning outcomes\nfor GreenComp.
