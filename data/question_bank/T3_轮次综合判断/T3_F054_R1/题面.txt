Living Labs can be defined\nas open innovation ecosystems in real-life operational environments which put citizens and/or end-users at the centre of the\ninnovation process (European Network of Living Labs, 2025).\nThey promote a systematic user co-creation approach that\nintegrates research and innovation activities in communities and/or multi-stakeholder environments. The agreement was signed by the project lead and the visiting researchers,\nhereby providing active written informed consent for participation in the FTT questionnaires.\nPre- and post-survey questionnaires\nBefore participating in the Transnational Access and the FTT, participants were asked to indicate which of the 5 topics of\nthe FTT they considered most relevant for them or their team (“During Transnational Access, a 2-day Fast Track Training\nprogram is provided comprising the following topics. Which topics do you consider most relevant to you/your team\n(Multiple answers possible)?”). A second survey was conducted after completing the FTT to evaluate its content. The\n\n\n\nsurvey questions can be consulted at Helsen et al. (2025). Surveys consisted of multiple choice questions, rating scales,\nand open-ended questions. The topics of the evaluation questionnaire included self-reported satisfaction with the training\nas a whole, perceived quality and usefulness evaluation for the fixed training components, and perceived confidence in\napplying the Living Lab concepts and methodologies in the researcher’s context after the training. To extend beyond\naddressing desirable features and allow to assess relative preferences, participants were also asked which topic they\nwould not attend again. Multiple answers could be indicated, but they were forced to choose at least one topic to avoid the\nsocially desirable answer ‘none of the above’. The questionnaire also included open question on topics they would like to\nadd and the most appreciated elements of the training.\nTraining and data collection procedure\nAfter (teams of) researchers were selected for the transnational access for one of the living labs of the VITALISE project,\nthey signed a service agreement including written informed consent for completing the questionnaires. Next, a short\nquestionnaire was sent to the researchers to monitor expectations and help prepare the agenda for the research stay. The\nschedule of their visit to the living lab infrastructure was prepared. The training was planned and prepared by the local\nliving labs, based on the materials provided by the lead program developer. The program could include a welcoming\nsession, five main blocks and one optional block. However, an abbreviated program could also be provided if some\nelements were not deemed necessary based on the profile, interests and expectations of the visiting researchers. The\ntraining was offered during the transnational access visit or online. An evaluation questionnaire was provided after\ncompletion of the program. Some participants only completed the expectations survey. Potential reasons were not\nattending the transnational access or training and not responding to the survey invitations.\nData analysis\nSPSS 28.0 was used for calculating averages for the ratings provided by the participants. A Friedman Test (with follow-up\nWilcoxon Signed Ranks Test) was used to investigate differences between the rating for the training components.\nBonferroni adjustment was used to correct for multiple tests. Since not all training blocks were provided to all\nparticipants, some responses were based on a subsample of respondents. Open ended questions with qualitative data\nwere organised and grouped using thematic and content analyses. The initial thematic analysis was conducted by the first\nauthor. After gaining familiarity with the data through a thorough review of the responses to understand their context and\ntone, the recurring responses were identified, tagged, and grouped where possible. A second researcher subsequently\nassessed the alignment of the responses with the identified themes and proposed modifications or additional themes\nwhere appropriate to reduce potential bias.\nResults\nThe Fast-Track Training program (FTT)\nThe full FTT program was structured into six blocks (Figure 2). The introductory segment lasted approximately one hour.\nThe methodology block was divided into two parts with a combined duration of 2 hours and 30 minutes. The remaining\nblocks were completed in roughly 1 hour and 30 minutes. The program could be tailored to the needs of the visiting\nresearcher, which implies that not all blocks were presented to all participants.\n\nFigure 2. Overview of the training elements.\n\n\n\nIn the welcoming part, both the visiting researchers and the hosting living labs introduced themselves. An icebreaker\nexercise helped to set the tone. The icebreaker is an animation technique that makes participants more conducive to\ncreativity and sharing. Typically carried out at the start of a meeting, it is an exercise that aims to relax the atmosphere and\nput the participants in a state of mind of openness necessary for the creative posture.\nThe second block aimed to provide a broad introduction in the world of Living Labs. The participants discovered for\nexample what defines a Living Lab, which roles and actors can be found in the Living Lab context, and which services\ncould be provided. Another important topic in this block was the ethical framework which had proven to be increasingly\nimportant in an innovation context where products, contexts and legislation can change at a quick pace. The block\nconcluded with the discussion of ethical challenges and how to tackle them. Learning objectives consisted of getting\nfamiliar with living lab terminology and operations. Moreover, we aimed to encourage participants to reflect on ethical\nquestions that might be relevant for their own research.\nThe third block was about the importance of harmonisation, which is the core business of the VITALISE project. Living\nLabs can use a variety of procedures operationalised in different ways. A harmonisation of the implemented services,\ninfrastructures and language can help to achieve more cost efficiency, optimal use of time and resources and wider\nexploitation of research results from local communities to offer their valuable services in the Research and Development\ncommunity. Therefore, the instructors guided the researchers through the objectives, challenges and different work\npackages of the VITALISE project, putting the focus on the different joint research activities to be conducted throughout\nthe project. By the end of this training block, participants were able to explain the importance of harmonisation in Living\nLabs and describe how the VITALISE project’s objectives, work packages, and joint research activities contribute to\nmore efficient use of resources and broader exploitation of research results.\nThe second training day started with a theoretical block on methodologies, followed by a methodology workshop.\nStarting from a design thinking perspective, different methods and tools were discussed. In the sixth block, theory was put\ninto practice with a methodology workshop. Three options were possible: 1) The hosting living lab supported the visiting\nresearchers with further elaboration of their study to be conducted during the visit, 2) the hosting living lab demonstrated a\nmethod or tool of their choice, or 3) The UX design speed testing template, provided by one of the consortium partners,\nwas used as an example. Learning objectives consisted of getting acquainted with different phases in design thinking and\nmethods and tools suitable to be used across the process.\nThe fifth block comprised the issue of panel management and how to engage target groups. Recruitment strategies were\nexamined as well as the motivators and barriers to participate for users. The benefit of having a panel database was\nexplained. Trainers elaborated on how to start building up a panel and how to engage users on a long term. Finally, it also\nincluded how to monitor your panel activities. Learning objectives consisted of being able to recruit, engage, and manage\nuser panels effectively, including monitoring participation over time.\nThe final block is a free block with content depending on the hosting living lab’s own expertise or best practices. As an\nexample, a training block on how to work with companies and business model preparation was included in Belgium.\nThe complete set of educational materials prepared for the VITALISE Fast Track Training is also publicly available in the\nproject website: [external resource identifier omitted]\nFTT survey results\nExpectations\nA total of 102 researchers who intended to visit the living labs were invited to complete the survey before the visit and\n91 participants could be included in the expectations questionnaire. Additionally, nineteen participants referred to specific content of the FTT training blocks. A wide variety of\nFTT topics was mentioned in this respect, which reflects the diversity of the interests of external researchers. The third\ncategory concerned the acquisition of new knowledge and insights in an efficient way (N = 14). Finally, seven researchers\nmentioned the flexible, on demand approach of the FTT, with even the opportunity to have the training (partly) online.\nSome additional strengths were mentioned in the general remarks section. Topics could also be expanded with more descriptions of use cases conducted by the hosting\nliving lab. Some participants mentioned the need to gain\ninsight into what kind of collaboration could be expected from Living Labs in future, referring to networking\ncollaborations, opportunities to collaborate in future (EU-)projects or in shared value models.\nDiscussion\nLiving Labs have demonstrated their potential for providing added value for innovation development (Hossain et al.,\n2019; Santonen et al., 2024a; Ståhlbröst, 2013). However, many researchers and companies lack the knowledge to\nimplement such methodologies in a sustainable way. This work presents and evaluates a novel Living Lab training\nprogram designed to onboard external researchers and familiarize them with Living Lab Research Infrastructures in the\ncontext of transnational visits and collaborations.\nThe current study introduces a flexible, short-term approach targeting more experienced audiences with existing expertise\nin research. This study proposes five fixed training blocks focused on introducing the participants to Living Labs, panel\nmanagement, methodologies in theory and methodologies in practice and adding the element of harmonisation that is\nconsidered important for research infrastructure users (Santonen et al., 2024b). The training was evaluated positively in a\npost-training participant survey. Participants were very satisfied with the training and the perceived quality and\nusefulness of all fixed blocks was high. After the training, most participants reported high perceived confidence levels\nin applying Living Lab methodologies. The training was rated to be at least as they expected but mostly better than\nexpected.\nMoreover, experiential learning has demonstrated great potential in learning and training (Hecht & Maass, 2008; Kang\net al., 2022). Many participants reported that the interactions with the trainers were a strength of the program. Suggestions\nfor improvement mainly concerned making the training even more interactive, with workshops showcasing tools and\ninstruments concerning all training themes. Some (more junior) researchers expressed the wish to have extra sessions\nwith in-depth discussions on their own research project, whereas for other (senior) researchers this was less relevant.\n\n\nParticipants also differed in which specific training blocks they appreciated most, which illustrates that not all participants\nhad the same needs in terms of training. These findings underscore the importance of offering flexible and tailored\ntraining programs that address both fundamental knowledge and practical application, ensuring relevance to the diverse\nneeds of researchers in Living Lab environments. For example, while the introduction to Living Labs received the highest\nrelevance scores before the training, it received a lower relative preference post-training since it was also the block that\nmost participants selected to remove when they had to. This appears to relate to the fact that this is the most general block\nand other blocks will extend insights from this block hereby potentially making it somewhat redundant when the\ncomplete training is provided. This ‘on demand’ choice of training blocks based on the researcher’s needs was very much\nappreciated. To enhance the training flexibility, the inclusion of one open block was also proposed that can be\npersonalized to the user needs and adapted to the Living Lab expertise. As the need for assistance and the time required\nto process new information differ between novices and experts (Persky & Robinson, 2017), the importance of tailoring\nthe content and delivery of the program to learners’ levels of expertise is acknowledged.\nGiven that Living Labs have faced challenges in economic sustainability (Fauth et al., 2024; Paskaleva & Cooper, 2021),\ncareful attention must be paid to the cost-effectiveness of the FTT program. Having the current open-access training\nmaterials available already increases the feasibility of offering training since it reduces preparation times (and according\ncosts), which are now limited to familiarizing oneself to program slides and content and reflecting on previous\nexperiences that a trainer might share. Implementing the current training program itself, is expected to take around\n2 days (with around 8 hours of lecture materials without the free block). Due to its modular and flexible approach,\nabbreviated formats are also possible.\nA significant challenge in the effective implementation of Living Labs lies in the lack of unified evaluation frameworks,\nwhich has been widely recognized in the literature (Chen & Chou, 2010; Kovács, 2015). A set of harmonized criteria for\nLiving Lab evaluation have been recently proposed and put into practice by ENoLL (Vervoort et al., 2022). Aligning the\ntraining aspects and modules with these criteria is crucial for the systematic advancement of Living Lab research. The\nFTT program presented in this study incorporates training components that emphasize not only the theoretical\nfoundations but also practical aspects such as governance, participant engagement, and methodological adaptability;\ndimensions identified as critical for Living Lab evaluation (Berberi et al., 2023; Hossain et al., 2019). The modular design\nof the training program aligns with recommendations to adapt evaluation models to diverse contexts and participant\nneeds, ensuring relevance and applicability. However, this flexibility does complicate measurement of learning gains and\nimpact assessment. Developing practical toolkits for Living Labs and their evaluation can provide valuable resources for\nestablishing new Living Labs and projects, supporting wider uptake and standardized best practices (Berberi et al., 2023).\nSome other recent initiatives have also started to address the gap in training, such as the international training school on\n“Knowledge transfer in and through Living Labs” which emphasized peer learning and methodological exchange across\nLiving Lab practitioners (Rohde et al., 2024). Recently ENoLL has also launched the ENoLL Living Labbers Academy\naiming at expanding the knowledge base of interested parties at a newcomer, intermediate, advanced, and expert level.\nSuch a training offered by an international non-profit network organization can be an important step towards developing a\nsystematic training approach for Living Lab methodologies that still remain scarce. A study by Diamantis et al. (2024) has\nalso proposed the design of a master course curriculum targeting innovation through Living Labs. In alignment with the\nproposed FTT structure, the curriculum included sections like panel management and the Living Lab methodology,\nincluding co-creation sessions, focus groups, experimentation and validation techniques. Konstantinidis et al. (2021)\nfocused on the introduction of Living Lab concepts such as co-creation to undergraduate students with promising results\nfrom the evaluation from the students’ perspective, focusing again on co-creation aspects but adapting to a less\nexperienced audience.\nThere are also some limitations to this study. The training could be adapted to the needs of the participants and was\nprovided by local living lab staff. While all trainers had been informed about the training protocol and used the same\ntraining materials, there will have been differences in the exact operationalization of the training and blocks. Such a\nflexible tailor-made approach can be a strength for participants but also reduces standardization and can complicate\ntraining evaluation. While variation in implementation limited precise assessment of exposure, it reflects real-world\npractice, and positive perceptions across sites suggest the program’s acceptability across diverse conditions. The study\ndid not employ a formal pre–post design and relied on self-reported post-training perceptions, limiting conclusions about\nlearning gains. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of perceived usefulness, acceptability, and\nfeasibility rather than validation of accelerating expertise or increasing confidence. The study used a small sample (not\nincluding all training participants which implies vulnerability for self-selection) and was situated within the healthcare\ndomain. To ensure generalizability, It would be beneficial to continue training initiatives and continuously evaluate and\nimprove the content, especially when transferring to different contexts. The current training was offered free of charge in\n\n\nthe context of subsidised collaborative research activities. This could have biased results. Finally, the survey responses\nreflected participants’ perceptions at a single time point, and thus, the long-term impact and usefulness of applying the\nknowledge in practice could not be measured. A follow-up study is recommended to assess sustained effects and to\nidentify potential new Living Lab projects initiated by FTT program participants.\nThis study contributes to the growing body of work aimed at promoting user-centred approaches in research and\ninnovation management. A flexible, modular training program was designed to help cater to the diverse needs of\nresearchers. The positive self-reported evaluation results and high perceived confidence levels support the potential\nadded value of experiential and customizable learning formats. However, further research is needed to establish formal\nlearning gains and long-term impacts of the training, as well as explore learning gains in diverse samples. By aligning\ntraining content with emerging harmonisation efforts and evaluation frameworks, such as those proposed by ENoLL, the\nprogram could contribute to the systematic advancement of Living Lab methodologies. Continued development of\nstructured, scalable, and context-sensitive training programs, supported by international collaborations and standardized\napproaches, could play a role in fostering sustainable and impactful Living Lab research across disciplines and borders,\nalthough more research is needed.\nEthics statement\nThe current procedures and materials were presented to the VITALISE internal ethical board and the data controller.\nFurther ethical approval was not sought for the present study because it was an observational study collecting nonmedical and non-sensitive data (feedback on training) from adult participants who voluntarily applied to take part in the\nVITALISE Horizon Europe project to participate in Transnational Access activities.
