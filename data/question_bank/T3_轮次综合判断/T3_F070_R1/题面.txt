Finally, there\nis a small percentage (around 5%) of studies that combine\nqualitative and quantitative methods.\nExamining the relationship between data collection methods\nand analysis techniques reveals a significant relationship\nbetween them. For example, attitude surveys are usually analysed using multivariate quantitative techniques. Content analysis\nand group experiments are used for both qualitative and\nquantitative data. Interviews and documentary analyses,\nwhich are primarily associated with inductive qualitative\nmethods, are used to analyse discourses and narratives in\n\narticles that seek to understand how disinformation narratives\nare constructed and perceived.\n\nNature of conclusions\nThe analysis of the conclusions (R.Q.3.1) reached by the\nstudies analysed can be divided into those that explain the\nconditioning factors for the processing of information about\nclimate change and the impact of disinformation about climate\nchange on the public.\nConditioning factors that modulate the processing of climate\nchange information\nIn terms of factors influencing the public’s propensity to\nbe influenced by misinformation about climate change and\nthe impact of interventions aimed at creating positive attitudes towards climate science, the studies reviewed primarily\npoint to the importance of experiential context as a determinant (Benegal & Scruggs, 2018; Gruener, 2022; Sambrook\net al., 2021; Schmid-Petri & Bürger, 2022), cognitive factors\nrelated to information processing (Zhou & Shen, 2021),\nthe level of existing knowledge about climate change (Lutzke\net al., 2019), the choice of topics addressed (Gruener, 2022)\nand the perception of risk associated with the impact and\nconsequences of climate change (Moscato & Valencia, 2023;\nRuiu & Ragnedda, 2021; Sambrook et al., 2021). According\nto Van der Linden et al. (2017), partisan ideology ranks above\nmotivated reasoning in media selection and content consumption, which is also related to political polarization (Feldman\net al., 2012; Krosnick & MacInnis, 2015; Leiserowitz\net al., 2013; McCright et al., 2015). Research by McBeth,\nLybecker and Sargent (2022), in line with the results of\nBenegal and Scrugg (2018), questions the predictive power\nof political ideology for climate attitudes. Their study showed\nno strict correlation between conservative political identity\nand climate skepticism and found evidence that individuals\ncan be influenced by the narrator’s identity through empathy,\nregardless of their ideological attribution.\nFor their part, Lutzke et al. (2019) point to the low level of\nclimate-specific knowledge as a primary obstacle to climate\nchange mitigation measures. Gruener (2022) emphasizes\nthe importance of the topics addressed in the content over\nother factors such as trust in social networks, the degree of\nperceived risk, the evidence contained in the messages or the\nway our thinking system adjusts to the information we receive.\nLai et al. (2022) link the presence of earlier physiological\nprocesses such as gestation to greater susceptibility to\nclimate change disinformation and willingness to share it.\nOn the other hand, the temporal and spatial proximity of the\nmeteorological events and their severity also influence the impact\nof the measures taken. The notion of human catastrophe in the\ncontext of climate change distances the understanding of its\nimpact from the individual realities of the public (Moscato &\nValencia, 2023). The adverse effects are presented in relation to unknown people and distant regions (Ruiu & Ragnedda,\n2021). This leads to a global dilution of the problem and\nlimits its impact to the audience, which is free from all kinds of\n\n\nemotional involvement and individual responsibility. Sambrook\net al. (2021) argue, based on behavioural decision research,\nthat extreme weather events only have an impact on\njudgments and decisions if they have occurred recently, and\nalways depending on how strong the subjects’ pre-existing\nbeliefs about the reality of climate change are.\nThe extent of the perceived risk also affects the persuasiveness\nof the content. The evidence found by Dahlstrom and Rosenthal\n(2018) suggests that negative narratives about climate\nchange are more likely to be ignored by audiences, while\nmore neutral narratives have a greater chance of resonating\nwith audiences because their impact is underestimated.\nOther studies (Zhou & Shen, 2021) confirmed that scientific\nevidence and misinformation can be processed differently.\nThus, in the case of scientific evidence, the factuality of the\nmessage and the credibility of the source would influence\nattitudes after exposure to the message, in contrast to\nempathy, which would have no effect on scientific evidence.\nIn contrast, perceived factuality of misinformation would have\nno effect on post-message attitudes, unlike empathy, where\na direct effect would be found. These results would be\nexplained by differences in the way scientific information is\nprocessed, which is more systematic, as opposed to false\ninformation, which is processed relatively superficially and\nautomatically.\nMalena-Chan (2019) presents the problems of inaction in\nthe face of climate change because of the cognitive dissonance that subjects exhibited when confronted with narratives\nthat encouraged involvement in environmental matters. Such\nan effect was present even among individuals with sufficient\nknowledge and personal motivation about climate change,\ndue to emotional factors and moral contradictions they\nmay experience at key moments of action (knowledge and\nunderstanding of climate change, agency and responsibility,\nand ability and action on the issue).\nEffects of climate change-related misinformation on audiences\nSome studies showed contradictory results on the impact\nand consequences of public misinformation. The experiment\nconducted by Drummond et al. (2020) found a moderate\neffect associated with exposure on three variables (belief in\nclimate change, scientific consensus, and belief in scientists)\nand several covariates related to demographics and political\nideology. While demographic characteristics and political ideology were stronger predictors than belief, the discreteness\nof the results allowed us to conclude that exposure to climate\nfake news is unlikely to strongly influence climate scepticism.\nIn contrast to the previous study, Wolff and Taddicken (2022)\nconcluded that unbiased users are not completely resistant to\nthe influence of climate change misinformation. A proactive\nstance in seeking information that confirms their initial position may explain this increased resistance. For their part, Van der\nLinden et al. (2017) found that misinformation can cancel\nout the effectiveness of scientific consensus communication\non climate change when both are presented together.\n\nKoch et al. (2021) found evidence in an experiment conducted\nin Germany on the effectiveness of warning labels, which\nled them to conclude that people with left-wing ideology are\nmore likely to be influenced and share misinformation. These\nfindings contribute to the growing evidence for the effectiveness of cues, but at the same time question their relevance for\nsystematic information processing versus motivated reasoning.\nLawrence and Estow (2017) found that individuals with\na liberal ideology were more likely to react with frustration and respond with commentary to information opposing\nclimate change. However, political orientation was not related\nto the tone of the comments, suggesting a degree of selfcensorship among these individuals. The study also concluded\nthat the preferred strategy was to use corrective information in\ncomments rather than calling for collaboration. Conservatives\nwere more likely to stick to information against climate change\n(backfire) than calls for collaboration in cases where the\ncomment contained corrective information. It is therefore\nhypothesized that the call for collaboration may prove to be\na more effective strategy as it invites dialog which allows for\nincreased knowledge of the scientific consensus, which would\nimpact pre-existing beliefs (Goldberg et al., 2019)\nFreiling and Matthes (2023) found that user corrections\nwere positively related to anger about climate change, which\nin turn was positively related to environmental activism.\nHowever, the data did not show a relationship between\nanger and correctness, nor between anger and environmental\nactivism.\n\nSolutions and operational recommendations offered\nThe solutions and recommendations (R.Q.3.2) offered in\nthe studies to combat climate change misinformation can be\ncategorized into two groups: the effectiveness of fact-checking\nand audience inoculation strategies, and technological\nand methodological solutions for designing intervention\nmodels.\nEffectiveness of fact-checking and audience inoculation\nstrategies\nRegarding the positive effects of fact-checking, Porter et al.\n(2019) found evidence supporting the idea that party affiliation\ninfluences beliefs about climate change but is not determinant\nof their formation. For example, positive effects on corrective\ninformation were found for Trump’s posted messages on\nclimate change, although the influence was found to be greater\nfor Democrats. These effects could not be replicated for\nmessages related to environmental regulation. The results\ncould find further explanation in the findings of Allen &\nMcAleer (2018), who found a predominantly negative emotion\nin Trump’s messages as well as a lack of a clear logical\nframework at the discursive level, suggesting which Trump\nis starting from a false understanding of climate change by\nconfusing it with the concept of global warming.\nBenegal and Scruggs (2018) challenge the premise of party\naffiliation as a predictor of climate change attitudes, finding\nevidence that bipartisan correctness leads Republicans to\n\n\nexpress much greater support for the scientific consensus on\nclimate change. This evidence thus suggests that it is possible\nto narrow the gap between Republicans and Democrats.\nSeveral studies looked at the benefits and limitations of\nvaccination as a solution to combat misinformation about\nclimate change. Van der Linden et al. (2017) found that the\neffectiveness of communication about the scientific consensus was nullified when presented in conjunction with\nmisinformation. The authors saw positive effects in the use\nof vaccination messages to enhance the impact of scientific\nconsensus messages. Subsequent studies (Maertens et al.,\n2020; Williams & Bond, 2020) have replicated earlier\nresearch findings, finding results that qualify or challenge the\nvan der Linden et al. (2017) findings. Maertens et al. (2020)\nincluded a one-week period between vaccination and misinformation exposure in their design. The results suggest\nthat the consensus message may be strong enough to remain\nsignificant on its own without the need for vaccination as\nadditional protection, although differences are observed when\nfollowed by vaccination. In line with these results, it could\nbe argued that since the consensus message is presented\nbefore the misinformation, the former could have a vaccine\neffect. In another replication of the same study, Williams and\nBond (2020) found that providing information about the scientific consensus on climate change increased perceptions\nof the scientific consensus as much as inoculation, which\noccurred prior to the provision of misinformation. However,\nthe authors were unable to replicate the finding that vaccination\nmessages counteracted the effects of misinformation to a\ngreater extent than the provision of information about the\nscientific consensus alone.\nOther authors point out that the effectiveness of vaccination is related to the appropriateness of the messages for the\ncontext of the audience (Green et al., 2022; Lewandowsky,\n2021; Treen et al., 2020), which is why effective designs for\nvaccination education are needed. It is also hypothesized that\nthe effectiveness of vaccination messages depends on the\ntopics addressed and their potential impact on the target\naudiences (Schmid-Petri & Bürger, 2022; Treen et al., 2020).\nBased on these findings, further research would be needed to\ndevelop more effective vaccination education designs (Green\net al., 2022).\nTechnological and methodological solutions for the design\nof intervention models\nFrom the analysed selection, various studies deal with the\npossibilities offered by technologies in climate science\ncommunication. Authors such as Brannon et al. (2021) emphasize the suitability of interactive journalistic products to present\nvaccination messages, and particularly the use of I-Docs\ntogether with gamification techniques. The application of\ncomputational methods in analysing the phenomenon of\nclimate change communication would also have proven\nuseful in identifying the institutional structures that fund and\ncoordinate scientific misinformation campaigns.\n\nThe application of computational methods (natural language\nprocessing, machine learning and social network analysis)\nto the analysis of content has led to very interesting results.\nFor example, research by Farrell et al. (2019) and Coan\net al. (2021) clearly identified the financial and political roots\non which denial discourses are based. Bhatia et al., 2021 have\napplied supervised and semi-supervised computer models\nbased on BERT to identify neutralization techniques used by\nclimate change deniers.\nOther work looks at the effectiveness of narratives to increase\nknowledge about climate change and counter misinformation. Some authors suggest the use of bottom-up models\nto demonstrate the impact of climate change on individual\neveryday experiences (Howarth & Parsons, 2021; Ogra,\n2022). Authors such as McQuaid et al. (2018) conclude that\ncitizen participation in policy development needs to be encouraged and that communication is more effective when it does\nnot originate from the authorities (Bevan et al., 2020). These\nsuggestions are also supported by the findings of studies\nexamining climate change communication at the local level.\nSuch studies point to the need to involve citizens in both the\nproblem and the solution, moving away from their usual victim\nrole, while increasing scientific knowledge about climate change\n(Ejaz et al., 2021; Naguimbing-Manlulu, 2021).\nOther studies point to the ineffectiveness of narratives that\nexploit fear or offer an overly catastrophic view of the consequences of climate change. On this aspect, Segal (2017)\nsuggests that utopian or dystopian narratives leave audiences\nstuck in the debate between extremes. Alternative models of\nnarratives are proposed by Herr (2022), who points to a design\nbased on the concept of ecological hope, as opposed to the\ndominant concept of fear. For their part, Choi and Leckie\n(2018) focus on exploring the slow causalities of geological\nand generational processes, for which there is little empirical\nevidence, and Largo Loayza (2022) focuses on the design of\nmodels of transmedia narratives and on the integration of\nstorytelling.\nLocal studies: media coverage of climate change and the spread\nof misinformation in social networks\nOf the sample analysed, a significant percentage of the\narticles (42.7% n=32) deal with the phenomenon from\na local perspective. These are mainly qualitative studies\ndealing with the analysis of the narratives from a structural and\nthematic point of view, the actors involved (promoters and\nrecipients) and the disinformation and fact-checking strategies.\nThe selected sample allows an approach to the phenomenon\nfrom a broad geographical perspective. Most of the studies focus\non the United States (n=11) and Canada (n=2), on European\ncountries such as the United Kingdom (n=3), Germany\n(n=2), the Baltic countries (n=2) and Sweden (n=1), on\nBRICS countries such as Brazil (n=3), China (n=2) and India\n(n=2), and on other recently emerging countries such as\nMalaysia (n=1) or developing countries such as Pakistan (n=1)\nand Uganda (n=1).\n\n\n\nSome of these studies (n=6) correspond to content analysis of\nnews from local newspapers in the Philippines (NaguimbingManlulu, 2021), Malaysia (Hassan et al., 2023), Pakistan\n(Ejaz et al., 2021), Latvia (Kleinberga et al., 2023), the USA\nand China (Moscato & Valencia, 2023) and the UK (Ruiu &\nRagnedda, 2021). Other work draws on surveys (n=4) (Chu\net al., 2023; Cia Alves et al., 2022; Hong, 2020; Ogra, 2022;\nRuiu & Ragnedda, 2021) or interviews (n=1) (McQuaid et al.,\n2018) to identify how citizens perceive the problem, or\nwebsite and social network analysis (Al-Rawi et al., 2021)\nto examine misinformation and its impact. There are also\nexamples of pre-designed experiments (de Almeida Soares &\nGomes Barreto, 2022; Drummond et al., 2020; Koch et al.,\n2021; Lawrence & Estow, 2017; Vu et al., 2022) that look at the\nimpact of misinformation and the effectiveness of remedies.\nFinally, other papers (n=2) aim to debunk models of narratives.\nThe media is often portrayed as amplifying the discourse of\npolitical elites in relation to climate change (Ejaz et al.,\n2021; Hassan et al., 2023; Kleinberga et al., 2023;\nNaguimbing-Manlulu, 2021; Silva, 2022), with the patterns\nbeing the same across countries (Dunwoody & Peters, 1992).\nLittle or no attention is paid to the average citizen, who is\nnot seen as part of the problem, and in some cases is portrayed\nin a victim role (Naguimbing-Manlulu, 2021). Similarly,\nthere is a tendency to present climate change in a global\ndimension without addressing its local impacts (McQuaid\net al., 2018), nor the complexity of mitigation and adaptation approaches (Kleinberga et al., 2023). In this sense, climate reporting has gone beyond the norm of balance to\nbecome interpretive journalism, as Brüggemann and Engesser\n(2017) have noted. Also common is the lack of scientific\nexplanations or individually implementable solutions (Ejaz\net al., 2021; Naguimbing-Manlulu, 2021), leading to a lack\nof citizen engagement.\nThis discursive monopoly would also enable the spread of disinformation (Allen & McAleer, 2018; Hassan et al., 2023;\nPorter et al., 2019; Silva, 2022) embedded in political propaganda, with the case of Bolsonaro in Brazil or Trump in the\nUS being clear representatives of this strategy (Silva, 2022).\nBased on the topics addressed in the climate change content\nreviews, the main areas of disinformation can be identified.\n\nDiscussion\n\nThe analysis of the objects of study, topics and theories under\nwhich the object of study is treated reflects a trend towards\na multidimensional understanding of the problem. However,\nalthough recent studies deal with a variety of objects and topics and draw on a wide range of theories, there is a notable\ndisconnect from the financial, economic and political roots of\ndisinformation.\nFindings suggest that despite interest in understanding the\npersonal and contextual dynamics that foster climate disinformation (Benegal & Scruggs, 2018; Sambrook et al.,\n2021; Schmid-Petri & Bürger, 2022), and efforts to develop\ntechnologies and methods to detect and neutralize it\n(Gruener, 2022; Lutzke et al., 2019), there is a limitation in\n\nlooking at the phenomenon from a systemic perspective that\nincludes financial, economic and political dimensions, as\ndocumented in previous studies on the influence of the hydrocarbon industry on the development of climate disinformation\n(Oreskes & Conway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes, 2017). This\nfragmented approach contrasts with evidence showing that\ndisinformation was a coordinated strategy used by actors with\nspecific economic and political interests to influence public\nperceptions and policies on climate change (Gelbspan,\n1997; Gore, 1993). In this sense, the literature has emphasized the importance of the financial, economic and political\nroots behind climate obstructionism (Almiron & Moreno,\n2022; Dunlap & McCright, 2011), suggesting that an effective understanding of the phenomenon requires an analysis\nthat integrates these dimensions in a systemic manner.\nThis confirms the relevance of hypothesis 1 and underlines the\nneed for more integrative research approaches that not only\naddress the technological and methodological aspects of climate\nmisinformation, but also consider the power and funding\nstructures that support and promote these narratives. Thus,\nby focusing on concrete and procedural aspects or mitigation\ntechniques rather than global approaches, there is still a significant gap in the systematic and global coverage of the topic.\nTherefore, H1 is only partially confirmed, suggesting that\nsystemic analysis needs to be further integrated into climate\ndisinformation research. In this sense, studies should be\ndeveloped that bring together methodological advances in\nthe detection of misinformation with a critical analysis of the\neconomic and political forces that perpetuate it, as suggested\nby the findings of Farrell et al. (2019) and the proposals for\nstrategies to combat scientific misinformation by Oreskes &\nConway (2011) and Supran & Oreskes (2017).\nIn terms of research methods and techniques, they show\nthat although a diversity of methods and techniques for data\ncollection and analysis can be observed in the recent literature, the ability of these investigations to translate into\noperational solutions and recommendations still offers\nsignificant scientific challenges.\nThe studies examined use a variety of methodological\napproaches, including content and discourse analysis, surveys,\nand experiments (Cook et al., 2015; Farrell et al., 2019). This\ndiversity of methods reflects the complexity of the phenomenon\nof climate misinformation and the need for multidisciplinary\napproaches to capture its various dimensions. However, the\nimpact of these methods on the development of effective\noperational solutions is limited. In any case, the challenges\nin combating climate disinformation are not only epistemological, but also structural and political. Previous literature has\nemphasized the importance of understanding disinformation as\npart of a broader obstructionist strategy funded and promoted\nby specific economic and geopolitical interests (Oreskes &\nConway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes, 2017). Therefore, the methods used need to be broadened to include critical analyses\nof the power and funding systems that support climate\ndisinformation. This involves not only identifying disinformation\ntactics, but also the actors, networks and resources that facilitate them. These aspects have been emphasized in works\n\n\nsuch as those by Dunlap and McCright (2011) and Almiron &\nMoreno (2022).\nThe variety of methods and data collection and analysis techniques used in the research reflects the effort to adapt the study\nmethods to the complexity of the phenomenon of climate\nmisinformation. However, the relationship between the choice\nof methods and the ability to identify and propose effective\nsolutions is not explicitly analysed in the results presented.\nH2 is only partially confirmed, showing that despite\nmethodological efforts in this direction, the effectiveness of\nresearch in translating its contributions into concrete and\neffective operational solutions is not uniform.\nFinally, analysing the findings and the solutions and recommendations from the perspective that it is important to consider\nclimate disinformation not only as an isolated phenomenon,\nbut as part of a broader structure of climate obstructionism\nunderpinned by financial, economic and political roots, shows\nthat obstructionism in relation to climate action is deeply rooted\nin economic and political interests aimed at maintaining the\nstatus quo (Oreskes & Conway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes,\n2017). The effectiveness of interventions against climate\nmisinformation therefore depends on the ability of research\nto understand and address these underlying dynamics and\nwhether it can demonstrate this systemic understanding of the\nphenomenon. This is supported by recent research that points\nto the need to implement communication and education strategies that not only warn against misinformation and disinformation, but also build citizen resilience (Cook et al., 2015;\nvan der Linden et al., 2017). However, there is a significant\ngap in the existing literature, which lacks more comprehensive approaches that combine critical analyses of the financial,\neconomic and political roots of misinformation with concrete\nproposals for action. Although some studies have begun to\nexplore these relationships, there is still a lack of greater\ninclusion of interdisciplinary analyses that can provide a\ncomplete picture of how climate disinformation is produced\nand disseminated and how it can be effectively combated.\nIn this sense, despite considerable progress, the ability to\nprovide operational recommendations that encompass the\ncomplexity of climate disinformation is limited, so H3 is only\npartially confirmed, as more systematized approaches are\nneeded that can effectively influence policies and practice\nagainst climate disinformation.\n\nConclusions\n\nThe following conclusions emerge from the discussion of the\nresults, focusing both on the progress identified and, on the\nlimitations, and gaps in research on climate change-related\nmisinformation from the perspective of obstructionism:\n1.      \u0007Limitations in the collection of scientific literature.\nThe studies analysed are limited as they mainly\ndeal with local studies on narratives and the impact\nof disinformation, vaccination and fact-checking\nat the local level. This has implications for the\n\ninformation they provide and, more importantly, their\nability to provide knowledge that can be used for\ndecision-making. There is a lack of studies dealing\nwith, for example, comparative perspectives, policy\nanalysis or the assessment of groups with attitudes,\nespecially those that play an important role in delaying or preventing climate action. This limitation,\nwhich conditions the availability of a broader\nand more systemic view of the phenomenon, not\nonly leads studies to focus their analysis on less valuable and urgent aspects, but also favours the use\nof disinformation as a strategy to obstruct climate\naction, as the variables that influence or influence\nthe effectiveness of anti-disinformation measures are\nnot revealed, preventing political and practical action\nto combat it.\n2.      \u0007Thematic and theoretical diversity and diversity of approaches to the object of study. The\nvariety of approaches to the object of study shows\nthe complexity of its approach, but also the difficulty\nof conducting more systemic and global analyses.\nAlthough some of the studies are interested in\nidentifying the personal and contextual factors that\npromote climate disinformation, in most cases this\nis done from very specific and limited perspectives.\nAssessing the development of technological tools\nto detect and neutralize it and analysing its impact\non public and political perceptions are valuable in\nterms of intervention techniques, but do not help to\nunderstand the core of the components that make up\nthe matrix of systemic disinformation with financial,\neconomic and political roots proposed in this study.\n\u0007This disordered plurality is also reflected in the\nwide range of theories and analytical frameworks\nused, which come from psychology, sociology,\ncommunication, international relations or criminology\nand highlight the theory of inoculation, cognitive\ndissonance and judgment theory.\n3.      \u0007More systematized methodological approaches\nare needed. The diversity of methodological\napproaches and the lack of large-scale studies limit\nthe development of replication studies that could\nhelp increase the type and number of interventions\nand measure their effectiveness. Consolidation and\nsystematization of methodological study models\nwould help to simplify study design, expand samples, and harmonize data collection and analysis\ntechniques, which would strengthen research and\nfacilitate the development of meta-analyses.\n4.      \u0007Large-scale research should be funded. The limitations of the sample designs also show that in most\ncases these are well-designed studies, but small in\nscale and scope. To bridge the gap between micro and\nmacro research, funding lines and calls for proposals\nshould be established to promote the development of\n\n\n\nlarge-scale research, such as studies covering all countries of the European Union. This global approach\nwould facilitate the development of more integrated\napproaches that identify, based on a critical analysis,\nthe fundamental elements that shape the landscape of\nscientific misinformation, focusing on the practices\nused as obstructionist tactics against climate change\naction.\n5.      \u0007It is necessary to promote studies that analyse the\nsystemic nature of the disinformation of the financial, economic and political climate. This need is\nreflected in the conclusions of the studies, which\nonly partially explain the components of the matrix\nof systemic disinformation with financial, economic\nand political roots. The studies describe and document the factors that influence the processing of\ninformation about climate change and the impact of\ndisinformation on audiences. This allows us to understand some of the psychological and social dynamics that facilitate the spread of disinformation, but\nthey deal only to a limited extent with specific mechanisms such as critical scientific scrutiny, denial\ncampaigns or the faking of scientific uncertainty.\n\u0007The same applies to the studies that explain how individual predispositions and risk perception influence\nsusceptibility to climate change information. However, they do not address how these strategies can\nbe part of coordinated obstructionist campaigns, nor\ndo they explain the extent to which critical scientific\nreviews, which have been documented as essential in the processes of scientific discrediting and\nconfusion, can be systematically deployed along\nwith strategic sponsorship actions by entities with a\nspecific interest in delaying or postponing climate\naction, policies, or programs.\n6.      \u0007Research should provide solutions or operational\nrecommendations. The effectiveness of fact-checking\nand inoculation strategies, as well as technological and methodological solutions for developing\nintervention models, represent significant advances\nin the fight against misinformation. However, these\nsolutions focus primarily on target audiences and\nthe detection or correction of misinformation without addressing in depth the underlying mechanisms\nand complex strategies that characterize systemic\ndisinformation promoted by actors with specific\ninterests.\n\u0007Moreover, while these techniques can mitigate\nthe impact of disinformation on individuals and\nstrengthen their resilience to information manipulation,\nin their formalized model they are ill-equipped to\nrespond to the hidden structures and funding of\nlarge-scale disinformation campaigns which rely on\ncomponents such as critical scientific reviews, denial\ncampaigns or the fabrication of scientific uncertainty,\n\nwhich in many cases are only discovered after\nthey have already developed and taken effect.\n\u0007The study provides valuable results to understand\nand defuse scientific misinformation. However,\nas it does not focus on the networks of influence\npromoted by financial actors, politicians and third\ncountries, it does not offer operational solutions to\nintervene in a space of information pollution whose\nunderstanding goes beyond the simple dissemination\nof false information about climate change, but rather\nfunctions in terms of a complex structural and\nsystemic set-up in which misinformation is key to the\nsuccess of climate mitigation policies.\n\nData availability\n\nNO data are associated with this article.\n\nUnderlying data\nZenodo Sample Records: Disinformation as a strategy of\nobstructionism on climate action: analysis of the limitations\nof the scientific literature for a systemic understanding of the\nphenomenon.       "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nThis study examines the scientific misinformation about climate\nchange, in particular obstructionist strategies. The study aims to\nunderstand their impact on public perception and climate policy and\nemphasises the need for a systemic understanding that includes the\nfinancial, economic and political roots.\nMethods\nA systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted using the PRISMA\n2020 model. The sample consisted of 75 articles published between\n2019 and 2023, sourced from Web of Science, Scopus and Google\nScholar. Methodological triangulation was performed to improve the\nanalysis.\nResults\nThe results show that technological approaches to misinformation\ndetection, such as immunisation and fact-checking, are widely used.\nHowever, few studies look in depth at the operational structures that\nsupport systematic disinformation.\nConclusions\n\n\nOp\n\nThe study emphasises the urgent need to expand and deepen\nresearch on climate disinformation and argues for more global,\ncomparative and adequately funded studies. It emphasises the\nimportance of addressing the systemic complexity of disinformation\nand integrating different theoretical and methodological approaches.\nThis will help to develop effective measures against hidden networks\nof influence and mitigate their disruptive effects. The research\nfindings are relevant for policymakers, scientists, academics, the\nmedia and the public and will help to improve strategies to combat\nclimate misinformation and promote science-based climate action.\n\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nThe urgency of environmental issues (Willis, 2020), including climate change and global warming (IPCC, 2023), is part of\na widespread concern and has been recognized as a key security challenge (The National Intelligence Council, 2021a).\nThis has led to numerous initiatives to mitigate its effects. The\nEuropean Union is systematically promoting the transition\nfrom a linear economy to a circular, green and blue economy\n(European Commission, 2022; European Commission, 2023),\nwhich requires public opinion to champion the associated\nchange (Carbonell-Alcocer et al., 2022; Trudel, 2019).\nInformation and communication measures are crucial in this\nchange process, as the success of this transition depends on\na profound change in citizens’ beliefs, attitudes, practices\nand behaviors (Mcdonough & Braungart, 2002).\nIt therefore depends on cultural and cognitive processes that\nmay encounter resistance to change, public inaction (Hornsey\n& Fielding, 2020) and manipulative information tactics through\nvarious vectors that exploit the vulnerabilities and cognitive\nbiases (Soon & Goh, 2018) of societies, groups and individuals to spread narratives that question and contradict scientific\nknowledge, thus contributing to polarized reactions. Consequently, this can act as a powerful inhibitor of attitudes and\nbehaviors that are susceptible to such change, limiting the\neffectiveness of science communication interventions that\npromote it and triggering a boomerang effect (Palm et al., 2017)\nThe spread of these narratives and the mechanisms and effects\nof scientific misinformation and disinformation have been\nwidely studied in their most immediate processes and mechanisms, both in their cognitive effects and in the way they\ninfluence individual attitudes and behaviors. Disinformation\nrefers to the deliberate and coordinated dissemination of false\n\nor misleading information, usually motivated by economic,\npolitical or strategic interests. On the other hand, misinformation refers to the unintentional spread of incorrect information, where the sender may believe the information to be true\n(European Commission, 2018; European Commission, 2020).\nThis distinction is key to understanding the tactics underlying\nobstruction strategies in climate action, where interested actors\npromote narratives to manipulate public perception and delay\nthe implementation of sustainable policies.\nIn addition to these approaches, critical media literacy (CML)\nallows us to understand how media narratives shape public perceptions of climate change. CML, and in particular eco-media\nliteracy, enables the critical analysis and deconstruction of misleading information in the media, especially in relation to environmental issues linked to corporate and political interests\n(Kellner & Share, 2019; Lopez, 2023).\nHowever, more systematic approaches that assess from a\ncritical perspective what are the main axes on which the ecosystem of scientific misinformation is built, and in particular\nthe one that uses this as an obstruction strategy on climate\nchange, are less common (Almiron & Moreno, 2022). As studied (Farrell et al., 2019), macro-conditions that restrict or\ncondition the creation and sharing of information can in\nthemselves, without creating disinformation, favor its spread.\nIn the scientific field, we find both influence operations\n(Larson et al., 2009; Walton, 2019) and orchestrated campaigns to create confusion, spread misinformation or incomplete\ninformation based on the development of alternative narratives\n(Roe & Shapira, 2021). These disinformation strategies and\ntactics have been used by business lobby groups such as the\nCompetitive Enterprise Institute-funded Cooler Heads Coalition (O’Harrow Jr., 2017) and industrialists, particularly the\ntobacco industry (Glenza, 2019) and the fossil fuel industry\n(Supran & Oreskes, 2017).\nIn the context of this study, systemic disinformation with financial, economic and/or political roots is considered to be the\ndeliberate and coordinated dissemination of false or misleading\ninformation that originates in or is motivated by the strategic\nobjectives and interests of relevant actors in these fields\nand influences public perceptions of climate change. It is\nconsidered systemic because it is part of a broader pattern or\nsystem designed or promoted by actors seeking to maintain,\npreserve or increase their financial benefits, influence the\nchange and/or development of policies that benefit them\neconomically, or achieve political or geostrategic objectives.\nSystematic disinformation aimed at obstructing climate\naction, often based on the false rhetoric of climate activism\n(Almiron & Moreno, 2022), has a profound impact on\ndecision-making, public trust and the effectiveness of policies\nand the actions they promote (Abudu et al., 2023). It is therefore important to know how scientific research approaches the\nstudy, what its findings are and what recommendations it makes\nfor tackling climate change in its various dimensions.\n\n\nBackground to scientific disinformation on climate\nchange\nThe first mentions of disinformation and misinformation about\nclimate change date back to the 1990s. The IPPC’s Second\nAssessment Report on Climate Change, published in 1995,\nalready contained in its foreword a general mention of the problem of misinformation as a tool that hinders the communication\nof environmental science. More specifically, we find accusations directly linking the hydrocarbon industry to the creation\nand promotion of climate disinformation campaigns. Several popular works are worth mentioning in this context\n(Gelbspan, 1997; Gore, 1993; McKibben, 1989). At the end\nof the decade, authors such as Ehrlich et al. (1999) stated that\ndisinformation was the main obstacle to the creation of\nknowledge about climate, despite the enormous amount of\ninformation that has been generated about environmental\nproblems in recent decades.\nOn the other hand, when reviewing the scientific literature,\nwe can see that time and again an extensive and undifferentiated use of concepts is made, leading to greater confusion about\nthe nature of the phenomenon at a systemic level. This can be\nobserved with the terms disinformation and misinformation,\nwhich are often used indiscriminately without referring to\nor attributing the malicious nature of disinformation.\nSimilarly, the concept of denial has been used as a catch-all\nterm to summarize all attitudes that question the scientific\nconsensus on climate change. Far from this ascribed uniformity, authors such as Almiron & Moreno (2022) warn of the\nneed to create different categories that represent the diversity\nof attitudes held in order to understand the complexity of the\nphenomenon: skepticism (doubts about climate science and\nthe attribution of pseudoscientific positions), countermovement (uncoordinated actions aimed at undermining climate\nscience and policy), contrarianism (opposition to climate action),\nretardism (the term applied to those who sow doubt about\naction to mitigate climate change and call for inaction), and\nobstructionism (actions aimed at hindering policies to reduce\npollution and emissions). Of all of them and given the increasing\nrecognition of the existing scientific consensus on climate\nchange and the direct experience of the effects of global warming\n(Leiserowitz et al., 2023), obstructionism is gradually taking\nshape as the dominant approach with a distinctly ideological\ncharacter, whose common factor is the defense of capitalism\nrather than the denial of climate change itself (Almiron &\nMoreno, 2022).\n\nObstructionism on climate action as a strategy\nThere is no doubt that misinformation and misinformation\nabout climate change is currently a recognized threat because\nit can prevent necessary decisions (United Nations, 2023).\nMuch of the scientific literature on the subject addresses the\nproblem of skepticism (Haltinner & Sarathchandra, 2023) and\nclimate change denial and, from a more complex and choral\nperspective of the problem, the obstruction of climate action\nand the strategies developed by the promoting actors to sow\nconfusion in public opinion about the existence of climate\n\nchange, the deconstruction of the arguments used, their scope\nand their impact on the public. As Almiron and Moreno point\nout, “obstructionism is both a narrative and a strategy. In both\ncases, the goal is the same: to delay policies aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions or pollution in general because\nthey are seen as a threat to the economy.” (2022, p. 14) From a\nbroad perspective, obstructionism is described as a process of\nideological mediation (Martin Serrano, 1977; Ricoeur, 1994) in\nwhich those social representations that favour inaction are\nconstructed by using mental frames (Lakoff, 2008) and selecting those social representations that can evoke them (Berger &\nLuckmann, 1966). Other recent studies have assessed the\nrole of specialised journalism in combating climate misinformation by recognising verification patterns and identifying\ncommon narratives and actors in the dissemination of false\ncontent (Fernández-Castrillo & Magallón-Rosa, 2023), and\nothers provide a detailed analysis of the way obstructionist arguments are amplified via platforms such as YouTube,\nillustrating the complex interplay between denial and\nobstructionism in climate change discourse (Olmeda, 2022)\nThe basis of obstructionism is the financial, economic and political roots that influence polarization on environmental issues to\npromote negative, delaying or adversarial discourses, among\nothers, through organizations funded by political and financial actors with strategic interests. In its paradigmatic dimension, its function is to construct a shared social imaginary\n(Castoriadis, 1975) as an alternative to scientific knowledge,\nusing the interrelation between this and the individual mental system to create a shared sense and feeling that binds social\ngroups and collectives together and can influence public opinion. These influence operations were investigated by Farrell\n(2016) using computational methods. This uncovered the institutional structures that fund and coordinate scientific disinformation campaigns (Goldman et al., 2017) and include “think tanks,\nphilanthropic foundations, corporations, trade associations, advocacy groups, front organizations, front companies, lobbyists,\nand public relations firms” (Dunlap & McCright, 2011).\nNumerous cases are documented in the literature. González\n& Agudo (2000), based on an earlier analysis by (Ong &\nGlantz, 2000) of the tobacco cartel’s decades-long disinformation operations, identified the keys to manipulation and\ncorruption in the scientific field, which they divided into five\nareas: (a) hiring scientific consultants to obtain information\nabout the studies conducted in order to expose possible biases\nor weaknesses in their design and conduct and jeopardize their integrity; (b) encouraging the development of review\narticles and publications arising from scientific meetings\nthat would lead to contrary conclusions or create confusion;\n(c) Funding collaborative agreements to influence studies;\n(d) Challenging the role of the discipline, in this case epidemiology, as a basis for public health policy; (e) Promoting alternative frameworks, e.g. through the development of a code of\nethics for health professionals.\nThese practices have also been observed in the oil industry\nfor decades. In the United States, the House Oversight\nCommittee has documented that oil companies have known\n\n\nabout the impact of their operations on climate change and\nglobal warming since the 1980s (Gramling, 2021) and how\nthey have used disinformation as a strategy to evade their\nresponsibilities and further increase their profits (Powell,\n2021). Supran (2021) has identified three keys in ExxonMobil’s\nrhetoric to disinform and create an alternative narrative, highlighting the similarity to the tobacco model: 1) downplaying\nthe seriousness of climate change; 2) demonstrating the value,\nimportance and necessity of fossil fuels for prosperity and\nprogress; 3) blaming consumers for climate change.\nAs the fourth edition of the Production Gap Report (SEI,\nClimate Analytics, E3G et al., 2023) points out, the environmental rhetoric and greenwashing of these companies\nis not matched by effective action. In fact, governments’\ncommitment to reducing fossil fuel production is low. They\nplan to produce twice as much as would be needed to limit\nwarming to 1.5°C.\n\nDisinformation as a counterargument\nParadoxically, we observe the use of the term disinformation as an argumentative strategy to disqualify climate\nscience. The positioning of these counterarguments in the\npublic sphere is part of the struggle to shift the focus of the\nOverton Window in relation to climate change, as in the face\nof public positions in favor of tackling the problem\n(Turrentine, 2019), others contribute to distrust in scientific knowledge, the rise of denialism, and entrenchment in\nauthoritarian attitudes (Herranen, 2023; Reay, 2016).\nAs Haltinner & Sarathchandra (2023) have pointed out, climate\nchange skepticism is a complex social phenomenon, not\njust a scientific one, which has an extraordinary diversity\nand therefore needs to be understood to be acted upon.\nWe find its beginnings in 1977 in the statements of the president of an aerosol company defending himself against\ncriticism of hydrofluorocarbons, denouncing their origin\nin a “campaign “orchestrated by the KGB’s Ministry of\nDisinformation” (Gordon & Suzuki, 1991, p. 25). Later, in\nthe early 1990s, the Competitive Enterprise Institute noted\nin a report that the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)\nwas “promoting irrational fears for its own benefit through\ndisinformation” (Smith, 1992, p. 4).\nAt other times, the concept of disinformation is used as a\ntool to discredit key proponents of climate change. A clear\nexample of this strategy can be found in a critical review of\n“Earth in the Balance: Ecology and the Human Spirit” by\nAlbert Gore Jr. (1993), written by denier Stephen Hahn. In\nhis review, Hahn presents a simplistic and Manichean account\nof the climate debate. He portrays the denier lobbies as “selfinterested cynics . . . seeking to cloud the underlying issue of\nthe environment with disinformation,” in contrast to the climate\nchange lobbyists who are portrayed are romantically” ... “They\nappear only as individual men and women of humble backgrounds, members of the environmental “resistance” (Hahn, 1993,\np. 1744).\n\nSimilarly, Gelbspan’s acclaimed 1997 work, “The Heat Is\nOn: The High Stakes Battle over Earth’s Threatened Climate,\nhas been criticized for the author’s allegedly unfounded accusations of disinformation against the hydrocarbon industry\nbecause “the organization and funding of the campaign are not\ndetailed and it is all too easy to defame those who question the global warming arguments as tools of big business”\n(Stolley, 1999, p. 235).\nAnother strategy of climate change deniers or sceptics is to\naccuse the representatives of the so-called alarmist lobby of an\nunjustifiably fatalistic discourse and of exaggerating the extent\nand effects of climate change. This strategy can be found among\ncertain representatives of popular literature and within the\nwork of conservative think tanks. A notable example is the\nwork Red Hot Lies: How Global Warming Alarmists Use\nThreats, Fraud, and Deception to Keep You Misinformed by\nChristopher Horner (2008). In this book, Horner outlines the\nneoconservative view that opposes environmental regulation by deliberately magnifying the scale of the climate crisis\nand arguing that such measures hinder economic growth and\nprosperity.\nThis same neoconservative line seems inextricably linked to\nthe political propaganda of Donald Trump’s presidential campaign and was crucial to his 2016 election victory (Abudu\net al., 2023). During this period, the conservative candidate\nforged a populist narrative around climate change mitigation\nmeasures, portraying them as an obstacle to the development of capitalism and a competitive disadvantage for the US\nin favour of countries such as China and India, which would\nhave been guaranteed deadlines for the energy transition\n(Nordensvard & Ketola, 2022). In campaign speeches, we\noften find qualifiers such as “anti-worker” and “job-destroying”\n(Abudu et al., 2023) for these regulations because they\nwould harm the working class because of the implied global\nredistribution efforts. In this way, Trump’s political propaganda\npushed the public against candidates who care about climate\nchange (Bovet & Makse, 2019).\nWhile this narrative appealed to economic fears, the broader\nimplications of climate change were not ignored by US national\nsecurity agencies. In fact, the US Intelligence Community, in its\nreport “Climate Change and International Responses Increasing Challenges to US National Security Through 2040”,\nasssessed that “climate change will increasingly exacerbate\nrisks to US national security interests as physical impacts\nincrease and geopolitical tensions rise over how to respond\nto the challenge” (National Intelligence Council, 2021b: i).\n\nAgents promoting and disseminating denialism\nThese arguments were debunked when it became known that\nthe hydrocarbon industry had been funding the denialist lobby\nfor decades. This evidence came to light through lawsuits\nagainst companies, information obtained through Freedom\nof Information Act (FOIA) requests (Mulvey et al., 2015),\nand through disclosure by researchers who had access to the\noriginals, as well as by the Royal Society and the Union\n\n\nof Concerned Scientists (UCS). Analysis of the documents\n(Gelbspan, 1997; Oreskes, 2011; Oreskes & Conway, 2011)\nshowed that the major industrial companies (Chevron, ConocoPhillips, ExxonMobil, BP, Shell and Peabody Energy) had\nbeen directly and persistently involved in disinformation\ncampaigns aimed at distorting reality about the risks of climate\nchange through trade associations and front organizations\nfor decades. Later, The Guardian documented in a detailed\nreport how the fossil fuel industry had deliberately misled\nthe American public about climate change (Cook et al., 2019).\n\nStrategies for combating scientific misinformation on\nclimate action\nSome of the literature has dealt with possible solutions to counteract these malicious or false information flows. A variety of\naspects, knowledge of which is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon, and which requires\nmultidisciplinary approaches from different perspectives and\nacademic disciplines: from psychology, computer science,\npolitical science, pedagogy and communication (Cook et al.,\n2015).\n\nFundamental to the success of this strategy was the role of conservative think tanks funded by industry through affiliated\nfoundations. According to the analysis of denialist literature conducted by Jacques et al. (2008), of the 141 books\npublished between 1972 and 2005 that promoted environmental\nscepticism, 130 were associated with conservative think tanks.\nTheir geographic origins were widely dispersed and included\nAustralia, Canada, Europe, and South Africa in addition to the\nUnited States (Jacques et al., 2008). Their large-scale organizational capacity was key to countering the initial success\nof the environmental movement in the 1970s and 1980s.\nThe list of these think tanks includes Competitive Enterprise\nInstitute, Heratland Institute, Marshall Institute, Cato Institute,\nAmerican Enterprise Institute, Claremont Institute, National\nCenter for Policy Analysis, National Center for Public Policy\nResearch, Centers for a Sound Economy Foundation, Reason\nPublic Policy Institute for Research on the Economy and the\nEnvironment, or the Pacific Research Institute (McCright &\nDunlap, 2010).\n\nFarrell et al. (2019) propose four main strategies to combat\nscientific disinformation in this area based on an analysis of how disinformation campaigns are developed as part\nof a multi-layered strategy to create a thematic agenda and\nideological focus on public opinion.\n\nAt the same time, the traditional media and, more recently,\nthe new digital channels act as vectors for the spread of disinformation. The work of Supran & Oreskes (2020) is revealing\nin this respect. It shows how ExxonMobil paid the New York\nTimes to publish editorial-style advertisements between\n1989 and 2004. The strategy of sowing doubt about climate\nscience was in turn facilitated by the norm of balanced media\ncoverage, where the different sides of the climate debate were\nequally represented (Dunlap & McCright, 2010).\nThe rise of social media in recent years has in turn contributed\nto the exponential spread of climate change disinformation,\nacting as an amplifier of denialist and obstructionist discourse\nand reaching an even wider audience. Creating confusion\nabout the scientific consensus on the existence of climate\nchange has become the fundamental goal of the denialist movement, mainly thanks to the use of automated bots that have\nreinforced the existing polarization (Chen et al., 2021). In this\nsense, Marlow, Miller and Roberts (2021) have systematized the\nmain mechanisms through which social networks contribute to\nthe polarization of discourse: a) exposure to uncivil conversations about controversial content, leading to increased affective polarization; b) configuration of a fragmented information\nenvironment, which reduces the overall quality and favours the\ninclusion of marginal viewpoints; and c) exposure of users to\na greater number of opposing viewpoints, which can lead to\nthe activation of boomerang-like responses or the hostile\nenvironment.\n\nThe first of these measures is public inoculation. The reception of scientific information is shaped by pre-existing ideologies and beliefs, so it is formed through a process of\n“cultural cognition”. Therefore, it is not enough to point out\nthe facts or refer to the scientific consensus, because in the\n“post-truth” era the value of evidence is weakened in favor of\nestablished beliefs, and it is easy to stir up skepticism in this\ncontext. An alternative approach is “attitudinal inoculation” with\ninoculation models against disinformation that anticipate the\nreaction to it. One way to do this is to provide data on who is\nbehind and funding the media that spread disinformation.\nThe second is to develop legal strategies. In the United States,\nmulti-million-dollar lawsuits against industries, particularly\noil companies such as ExxonMobil, have exposed how the\ncompany concealed data from its own reports on the impact\nof its operations on climate change for years (Supran &\nOreskes, 2017). Multi-million-dollar judgments, as in the case\nof the tobacco industry or the chemical industry such as Dupont\n(Bilott, 2020), despite their complexity, have an important\ncorrective effect on the way this issue is handled by large\ncompanies.\nThe third possibility is the use of political channels and mechanisms. The politics of polarization has found its niche in social\ndiscontent by exploiting, especially through social networks,\npolitically based scientific disinformation arguments that\nbetter fit certain beliefs: such as energy independence, deregulation or nationalism and non-interference, as well as the\neconomic interests of certain collectives that see their lucrative businesses threatened. These strategies are covert\ncommunication measures that finance seemingly neutral civil\nmovements. Or they use the social structures of religious\norganizations to spread or stop certain initiatives.\nFinally, financial transparency must be promoted. It is necessary\nto make funding from private philanthropists and industry\ntransparent so that we know who they are funding and for\nwhat purposes. As mentioned earlier, large corporations are\nbehind the groups spreading scientific disinformation about\nclimate change. So the opacity enabled by mechanisms such as\n\n\nfoundation funding, which protect the identity of donors, must\nbe avoided.\nMisinformation has become the dominant strategy of the\nclimate denier, feeding a limited understanding of climate\nchange in the scientific literature and in public opinion. Since\nthe 1990s, disinformation and misinformation promoted by\nthe hydrocarbon industry has been studied as a method to\nobstruct scientific communication about climate change, its\ncauses and consequences. Despite the vast amount of information available, the published scientific literature has not helped\nto reduce the confusion, likely due to the limited scope and\nfocus of the studies conducted.\n\nObjectives\nDisinformation processes in the context of climate change\nrepresent a complex and multidimensional problem that, while\nhaving local characteristics, also has systemic and global\nfeatures that contribute to hindering climate action.\nThe overall aim of this review is to understand, based on a systematic review of the scientific literature (SLR), how research\non this subject of investigation is approached considering\nthe climate action obstructionism approach and what are the\ncharacteristics of the research conducted to assess whether\nits results provide actionable information that enables decision making aimed at improving interventions to promote\nsustainability and the circular economy.\nThe specific objectives and research questions are listed in Table 1.\nAs a general framework for analysis, a matrix of systemic disinformation with financial, economic, and political roots is drawn\nup, the details of which are shown in Figure 1 and Table 2.\nIt was developed from the analysis of global approaches that\nmainly reflect the literature reviewed in the state of the art,\nwhich includes scientific articles as well as technical reports,\ndissertations and working papers.\n\nHypothesis\nThe research proposes the following hypotheses:\nH1. Studies that systematically and comprehensively address\nclimate change disinformation, including a detailed analysis of its financial, economic, and political roots, allow for a\nmore accurate identification of the dynamics tat sustain disinformation and provide a solid basis for developing targeted\ninterventions to counteract the obstruction of climate action\n(R.Q.1.1 and R.Q.1.2).\nH2. Research that employs diversified methods and techniques, such as quantitative and qualitative analysis, adapted\nto the complexity of the climate disinformation phenomenon,\nprovides results that enable the development of operational\nstrategies and the formulation of concrete recommendations to combat disinformation and promote sustainability and\ncircular economy (R.Q.2.1 and R.Q.2.2)\nH3. Studies that can critically evaluate the findings and propose\nsolutions or operational recommendations based on a holistic\nunderstanding of the financial, economic and political\nfactors underlying climate change disinformation provide\nuseful insight into the development of policies and measures to address climate action obstruction (R.Q.3.1 and\nR.Q.3.2).\n\nMethods\n\nThe methodology used was a systematic literature review\n(SLR) using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic\nReviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA). For example, positive effects on corrective\ninformation were found for Trump’s posted messages on\nclimate change, although the influence was found to be greater\nfor Democrats. These effects could not be replicated for\nmessages related to environmental regulation. The results\ncould find further explanation in the findings of Allen &\nMcAleer (2018), who found a predominantly negative emotion\nin Trump’s messages as well as a lack of a clear logical\nframework at the discursive level, suggesting which Trump\nis starting from a false understanding of climate change by\nconfusing it with the concept of global warming.\nBenegal and Scruggs (2018) challenge the premise of party\naffiliation as a predictor of climate change attitudes, finding\nevidence that bipartisan correctness leads Republicans to\nexpress much greater support for the scientific consensus on\nclimate change. This evidence thus suggests that it is possible\nto narrow the gap between Republicans and Democrats.\nSeveral studies looked at the benefits and limitations of\ninoculation as a solution to combat misinformation about\nclimate change. Van der Linden et al. (2017) found that the\neffectiveness of communication about the scientific consensus was nullified when presented in conjunction with misinformation. The authors saw positive effects in the use of\ninoculation messages to enhance the impact of scientific\nconsensus messages. Subsequent studies (Maertens et al.,\n2020; Williams & Bond, 2020) have replicated earlier\nresearch findings, finding results that qualify or challenge the\nvan der Linden et al. (2017) findings. Authors such as McQuaid et al. (2018) conclude that\ncitizen participation in policy development needs to be encouraged and that communication is more effective when it does\nnot originate from the authorities (Bevan et al., 2020). These\nsuggestions are also supported by the findings of studies\nexamining climate change communication at the local level.\nSuch studies point to the need to involve citizens in both the\nproblem and the solution, moving away from their usual victim\n\n\nrole, while increasing scientific knowledge about climate change\n(Ejaz et al., 2021; Naguimbing-Manlulu, 2021).\nOther studies point to the ineffectiveness of narratives that\nexploit fear or offer an overly catastrophic view of the consequences of climate change. On this aspect, Segal (2017)\nsuggests that utopian or dystopian narratives leave audiences\nstuck in the debate between extremes. Alternative models of\nnarratives are proposed by Herr (2022), who points to a design\nbased on the concept of ecological hope, as opposed to the\ndominant concept of fear. For their part, Choi and Leckie\n(2018) focus on exploring the slow causalities of geological\nand generational processes, for which there is little empirical\nevidence, and Largo Loayza (2022) focuses on the design of\nmodels of transmedia narratives and on the integration of\nstorytelling.\nLocal studies: media coverage of climate change and the spread\nof misinformation in social networks\nOf the sample analysed, a significant percentage of the\narticles (42.7% n=32) deal with the phenomenon from\na local perspective. However, despite addressing diverse\nobjects and topics, there is a notable gap in studies that systematically incorporate the financial, economic and political\nroots of disinformation.\nFindings suggest that that although there is interest in understanding the personal and contextual dynamics that foster climate disinformation (Benegal & Scruggs, 2018; Sambrook et al.,\n2021; Schmid-Petri & Bürger, 2022), and efforts to\ndevelop detection technologies and methods (Gruener,\n2022; Lutzke et al., 2019), the systemic inclusion of financial, economic, and political dimensions remains limited\n(Oreskes & Conway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes, 2017).\nThis fragmented approach contrasts with evidence showing that disinformation is a coordinated strategy driven\nby specific economic and political interests (Gelbspan,\n1997; Gore, 1993). In this sense, the literature has emphasized the importance of the financial, economic and political\nroots behind climate obstructionism (Almiron & Moreno,\n2022; Dunlap & McCright, 2011), suggesting that an effective understanding of the phenomenon requires an analysis\nthat integrates these dimensions in a systemic manner.\nThis confirms the relevance of hypothesis 1 and highlights the need for more integrative research approaches\nthat go beyond technological and methodological aspects,\nby systematically addressing the financial, economic, and\npolitical structures that sustain climate disinformation. Thus,\nby focusing on concrete and procedural aspects or mitigation\ntechniques rather than global approaches, there is still a significant gap in the systematic and global coverage of the topic.\nTherefore, H1 is only partially confirmed, suggesting that\nsystemic analysis needs to be further integrated into climate\ndisinformation research. In this sense, studies should be\ndeveloped that bring together methodological advances in\n\n\nthe detection of misinformation with a critical analysis of the\neconomic and political forces that perpetuate it, as suggested\nby the findings of Farrell et al. (2019) and the proposals for\nstrategies to combat scientific misinformation by Oreskes &\nConway (2011) and Supran & Oreskes (2017).\nIn terms of research methods and techniques, they show\nthat although a diversity of methods and techniques for data\ncollection and analysis can be observed in the recent literature, the ability of these investigations to translate into\noperational solutions and recommendations still offers\nsignificant scientific challenges.\nThe studies examined use a variety of methodological\napproaches, including content and discourse analysis, surveys,\nand experiments (Cook et al., 2015; Farrell et al., 2019). This\ndiversity of methods reflects the complexity of the phenomenon\nof climate misinformation and the need for multidisciplinary\napproaches to capture its various dimensions. However, the\nimpact of these methods on the development of effective\noperational solutions is limited. In any case, the challenges\nin combating climate disinformation are not only epistemological, but also structural and political. Previous literature has\nemphasized the importance of understanding disinformation as\npart of a broader obstructionist strategy funded and promoted\nby specific economic and geopolitical interests (Oreskes &\nConway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes, 2017). Therefore, the methods used need to be broadened to include critical analyses\nof the power and funding systems that support climate\ndisinformation. This involves not only identifying disinformation\ntactics, but also the actors, networks and resources that facilitate them. These aspects have been emphasized in works\nsuch as those by Dunlap and McCright (2011) and Almiron &\nMoreno (2022).\nThe variety of methods and techniques used reflects an effort to\nadapt to the complexity of climate misinformation. However,\nthe relationship between the choice of methods and their ability to develop concrete operational strategies and recommendations, as hypothesized in H2, is not thoroughly addressed. As\na result, H2 is only partially confirmed, indicating that despite\nefforts in this direction, the effectiveness of research in generating actionable outcomes is uneven and requires further\nrefinement.\nFinally, analysing the findings and the solutions and recommendations from the perspective that it is important to consider\nclimate disinformation not only as an isolated phenomenon,\nbut as part of a broader structure of climate obstructionism\nunderpinned by financial, economic and political roots, shows\nthat obstructionism in relation to climate action is deeply rooted\nin economic and political interests aimed at maintaining the\nstatus quo (Oreskes & Conway, 2011; Supran & Oreskes,\n2017). The effectiveness of interventions against climate\nmisinformation therefore depends on the ability of research\nto understand and address these underlying dynamics and\nwhether it can demonstrate this systemic understanding of the\n\nphenomenon. This is supported by recent research that points\nto the need to implement communication and education strategies that not only warn against misinformation and disinformation, but also build citizen resilience (Cook et al., 2015;\nvan der Linden et al., 2017). However, there is a significant\ngap in the existing literature, which lacks more comprehensive approaches that combine critical analyses of the financial,\neconomic and political roots of misinformation with concrete\nproposals for action. Although some studies have begun to\nexplore these relationships, there is still a lack of greater\ninclusion of interdisciplinary analyses that can provide a\ncomplete picture of how climate disinformation is produced\nand disseminated and how it can be effectively combated.\nDespite considerable progress, the ability to provide operational recommendations that fully encompass the complexity of climate disinformation remains limited. H3 is only\npartially confirmed, as more interdisciplinary and systematized\napproaches are needed to effectively inform policies and practice, particulary by integrating critical analyses of the financial,\neconomic, and political dimensions of the phenomenon.\n\nConclusions\n\nThe following conclusions emerge from the discussion of the\nresults, focusing both on the progress identified and, on the\nlimitations, and gaps in research on climate change-related\nmisinformation from the perspective of obstructionism:\n1.      \u0007Limitations in the collection of scientific literature.\nThe studies analysed are limited as they mainly deal\nwith local studies on narratives and the impact of disinformation, inoculation and fact-checking at the local\nlevel. This has implications for the information they\nprovide and, more importantly, their ability to provide\nknowledge that can be used for decision-making. There\nis a lack of studies dealing with, for example, comparative perspectives, policy analysis or the assessment\nof groups with attitudes, especially those that play an\nimportant role in delaying or preventing climate action.\nThis limitation, which conditions the availability of a\nbroader and more systemic view of the phenomenon, not\nonly leads studies to focus their analysis on less valuable and urgent aspects, but also favours the use of disinformation as a strategy to obstruct climate action, as the\nvariables that influence or influence the effectiveness of\nanti-disinformation measures are not revealed, preventing\npolitical and practical action to combat it.\n2.      \u0007Thematic and theoretical diversity and diversity\nof approaches to the object of study. The variety of\napproaches to the object of study shows the complexity of\nits approach, but also the difficulty of conducting more\nsystemic and global analyses. Although some of the\nstudies are interested in identifying the personal and\ncontextual factors that promote climate disinformation, in\nmost cases this is done from very specific and limited perspectives. Assessing the development of technological\ntools to detect and neutralize it and analysing its impact\n\n\n\non public and political perceptions are valuable in terms\nof intervention techniques, but do not help to understand the core of the components that make up the matrix\nof systemic disinformation with financial, economic\nand political roots proposed in this study.\n\u0007This disordered plurality is also reflected in the\nwide range of theories and analytical frameworks\nused, which come from psychology, sociology,\ncommunication, international relations or criminology\nand highlight the theory of inoculation, cognitive\ndissonance and judgment theory.\n3.      \u0007More systematized methodological approaches are\nneeded. The diversity of methodological approaches and\nthe lack of large-scale studies limit the development of\nreplication studies that could help increase the type and\nnumber of interventions and measure their effectiveness. Consolidation and systematization of methodological study models would help to simplify study design,\nexpand samples, and harmonize data collection and analysis techniques, which would strengthen research and\nfacilitate the development of meta-analyses.\n4.      \u0007Large-scale research should be funded. The limitations of the sample designs also show that in most cases\nthese are well-designed studies, but small in scale and\nscope. To bridge the gap between micro and macro\nresearch, funding lines and calls for proposals should be\nestablished to promote the development of large-scale\nresearch, such as studies covering all countries of the\nEuropean Union. This global approach would facilitate the development of more integrated approaches that\nidentify, based on a critical analysis, the fundamental elements that shape the landscape of scientific misinformation, focusing on the practices used as obstructionist\ntactics against climate change action.\n5.      \u0007It is necessary to promote studies that analyse the\nsystemic nature of the disinformation of the financial,\neconomic and political climate. This need is reflected\nin the conclusions of the studies, which only partially\nexplain the components of the matrix of systemic disinformation with financial, economic and political roots. The\nstudies describe and document the factors that influence the processing of information about climate\nchange and the impact of disinformation on audiences.\nThis allows us to understand some of the psychological\nand social dynamics that facilitate the spread of disinformation, but they deal only to a limited extent with\nspecific mechanisms such as critical scientific scrutiny,\ndenial campaigns or the faking of scientific uncertainty.\n\u0007The same applies to the studies that explain how individual predispositions and risk perception influence\nsusceptibility to climate change information. However, they do not address how these strategies can\nbe part of coordinated obstructionist campaigns, nor\ndo they explain the extent to which critical scientific\n\nreviews, which have been documented as essential in the processes of scientific discrediting and\nconfusion, can be systematically deployed along\nwith strategic sponsorship actions by entities with a\nspecific interest in delaying or postponing climate\naction, policies, or programs.\n6.      \u0007Research should provide solutions or operational\nrecommendations. The effectiveness of fact-checking\nand inoculation strategies, as well as technological and\nmethodological solutions for developing intervention models, represent significant advances in the fight\nagainst misinformation. However, these solutions focus\nprimarily on target audiences and the detection or correction of misinformation without addressing in depth the\nunderlying mechanisms and complex strategies that characterize systemic disinformation promoted by actors with\nspecific interests.\n\u0007Moreover, while these techniques can mitigate\nthe impact of disinformation on individuals and\nstrengthen their resilience to information manipulation,\nin their formalized model they are ill-equipped to\nrespond to the hidden structures and funding of\nlarge-scale disinformation campaigns which rely on\ncomponents such as critical scientific reviews, denial\ncampaigns or the fabrication of scientific uncertainty,\nwhich in many cases are only discovered after\nthey have already developed and taken effect.\n\u0007The study provides valuable results to understand\nand defuse scientific misinformation. However,\nas it does not focus on the networks of influence\npromoted by financial actors, politicians and third\ncountries, it does not offer operational solutions to\nintervene in a space of information pollution whose\nunderstanding goes beyond the simple dissemination\nof false information about climate change, but rather\nfunctions in terms of a complex structural and\nsystemic set-up in which misinformation is key to the\nsuccess of climate mitigation policies.\n\nData availability\n\nNO data are associated with this article.\n\nUnderlying data\nZenodo Sample Records: Disinformation as a strategy of\nobstructionism on climate action: analysis of the limitations\nof the scientific literature for a systemic understanding of the\nphenomenon.
