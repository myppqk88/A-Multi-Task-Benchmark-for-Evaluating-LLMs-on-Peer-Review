      "text": "Abstract\nPurpose\nThis paper reviews the existing literature on the divergence of\nEnvironmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) ratings and explores\nvarious aggregation methods. It highlights the challenges posed by\ninconsistent ESG ratings and their implications for investment\ndecisions.\nDesign/methodology/approach\nThe study conducts a comprehensive review of prior research\nfocusing on ESG ratings, examining their correlation levels and the\nmethodologies employed to assess corporate sustainability. It also\ninvestigates traditional aggregation techniques and modern machine\nlearning approaches used to address these inconsistencies.\nFindings\nPast literature reveals that ESG ratings exhibit a low level of\ncorrelation across different providers, raising concerns about their\nreliability as investment indicators. Although some studies propose\nadvanced aggregation methods to enhance accuracy, significant gaps\nremain in understanding how to effectively consolidate ESG\ninformation to create a dependable sustainability indicator.\nOriginality\nThis paper provides a critical analysis of the current state of ESG rating\nmethodologies, emphasizing the need for improved aggregation\nstrategies. It underscores the importance of future research in\nleveraging ESG data to develop more consistent and reliable\nmeasures of corporate sustainability.\n\n\n\n1 Introduction and background\n\nThe environmental impact of corporate activities has become\nincreasingly important over the last decades (Yu et al., 2021).\nIndeed, companies are made accountable for the impact of\ntheir business activities on the planet and society through the\nmeasurement of their Corporate Social Performance (CSP).\nMany companies have implemented over time sustainability reporting that started initially on a voluntary basis (e.g., the\nGlobal Reporting Initiative - GRI) (Monteiro et al., 2023, for\na review) and was later incorporated into the regulatory provisions. For instance, in Europe, the Non-Financial Reporting\nDirective (NFRD) (Directive 2014/95/EU) introduced in 2014\nthe requirement for large companies and public-interest entities\nwith more than 500 employees to publish non-financial information related to ESG topics. The directive was later improved\nand refined by the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive\n(CSRD) (Directive 2022/2464/EU). The new rules expand the\nNFRD, with the objective to improve the quality and consistency of information provided by companies on the topic of\nsustainability. Additionally, it enlarges the applicability of\nthese rules to all large companies and, gradually, to listed\nSMEs. The Directive also introduces European Sustainability\nReporting Standards and the external assurance (verification)\nof sustainability information.\nBeside the corporate disclosure, several data providers have\nstarted to distribute sustainability scores or ratings, often proxied through the ESG profile of the companies (Pollman, 2022).\nThis information, together with companies’ disclosure in annual\nreports or sustainability reports complements financial information available on the market and enables investors to assess the\nsustainability of corporates, i.e., considering how companies’\nbehaviour affects external stakeholders and paying attention to the economic, environmental, social, and time factors\n(Lozano, 2012; Lozano, 2018; Muñoz-Torres et al., 2019). In\nother words, this set of information contributes to the reduction of asymmetries of information for investors and, at the\nsame time, a reduction in the cost of capital for corporates (Yu\net al., 2021).\nDespite these efforts to promote the diffusion of sustainability disclosure, the birth of multiple ESG ratings providers has\nbrought to a proliferation of methods and scores that often\ndisagree and create “aggregate confusion” on the market as\ndiscussed by Berg et al. (2022). This has the perverse effect\nof generating additional uncertainty on the market, rather than\nactually reducing asymmetries of information for investors.\nMotivated by such considerations, the present paper provides\na review of the financial and statistical literature concerning\nthe analysis of ESG scores’ divergence and the development of\nmethodologies for ESG ratings/scores aggregations.\nThe paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a brief\noverview of the ESG ratings and scores; Section 3 motivates the\nneed for developing ESG aggregating measures by presenting\nthe relevant literature focused on ESG rating divergence and\n\nits possible sources; Section 4 presents the most relevant\ncontributions in terms of ESG rating aggregation; Section 5\nconcludes.\n\n2 ESG ratings and scores\n\nThe ESG profile of companies is often taken as a proxy of\ntheir sustainability behaviour (Eng et al., 2022).\nWhile Environment factors relate to the impact on the environment deriving from the production of goods or services,\nsuch as carbon emissions, preservation of the natural environment, biodiversity protection, waste and water management\n(European Commission, nd; Robeco, nd; Times, nd), Social\nfactors refer to the impact of the company on society, including issues of employee satisfaction, diversity, inequality,\ngender gap, protection of young and children, investment in\nhuman capital and communities, and human rights (European\nCommission, nd; Van Duuren et al., 2016). Governance\nfactors instead are accounted for to evaluate the “good” governance of companies, which can contribute to a more\nsustainable and balanced firms’ growth, and thus to a more\nsustainable economic development (Adams & Mehran, 2012;\nAlkaraan, 2023; Esteban-Sanchez et al., 2017).\nThese three factors have also become the basis for investment\ndecisions within sustainable financing and can drive the choice\nof investors in terms of which companies to finance through\nequity or debt. Furthermore, since International authorities\nhave started to include sustainability in their agenda (European\nCommission, 2018; European Commission, 2021; UN, 2015),\npolicymakers and regulators have developed specific measures.\nIn the financial context, European regulators request financial\nintermediaries to include ESG aspects for lending and investment activities (EBA, 2020; ESMA, 2020a; ESMA, 2020b) and\ncall for the inclusion of ESG aspects into creditworthiness\nevaluations provided by credit rating agencies (European Action\nPlan for Financing Sustainable Growth) (European Commission,\n2018), to direct funds to the best-performing companies in\nterms of environmental and social impact. The ESG factors\nhave also a link with economic performance. Events related to\nclimate change can affect profitability and produce losses that\ncan affect companies’ ability to meet their credit obligations.\nCompanies are therefore pushed by different actors towards a\nmore sustainable behaviour. In addition, they are pushed to\ndisclose more about their ESG behaviour, to reduce asymmetries\nof information and improve their ability to retrieve cheaper\nfunds (Yu et al., 2021).\nTo inform the investors concerning corporate ESG performance,\nspecialised companies (including rating agencies) provide\nmeasures and proxies for ESG behaviour, publishing ESG\nratings or scores that should express the level of sustainability\nand the degree of accountability of companies on ESG aspects\n(Avetisyan & Ferrary, 2013; Scalet & Kelly, 2010). As each\nrating provider collects information from different sources\n(company reports, news, stock exchange information, etc.) and\napplies proprietary methodologies to combine available inputs\nand produce a synthetic measure of ESG behaviour, ESG\n\n\nevaluations assigned by different providers often produce divergent results (Abhayawansa & Tyagi, 2021; Berg et al., 2022;\nBillio et al., 2021; Dimson et al., 2020; Dorfleitner et al., 2015),\nyielding to potential “sustainability arbitrage” as recently\ndiscussed in Pollman (2022). Indeed, multiple ESG ratings for a given company can differ, providing opaque information about the company’s ESG profile. A recent survey\nby KPMG showed the existence of more than 160 ESG ratings\nand data providers (KPMG, 2020), with numerous agencies\n(eg. Bloomberg, Thomson Reuters, S&P, etc.) whose ESG ratings may differ. The potential presence of divergent scores for\nthe same companies can represent an element of uncertainty\nfor investors and regulators, who may need to choose among\ndifferent measures and/or combining them into a single metric.\n\n3 Divergence of ESG scores\n\nSeveral recent works showed poor convergence between ESG\nratings provided by different agencies and analysed the sources\nof such inconsistencies. Indeed, understanding the origin of\nESG rating divergence can help:\n• \u0007regulators to put in place proper measures to increase\nand harmonise ESG disclosure of companies;\n• \u0007investors to choose between alternative rating providers or\nto manage divergence through the aggregation of scores\nissued by different providers;\n• \u0007companies to improve disclosure of their ESG approach.\nSeveral works showed little convergence between different ESG\nratings through empirical studies. Among them, Dorfleitner\net al. (2015), analysing ESG rating data of publicly traded\ncompanies provided by three agencies in the 2002–2012\nperiod, find that the ratings assigned by the considered providers exhibit low linear correlations and significantly differ in\ndistribution, with a small percentage of companies assigned to\nthe same score quartile. The differences in the scoring approaches\n- revealed in the qualitative evaluation of the three rating\nmethodologies performed in the same paper - partially explain\nsuch inconsistencies. Similar conclusions were reached by\nChatterji et al. (2016), who documented the lack of agreement across ESG ratings issued by six providers between 2002\nand 2010, even after adjusting for explicit differences in the\ndefinition of CSP held by the different raters. In their review of\nthe related literature, Abhayawansa and Tyagi (2021) examined the possible causes of the differences in the ESG ratings\nand rankings produced by different agencies, and found that\ndivergences in ESG scores are mainly attributable to disagreements about the definition, composition and weightings for\neach of the E, S and G factors and to differences in the methodological approaches. In particular, according to Capizzi\net al. (2021), the weight component is crucial in explaining\nESG rating divergences.\nThe issue of divergence of ESG ratings and its possible causes\nwere explored in depth by Berg et al. (2022), who provided\na broad empirical study to document the ESG evaluations’\ndivergence and identify the factors that contribute to the\ndifferences. Specifically, the authors consider data from six\ndifferent ESG rating providers in 2014, including ESG ratings\n\nand the related underlying variables. The latter are grouped\nby imposing a taxonomy of categories, which is then used to\ndecompose the divergence into contributions of scope, measurement, and weight. By re-estimating ESG ratings based on the\nidentified categories, the authors found that the most important\ndimensions in driving ESG rating divergence are climate risk\nmanagement, product safety, corporate governance, corruption,\nand environmental management system. Further differences\namong ratings arise when considering the Environmental, Social\nand Governance dimensions separately. Such discrepancies are\nconnected to the nature and measurement of the three factors.\nIndeed, while the Environmental impact can be relatively easy\nto measure, the Social and Governance impacts are related\nto qualitative aspects, which are more difficult to assess.\nMuñoz-Torres et al. (2019) find that rating agencies, when\ncomputing ESG scores, mostly focus on the environmental\ndimension (e.g. waste reduction, energy consumption, water\nmanagement).\nThe sources of divergence of ESG rating were the focus of\nthe work by Billio et al. (2021), whose findings revealed a lack\nof commonality in the ESG score criteria used by prominent\nagencies, with the consequence that agencies assign even opposite ratings to a given company. Differently from previous\nworks, Billio et al. (2021) also investigate the implications that\nsuch disagreements may have on ESG portfolios performance. Indeed, the identification of sustainable investments and\ntherefore the choice of the relevant benchmarks depends on\nthe ratings provided by agencies. ESG rating heterogeneity\nleads to the identification of alternative benchmarks, with\nthe consequence that the impact of ESG profile on asset\nprices is weak and green investments often show no significant difference in financial performances with respect to\nnon-ESG ones.\nDespite some criticisms, evidence shows that rating agencies worked to update their computational methodologies for\nESG ratings, although they are not able to entirely capture into\nratings (Escrig-Olmedo et al., 2019; Muñoz-Torres et al., 2019).\nThe impact of ESG rating discordance on financial markets\nwas also studied by Wang et al. (2024), who found that low\nESG score correlation negatively impacts stock excess return\nrates. In particular, using Chinese market data, the authors found\nthat the smaller the distance between ESG rating issued by\ndomestic and foreign agencies, the higher the excess returns on\nstocks.\nOverall, the presence of ESG scores in the market can encourage companies to improve their CSP behaviour (Zeng & Xu,\n2019), but it also presents possible drawbacks and these must\nbe tackled to ensure comparability of ESG measures.\nStandardisation of ESG metrics is, in fact, of primary importance to enable investors to choose among investment opportunities, to allow companies to take appropriate measures to improve\ntheir environmental, social and governance footprint, and to\nimprove the degree of transparency of ESG rating providers, as\nrecently underlined by the International Organization of Securities\nCommissions (OICV-IOSCO, 2021).\n\n\nThe importance of ESG metrics is further destined to grow in the\nfuture and ESG ratings will influence investors’ decisions, therefore affecting firms’ ability to finance their investment and also\ntheir capability to pursue a more sustainable business model.\nThe need for ESG metric standardisation is therefore strongly\nfelt by the market, motivated by the growing importance of\nsustainable finance1 and understanding how ESG ratings provide information to the market and how these ratings can affect\ncreditworthiness is a major managerial and policy challenge.\n\n4 ESG score aggregation\n\nIn the economic and financial context, it is frequent to construct indicators as the synthesis of multiple input variables. The\nweighted mean represents a natural solution, but the estimation\nof weights can be problematic and their choice can be subjective, strongly affecting the final indicators. The issue is so\nrelevant that the European Commission provided a set of recommendations for the construction of aggregated metrics to be\nused for policy purposes and indicated the steps to be followed\nto build composite indicators, including data management,\nweighting, uncertainty and sensitivity analysis (European Union\nand Joint Research Centre, 2008).\nData-driven techniques can help build aggregate measures from\nseveral input variables. A method widely used in the statistical literature is Principal Component Analysis (PCA), that aims\nat creating one or more new factors from a larger set of\nobserved variables, where each component is a linear combination of the original variables and orthogonally independent\nfrom the other components. A paper by Gucciardi et al. (2024)\nemploys PCA (together with pairwise correlation analysis and\nOLS) to evaluate the “common factors” driving Environmental scores. The study finds a limited number of common factors\nunderlying the E score of different providers, mainly linked to\nmore quantitative variables (e.g., natural resources use).\nAn alternative to PCA, allowing to take the temporal dimension into account, is represented by Dynamic Factor Models\n(DFM), which, similarly to PCA, creates new factors through a\nsystem of latent variables and parameters associated with the\ncontribution of each observed variable (see Bitetto et al., 2021).\nBoth PCA and DFM make it possible to assess the role played\nby each variable, easing the interpretability of results. However, both approaches call for a large enough set of variables\n(at least 10–15), which is not always available.\nThe need for synthesising variables in a single indicator also\narises in the ESG context, and is related to the empirically\nfound divergence of ESG ratings discussed in Section 3. Kurbus and\nRant (2025) discuss the legal drivers of ESG ratings disagreement, differentiating between civil law and common law firms:\ncorrelation disagreement is lower for civil law firms, while dispersion disagreement is low for common law corporates. Also\ncountry and company characteristics can influence the ESG\nratings disagreement (Rubino et al., 2024). Despite some criticisms, evidence shows that rating agencies worked to update\ntheir computational methodologies for ESG ratings, although\nthey are not able to entirely capture into ratings (Escrig-Olmedo\net al., 2019; Muñoz-Torres et al., 2019).\nMany studies focus on the impact of ESG divergence on\ncorporate performance, market efficiency, and portfolio decisions and performance. ESG score disclosure can encourage\ncompanies to improve their CSP behaviour (Zeng & Xu, 2019),\nbut ESG disagreement presents possible drawbacks. Firms\ntend to reduce corporate green innovation (Li et al., 2024; Li\net al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024; Zhu et al.,\n2025) or total factor productivity (Li & Yang, 2025) when\nESG ratings disagree. But ESG rating divergence can result in\nadditional efforts by companies to promote the quality and quantity of green innovation (Wan et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024;\nZhou et al., 2025). ESG rating disagreement can also act as a\nmoderating factor for the corporate performance, e.g., efficiency\n(Lin et al., 2025).\nBillio et al. (2021) investigate the implications that ESG\nrating disagreements may have on ESG portfolios performance.\n\n\n\nIndeed, the identification of sustainable investments and therefore the choice of the relevant benchmarks depends on the\nratings provided by agencies. ESG rating heterogeneity leads\nto the identification of alternative benchmarks, with the consequence that the impact of ESG profile on asset prices is weak\nand green investments often show no significant difference in\nfinancial performances with respect to non-ESG ones.\nESG rating disagreement improves stock pricing efficiency\n(Yin et al., 2025), also under high analysts’ attention (Tan\net al., 2025), but contradicting evidence highlights the benefits of reducing investor ambiguity via an improved market\nefficiency (Bachner, 2025).\nESG rating discordance also negatively impacts stock excess\nreturn rates; using Chinese market data, Wang et al. (2024)\nfound that the smaller the distance between ESG rating issued\nby domestic and foreign agencies, the higher the excess returns\non stocks.\n\n5 ESG score aggregation\n\nIn the economic and financial context, scholars frequently\nconstruct indicators to synthesise multiple input variables.\nThe need for synthesising variables in a single indicator also\narises in the ESG context and is related to the empirically found\ndivergence of ESG ratings discussed in Section 3. Indeed,\nwhen ESG scores provided by different agencies for a given\ncompany are discordant, a possible solution for the investor/\nregulator is to use an aggregated measure which synthesises\nthem into a single metric.\nThe recent academic literature provides ways to correct the\ndivergence in ESG ratings (Dupuy & Garibal, 2022) and\nsolutions to build ESG-aggregated scores.\nThe weighted mean represents a simple and reliable solution\n(Bissoondoyal-Bheenick et al., 2024), but the estimation of weights\ncan be problematic and their choice can be subjective, affecting the final indicators. Indeed, the most natural mathematical\ni\nformulation for an aggregated ESG indicator ( ESGaggr\n) is a\nlinear combination of the scores assigned to the same company i by a set of k = 1,...,K providers ( ESGki ) and the\ncorresponding wk weights :\ni\nESGaggr\n=\n\nK\n\n∑ w ESG\nk =1\n\nk\n\ni\nk\n\nHowever, the estimation of weights can be problematic, and\ntheir choice can be subjective, affecting the final indicators. The\nissue is so relevant that the European Commission provided\na set of recommendations for the construction of aggregated metrics to be used for policy purposes and indicated the steps to be\nfollowed to build composite indicators, including data management, weighting, uncertainty and sensitivity analysis (European\nUnion and Joint Research Centre, 2008). Erhart (2022) refines\nthe weighted mean approach using Montecarlo simulations\nand varying weights (1/N ±25%) to build an aggregate ESG\nscore by the arithmetic, geometric, or harmonic mean.\n\nData-driven techniques can help build aggregate measures from\nseveral input variables. A method widely used in the statistical\nliterature is Principal Component Analysis (PCA), that aims\nat creating one or more new factors from a larger set of\nobserved variables, where each component is a linear combination of the original variables and orthogonally independent from the other components. Gucciardi et al. (2024)\nemploy PCA (together with pairwise correlation analysis and\nOLS) to evaluate the “common factors” driving Environmental scores. The study finds a limited number of common factors\nunderlying the E score of different providers, mainly linked to\nmore quantitative variables (e.g., natural resources use).\nBissoondoyal-Bheenick et al. (2024) apply PCA and voting\naverage to Sustainalytics, MSCI, and Asset4 ESG ratings\nand conclude the methods might contribute to ESG rating\ndivergence.\nThe limits of PCA emerge when building indexes with a normative value, according to Gai et al. (2023), who developed\nan alternative aggregation method based on Multidimensional\nSynthesis of Indicators (MSI). The authors claim this method\ncan also help in detecting greenwashing practices in companies.\nDynamic Factor Models (DFM) also represent an alternative to\nPCA that allows taking the temporal dimension into account.\nSimilarly to PCA, DFM creates new factors through a system\nof latent variables and parameters associated with the contribution of each observed variable (see Bitetto et al., 2021).\nBoth PCA and DFM assess the role played by each variable,\neasing the interpretability of results. However, both approaches\nrequire a large set of variables (at least 10–15), which is not\nalways available. Note that the above-mentioned issue of\nweighting is also crucial in this situation, as the weight attributed to each rating should not only be easily estimated, but\nalso interpreted, allowing the stakeholders to be aware of\nthe contribution of each indicator to the assessment of the\ncompany sustainability profile.\nAgosto et al. (2023a) and Agosto et al. (2023b) relied on\na Bayesian approach - based on Giudici et al. (2003) and\nCerchiello and Giudici (2014) - to obtain an aggregated indicator for the ESG performance of companies by integrating ratings assigned by different providers. In both works the\naggregated ESG indicator is computed starting from single ESG\nscores, by attributing a weight to each. The proposed weighting procedure is data-driven, as it relies on the relationship\nbetween the ESG performance and the creditworthiness\nmeasured by credit ratings issued by recognised agencies.\nIn Agosto et al. (2023b), each available ESG score receives\na weight that is a function of the likelihood of the observed\ncounts of credit rating classes, under the alternative partitions\ngenerated by the ESG scores. The likelihood weights are\nobtained through the application of Bayes’ theorem. The relationship between ESG scores and credit rating was suggested\nby previous research and prompted by policymakers and regulators (EBA, 2020; Lagoarde-Segot, 2019). The proposed methodology is applied to a sample of 791 European companies\nfor which the ESG scores issued by three different providers,\n\n\n\ntogether with Moody’s credit rating, are available. For each\nof the mentioned variables, the most recent value in the\n2018–2020 period is considered. After estimating the likelihood\nweights for the considered ESG scores, the authors performed\nan out-of-sample analysis to check whether the combined score\nimproves the accuracy of credit rating predictions, as measured by using the distance (in notches) between the observed\nand predicted credit rating class. The findings show that the\nmerged score reduces the overestimation of credit risk (lower\npositive error) for the best rated companies (AA, A and BBB\nclasses) with respect to the individual scores, while, for the BB\nand B-rated companies, the cases of credit risk underestimation (negative errors) are more frequent and higher in magnitude\nwith the combined ESG score than with the individual ones. In\nother words, according to the results of Agosto et al. (2023b),\nthe merged sustainability rating seems to better recognise\nthe most credit-reliable companies.\nThe Bayesian approach is also used by Agosto et al. (2023a),\nwho built an indicator of ESG performance of listed companies\nthat integrates the ESG scores assigned by different providers.\nIn this work, the credit-related target variable used to weight\nthe single scores is a binary one which indicates whether a\ncompany’s credit rating is speculative or investment grade,\naccording to the Bloomberg Issuer Default Risk model. Specifically, the higher the capability of the ESG score to classify\ncompanies into speculative and investment grade, the higher the\nattributed weight. The authors apply the described methodology\nto a training sample of European listed companies and validate the estimated weights on a testing sample. For each\ncompany, the probability of belonging to a speculative rating\nclass is calculated as the weighted mean of the probabilities\nassigned by the three considered scores, using the Bayesian\nlikelihood-based weights. By comparing the classification performance - in terms of Receiving Operating Curve (ROC) of the aggregated score with that of the individual ones, the\nauthors found that the merged score performs better in the tails\nof the distribution, where the more extreme financial profiles\n(very bad or very good companies) lie. The authors also\nshowed that the results are robust to the inclusion of financial\nratios as control variables.\nBesides Bayesian learning techniques, machine learning and AI\nalgorithms are used to statistically aggregate single indicators.\nTech-driven alternative methods provide a more transparent\nand democratic aggregation process (Hughes et al., 2021).\nIndeed, Agosto et al. (2023a) applied an artificial intelligence\nmethod, XGBOOST (Chen, 2015), an AI ensemble model\nwhich works over the idea of combining several weak classifiers to create a strong one characterised by extremely good performance thanks to a regularised gradient boosting framework.\nThe XGBOOST and the Bayesian model lead to very similar\nresults in terms of classification performance, but, as claimed\nby the authors, the latter provides explainable weights which\ncan be interpreted and used in further evaluations. Indeed,\nwhen, as in this case, a machine learning technique not explainable by design leads to a poor gain in predictive accuracy,\nthe use of computationally expensive AI methods like Shapley values is not justified. López-García et al. (2025) apply\nUn-weighted TOPSIS - technique for order preference by\nsimilarity to ideal solution - to Top 100 EMEA companies\n\nand provide an ESG ranking of companies that solves the\ndivergence in ratings.\nIt is worth mentioning that, regardless of the specific aggregation methodology, it is difficult to measure and compare\nthe performance of the obtained ESG indicators, due to the\nabsence of a target variable. Indeed, differently from credit scoring models, which are validated using the actual incidence of\ndefaults, the ESG behaviour cannot be easily and objectively\nsummarised by an observed variable. Indeed, among the presented works, some use unsupervised techniques such as PCA\n(Bissoondoyal-Bheenick et al., 2024; Gucciardi et al., 2024),\nwhile others rely on the relationship between ESG and financial performance (Billio et al., 2021; Gai et al., 2023) or credit\nworthiness measured by agency ratings (Agosto et al., 2023a;\nAgosto et al., 2023b).\n\n6 Concluding remarks\n\nThis paper has reviewed the literature on the divergence of\nESG ratings. It has become evident that the proliferation of\nESG rating providers has brought a high number of ratings\nthat investors, policymakers and markets can access to evaluate the sustainability of companies. While this can be positive,\nthere is a general lack of consensus on the “best” rating to be\nused as these ratings have a high level of divergence. Hence, this\ncan create confusion and have the perverse effect of increasing\nuncertainty instead of reducing asymmetries of information.\nTo partially overcome this issue, a few studies have tried\nto employ statistical aggregation techniques to leverage the\ninformation included in each rating and provide more coherent and complete information on the overall ESG profile of\nfirms. Studies employ both traditional techniques (e.g., PCA\nor OLS) and more advanced algorithms (e.g., XGBOOST).\nThere is, to date, no strictly preferable approach and further\nresearch is needed to provide additional insights into the divergence of ESG ratings and new attempts are needed to improve\nthe utility and accuracy of aggregation methods. In addition,\nthe unavailability of an homogeneous and easily implementable testing framework represents a limitation of the existing\nESG aggregation methodologies. Applying cluster analysis\nto the proposed ESG scores and identifying common financial\nand strategic features of companies belonging to the same\nclusters is one of the aspects that deserve further research,\ntogether with a more in- depth analysis of the topic of\nexplainability of the weights attributed to different scores.\nFor sure, the definition of a harmonised regulatory framework\nand an increase in the transparency of these ratings will help to\nsolve ESG score divergence. Also, from the corporates’ point of\nview, understanding what are the drivers of ESG ratings will\nhelp them to better plan their strategies to improve their environmental, social and governance performance in an effective way that produces a tangible effect on their performance\nor risk and hence that determines their ability to stay on the\nmarket.\nPolicy efforts to improve standardisation of ESG metrics\nremains vital for investors to choose among investment opportunities; companies to take appropriate measures to improve\ntheir environmental, social and governance footprint; and ESG\n\n\nrating providers to improve their transparency (OICV-IOSCO,\n2021).\n\nData availability\n\nEthics and consent\n\nReferences\nAbhayawansa S, Tyagi S: Sustainable investing: the black box of\nEnvironmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) ratings.
