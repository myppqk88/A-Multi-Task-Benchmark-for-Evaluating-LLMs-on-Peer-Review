Nigeria’s\ngovernment plans to increase the production.\n▪ Federal Control of the Petroleum Industry: NNPC Ltd. is established as a corporation under CAMA in\naccordance with Section 53 of the act in order to conduct commercial petroleum operations. Based on the\nphrasing of 53(3), it appears that the federal government will be the only shareholder in this company. State\ngovernments have no say in the administration or distribution of dividends from the corporation because the act\nstipulates that all shares are to be owned by the federal government and held by the ministry of finance on its\nbehalf.\n\n\n▪ The act also appears to undermine Section 162 of the constitution, which specifies that federal revenues are to be\ndeposited into a Federation account and distributed to the different tiers of government per the constitution.\nIn this situation, the NNPC Ltd. would benefit from being fully unbundled to increase its viability, but as it is,\nthis has just bolstered NNPC Ltd.’s attitude of non-accountability.\n▪ Host Community Development Trust: The PIA does not address the midstream operators’ contribution to the\nHCDT. However, the authority may issue regulations defining the contribution. The HCDT is to be financed at\nthe rate of 3% of the operator’s upstream operations’ total operating expenses from the previous year. However,\nthe PIA does not specify how much revenue midstream operators must put into the HCDT. The amount to be\ncontributed is expected to be outlined in regulations issued by the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream\nPetroleum Regulatory Authority.\n▪ The PIA’s impact on the existing fiscal framework is its most crucial component. The PIA reduces royalties and\nintroduces a new HCT to replace the Petroleum Profits Tax. It is possible that the new royalty system, which will\nallow the government to profit from rising oil prices, may reduce the likelihood of future alterations to the fiscal\nsystem.\n▪ One expected benefit of the PIA is a turnaround in Nigeria’s petroleum investment decline, attributable\nto the PIA’s potential to make the country more competitive relative to other petroleum-producing countries.\nThe discovery of oil and gas in other regions of the world, notably West Africa, helps to explain the drop in\nInvestment in Nigeria’s petroleum industry. Despite being one of the continent’s top producers and holding one\nof the highest reserves, Nigeria only received 4% of the $70 billion invested in Africa’s oil and gas sector\nbetween 2015 and 2019, as reported by KPMG. As an alternative to relying solely on upper government action, this system establishes\nmeasures to have communities police themselves.\n▪ The PIA levies a one per cent charge on the wholesale price of petroleum products and threatens sanctions\nagainst petroleum corporations that misrepresent their financial standing. Under section 1 of the Nigeria’s PIA 2021 the Federal Government\nin Nigeria, on the other hand, owns all of the petroleum found within the territory, including the country’s continental\nshelf and exclusive economic zone. In Norway, Statoil is the commercial arm of the petroleum industry representing the\nstate.53 Statoil engages in exploration and production, natural gas supply, pipelines, decommissioning and research and\ndevelopment. In terms of transparency, Norway’s upstream petroleum industry has become more transparent as a result of\nStatoil, which operates like other for-profit oil corporations with no special status as a statutory conglomerate and where\nthe government mainly serves a supervisory role and levies corporate taxes. The Norwegian Act also addresses domestic\nenergy supply security through state participation. Norway dominates the retail product market because they prioritise its\ninhabitants before selling to other countries. The Norwegian energy policy supports the security of supply of petroleum\ndirectorate and aids the ministry with industry administration and regulatory issues. Policy concerns are separated from\ncommercial management and regulatory challenges in this context. In contrast, the minister of petroleum in Nigeria fulfils\ncommercial regulatory and policy functions.54\nDiscussion\nNotwithstanding the huge potential offered by the Nigeria’s PIA 2021, the study revealed the following gaps under the\nNigeria’s PIA 2021 which are not in line with the international best practices and which may impede the Nigeria’s PIA\neffectiveness, among others:\n▪ Gas flaring and Energy Transition: There is silence on the subject of energy transition in the Nigeria’s PIA.\nNigeria’s government plans to increase the production.55\n▪ Federal Control of the Petroleum Industry: NNPC Ltd. is established as a corporation under CAMA in\naccordance with Section 53 of the act in order to conduct commercial petroleum operations. Based on the\nphrasing of 53(3), it appears that the federal government will be the only shareholder in this company. State\ngovernments have no say in the administration or distribution of dividends from the corporation because the act\nstipulates that all shares are to be owned by the federal government and held by the ministry of finance on its\nbehalf.56\n▪ The Act also appears to undermine Section 162 of the constitution, which specifies that federal revenues are to\nbe deposited into a Federation account and distributed to the different tiers of government per the constitution.\nIn this situation, the NNPC Ltd. The PIA 2021 may undermine this constitutional provision. Specifically, by\nallowing the NNPCLTD to retain 30% from the production sharing contracts, profit sharing, and risk service\ncontracts meant for frontier basins exploration, the PIA contravenes section 162 of the 1999 Constitution. This\nretention of 30% will reduce the oil revenue accruing to the Federal Government, states, and local governments,\nmaking it challenging for the various government bodies to meet their statutory obligations. Furthermore, the\nstudy has highlighted how the case study countries, have embraced clean sources of energy and energy mixed to\ncombat greenhouse gas emissions, have retained 0% for frontier basin exploration. These countries serve as\nvaluable examples that could be emulated through constant reform under the PIA. NNPC would benefit from\nbeing fully unbundled to increase its viability, but as it is, this has just bolstered NNPC Ltd.'s attitude of nonaccountability.57\n▪ Host Community Development Trust: The Nigeria’s PIA does not address the midstream operators’ contribution to the HCDT. However, the authority may issue regulations defining the contribution. The HCDT is to be\nfinanced at the rate of 3% of the operator’s upstream operations’ total operating expenses from the previous year.\n\n\nHowever, the Nigeria’s PIA does not specify how much revenue midstream operators must put into the HCDT.\nThe amount to be contributed is expected to be outlined in regulations issued by the Nigerian Midstream and\nDownstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority.58\n▪ The Nigeria’s PIA impact on the existing fiscal framework is its most crucial component. The PIA reduces\nroyalties and introduces a new HCT to replace the Petroleum Profits Tax. It is possible that the new royalty\nsystem, which will allow the government to profit from rising oil prices, may reduce the likelihood of future\nalterations to the fiscal system.\n▪ One expected benefit of the Nigeria’s PIA is a turnaround in Nigeria’s petroleum investment decline,\nattributable to the Nigeria’s PIA potential to make the country more competitive relative to other petroleumproducing countries. The discovery of oil and gas in other regions of the world, notably West Africa, helps to\nexplain the drop in Investment in Nigeria’s petroleum industry. Despite being one of the continent’s top\nproducers and holding one of the highest reserves, Nigeria only received 4% of the $70 billion invested in\nAfrica’s oil and gas sector between 2015 and 2019, as reported by KPMG. Data from Nigeria’s National Bureau\nof Statistics reveal that the industry represented 1.11 percent of total capital imports into the country in 2020,\nreflecting the decline in investment in the industry.59\n▪ The Nigeria’s PIA also attempting to shift responsibility for securing facilities to the surrounding host\ncommunities, noted that Damage to oil and gas infrastructure due to vandalism or sabotage will be repaired\nusing this host community entitlement. As an alternative to relying solely on upper government action, this\nsystem establishes measures to have communities’ police themselves.60\n▪ The Nigeria’s PIA levies a one per cent charge on the wholesale price of petroleum products and threatens\nsanctions against petroleum corporations that misrepresent their financial standing. The new law, which\nreplaces ten prior laws, prohibits residents from serving as trustees or executives for host communities.\nIt stipulates that oil-producing regions must disperse revenues for capital projects within a fund established\nwithin 12 months.61\nKey lessons on petroleum policies identified under Qatar, Kuwait and Norway regimes\nInvestors, especially those in the petroleum industry, in particular, will find that the policies of Qatar are not demanding.\nState ownership extends to all oil and gas reserves. There is a consensus that all-natural resources belong to the state.\nDomestic production accounts for the vast majority of Qatar’s hydrocarbon needs. Qatar Energy has the exclusive\nright to explore for, produce, invest in, and develop any hydrocarbons, including but not limited to petroleum and natural\ngas. Obtaining a licence or permit from Qatar Energy is a necessary step for any company interested in operating\nin the petroleum industry in Qatar.62 Following a decision by Qatar Energy and the State of Qatar, leases, licences, and\nconcessions for the exploitation of hydrocarbons are issued. The contractor agrees to provide Qatar Energy with a detailed\noverview of the planned petroleum activities before beginning such operations. Plans, location, production capacity,\noperating modes, engineering data, cost estimates, various computations, and other pertinent documents, information,\nand statistics are all included in this description. Rights in produced petroleum can be transferred to oil and gas companies\nthrough net production sharing methods under the Exploration and Production Sharing Agreement and the Development\nand Production Sharing Agreement.63\nOil production in Kuwait is managed by the state-owned KPC. For KOC, digitising their oil fields is crucial to attaining\ntheir objectives. Pilot projects to digitalize oil fields were completed by KOC, which used state-of-the-art technology to\nremotely monitor and run oil fields at four different sites in West Kuwait, North Kuwait, South Kuwait, and East Kuwait.\nKuwait has also set aside a substantial budget for technological efforts aimed at lowering costs and improving operational\nprocedures, as oil is the country’s primary natural resource and there is a need to reduce the risk associated with falling oil\nprices.64\nWhen it comes to advanced, well-managed hydrocarbon economies, Norway is a prime example. The petroleum\nresources are the property of the Norwegian people, as stated in the Norwegian Petroleum Act and by the UN General\nAssembly Declaration on permanent sovereignty. As a result of taking full charge of their natural resources, the\nNorwegian people will be able to better their livelihood. Statoil is the state-backed commercial arm of Norway’s\npetroleum industry. Statoil is involved in a wide variety of industries, including exploration, production, natural gas\nsupply, pipelines, decommissioning, and Research and development. In Norway, the gas pipelines are managed by a\ncompany called Gassco. While the Norwegian gas pipeline is a de facto monopoly, the country’s gas transport\ninfrastructure is managed to increase productivity and decrease biases. Norway’s regime permits and encourages\n\n\ncorporations that produce gas to sell it through a system that lets each licence sell its gas. This is not the situation in\nNigeria, where the federal government maintains a monopoly.65\nSections 4 to 7 of the Norwegian Petroleum Act provide for joint and multiple liabilities for polluters, ensuring that\nvictims of environmental damage receive adequate compensation, and also sets rigorous obligations on licence holders.\nStatoil in Norway has grown more transparent by functioning like other for-profit petroleum corporations with no special\nstatus as a statutory conglomerate and where the government primarily functions in a supervisory role and charges\ncorporate taxes.66\nThe Norwegian Petroleum Act requires licensees or lessees to submit a two- to five-year decommissioning plan\n(including a thorough environmental impact assessment) when a licence expires, is relinquished, or the use of a facility\nis permanently discontinued. The Norwegian Act addresses domestic supply security through state engagement as well.\nNorway dominates the retail goods industry by prioritising sales to its citizens over sales to other countries.\nIt is obvious that, whereas all these nations have peculiar attributes that are in line with the global best practices in the\npetroleum industry, many of these provisions were not taken advantage of in the Nigeria’s PIA 2021. Firstly, there is no\nstate ownership of crude oil. This therefore possesses a peculiar disadvantage to investors in that the petroleum industry in\nNigeria is regulated by several agencies. This is a major variance from what is obtainable in Qatar. Unlike Norway,\nNigeria has not introduced the appropriate management of hydrocarbon economies. Likewise, Nigerian-owned corporations are not involved in the exploration, and production of crude oil, likewise, most of the natural gas supply are flared\nin a bid to increasing the exploration capacity of the crude oil. The Norwegian Act also contains adequate provisions for\nthe compensation of victims of environmental degradation and transparency of the state-owned oil company. The Statoil\ncompany in Norway functions as a profit corporation which pays corporate taxes. The federal government merely\nperforms a supervisory role in the administration of the company.67 The Norwegian Act also caters for supply security by\nprioritising the sale of retail goods by the industry to its citizens over sale to other countries. Kuwait on the final note, has\npolicies invested at technological efforts which is aimed at lowering costs and improving operational procedures in the\npetroleum industry.68\nAll these are the various advantages of the petroleum industry in Qatar, Kuwait and Norway, advantages which the\nNigeria’s PIA 2021 could have incorporated. However, these were not incorporated into the Nigeria’s PIA, whether\ndirectly or otherwise. This further diminished the utility of the Nigeria’s PIA, in the light of global best practices, using the\nthree countries of Qatar, Norway and Kuwait examples for analysis.69\nOil and gas administration by the government in Nigeria is also described as being purely utilitarian. The Nigerian\ngovernment’s decision-making is heavily impacted by utilitarianism, as shown by an analysis of the country’s petroleum\nadministration through the lens of utilitarian principles. Examining the effects of the Nigerian government’s utilitarianinfluenced oil and gas management practises on the Niger Delta’s host communities, sheds insight into the problems\nthat the region faces. Although progress has been slow in the Niger Delta region, the Federal Government controls most\nof the revenue on behalf of the country. Host Community members in the Niger Delta are directly affected by the\npetroleum industry’s lack of attention to sustainability, even when the majority of Nigerians gain from oil production.\nThe administration of Nigeria’s oil and gas resources under the utilitarian approach did not effectively protect the human\nrights of the inhabitants of the Niger Delta.70\nThis study, however, concludes that the sovereign or domanial ownership theory is the best applicable theoretical\nframework for producing positive applications of the Nigeria’s PIA.71 This must however be reflected in such a way that\nthe federal government does not assume absolute control and authority over natural resources (petroleum included) but\nmust cut across to the state government, a reality of which is not currently practised in Nigeria.72\nConclusions\nThe Nigeria’s PIA serves as a cornerstone upon which further progress can be made. While no legislation is perfect,\nNigeria could benefit from commencing steps to work and improve various aspects of the Nigeria’s PIA. The Niger Delta,\nwhich is responsible for most of Nigeria’s oil output, should not have to shoulder such a disproportionate share of the\ncountry’s oil revenues. This is inconsistent with the fact that 30% of NNPC Ltd.’s revenues, is set aside for frontier\nexploration. Despite the Nigeria’s PIA many flaws, it did provide a strong foundation for the industry and is a step in the\nright direction. By highlighting some of the gaps in the Nigeria’s PIA and petroleum operations in Nigeria, this study adds\nto the body of existing knowledge. The study will serve as a guide for the Nigerian government to reflect these practices\nby adopting consistent measures, and policies or making required modifications, while also identifying inadequacies of\nthe existing regime and referencing best practices in other jurisdictions. The study will also aid lawmakers to make\n\n\namendments to legislation, regulations, and policies when Nigeria’s Nationally Determined Contributions are not\nreflected in domestic laws.73 Given the significance of including Nigeria’s international climate change obligations in\ndomestic legislation like the PIA, regulators in Nigeria would be better prepared to enforce the current laws, rules, and\npolicies and fulfil the country’s international obligations; therefore, in view of the findings above, and in relation to\nachieving the intendment of the Nigeria’s PIA 2021, this study recommends as follows: The Nigeria’s PIA should be\namended to include provisions which cater for the underlisted:\na. petroleum corporations must be compelled to install suitable gas recapturing and/or re-injection facilities,\nsimilar to what is performed in developed countries. Gas flaring must also be outrightly prohibited with no\nexceptions. Since most infrastructure currently in place condone gas flaring, a timeline should be introduced by\nthe amendment, within which all gas flaring infrastructure must be phased out.\nb. To attain the desired objectives of the Nigeria’s PIA, the HCDT resources must be appropriately administered.\nThe Nigeria’s PIA should therefore contain clear provisions on the appropriation of petroleum receipt quota for\nhost communities, as well as the regulation of the actives of the HCDT. To address the different neglect, and\nenvironmental degradation of oil-bearing areas, the operator’s contribution of 3% of its yearly operating budget\nfor the prior year in upstream petroleum activities to the HCDTF is incredibly low. At least ten per cent is\nfeasible considering the irreversible environmental impact of operations. All parts of the country, but notably the\nhost communities, should have their voices heard through a proper and thorough amending of the act. This\nshould also include provisions to cater for collaborations between various operators in a host community,\nin order to prevent the duplication of efforts and cost maximisation in the development of host communities.\nc. Also, the Nigeria’s PIA mandates that 30% of the profits of petroleum receipts be invested in the exploration\nand development of frontier basins, or remote areas of land along the borders of some Northern states and\nLake Chad where there is little confidence in the presence of crude oil. Even though it is not guaranteed, NNPC\nLtd. is required to invest 30% of its profit in developing the frontier basins, which are new places where oil is\nanticipated to be found. It appears that this provision is unnecessary and ambiguous. Therefore, it has to be\nmodified or expunged.\nd. The 30% from the production sharing contracts, profit sharing and risk service contracts which are be allocated\nfor frontier basins exploration under the Nigeria’s Petroleum Industry Act (PIA). The revenue which is meant to\nbe remitted to the federation will reduce oil revenue and the monthly allocation to the various government\nespecially the state governments who rely on it to run their day-to-day statutory obligations. The allocation of\n30 percent of NNPC LTD’s profit for exploration is relatively excessive compared to the selected case study\ncountries which have embraced clean sources of energy and energy mixed respectively to combat greenhouse\ngas emission due to it adverse effects on climate with the 0% kept for frontier basin exploration in case studies\ncountries.\ne. This study also addresses an important problem of why the host community should not be held responsible for\nany act of sabotage on the corporations’ facilities located in the community, especially since most such acts of\nsabotage could be committed by strangers in connivance with some operational staff of the corporations. There\nis therefore the need to amend the Nigeria’s PIA to include such provisions.\nf. Likewise, the Nigeria’s PIA needs to establish a system for resolving disputes between host communities and\ncorporations.
