Another similar example is the case of the 9/11 terror attacks. According to one of\nthem, although KR requires restrictive safety protocols for official events, the same requirement does not apply to public\nspaces where a large number of people gather informally (Lee, 2022b). Because of such ambiguous safety measures, not a\nsingle public agency promptly claimed related responsibilities to take charge of such a massive crowd. Similarly, highranking officials admitted that the nation did not have any emergency plan to manage such events without official\norganizers (Loh, 2022). Thus, a clear gap exists between the emergency plans and the emerging reality in KR.\nWhen focusing on emergency planning for ordinary events, the nation did not equally consider contingency planning for\nspecial events (Choi et al., 2022: 1-10). In fact, many professionals in the field of emergency management are not aware\nof the differences between the two types of planning. Further, the Korean research field has not delved into crowd crush as\na major research topic. Many Koreans have come to wonder what their governments have in place for managing crowd\ncrushes. Further, they must address not only inclusion but also cooperation, timeefficiency, advanced technologies, public awareness, political rationality, education and training, and others.\nLiterature review\nCrowd crush is a catastrophe involving a body of moving people packed tightly within a limited space (or dangerously\novercrowded), resulting in mass deaths from compressive asphyxia. Three kinds of group motivation have been\nsuggested as the major cause of crowd crush: traffic interruption, flight, and craze (Raineri, 2004: 1-14). A few cases\nof crowd crush resulting from traffic interruption have been reported in the past. Other cases of crowd crush result from\nthe high extent of an individual’s tendency to choose flight, which includes the desire to run away, escape, or avoid. The\nremaining incidents of crowd crush happen due to mass craze while rushing to obtain a common objective.\nFor effective crowd crush management, all stakeholders in the field of emergency management must respond to the needs\nof mass crowds with respect and sensitivity (Carter et al., 2018: 937-940). Without understanding the specific needs of\nmass crowds, it would be almost difficult to improve catastrophic situations in time. Emergency responders must openly\nand keenly communicate with mass crowds for their compliance, in particular before and during crowd crush. To this end,\nmajor stakeholders must sustain various guidelines and strategies for the management of potential crowd crushes.\nJohari’s window for interpersonal awareness includes four areas: the open area, the hidden area, the blind area, and the\nunknown area (Luft, 1961: 6-7). Within this context, the Itaewon crowd crush should be included in the blind area. As the\nKorean government showed its inefficiency in managing unexpected events such as the Itaewon crowd crush, many\npractitioners in foreign countries seriously discussed strategies related to crowd management. In other words, situations\nsuch as the Itaewon crowd crush were not anticipated in KR, but other countries are aware of it and have strategies in\nplace. This has increased the\nlikelihood of crowd crush.\nThere are two kinds of events according to the field of emergency management: ordinary events and special events.\nOrdinary events have four distinctive characteristics (Mair and Whitford, 2013: 6-8; Richards et al., 2022: 1-22). A local community does not have the same number of\nresources as other communities. The number of people attending such events depends on several criteria. Also, additional\nemergency mitigation, preparedness, and planning usually entail complicated processes.\nPlans are a set of targeted actions carried out to achieve a goal; in other words, planning is a lasting process that leads to\ngoal fulfillment (HACCP Mentor, 2018). Also, while planning is the whole procedure of developing a series of plans,\nmany kinds of planning are available in the field of emergency management. Two kinds of planning are chosen and\ndiscussed in this research paper, namely emergency planning versus contingency planning, to analyze the relationship\nbetween ordinary events and special events.\nTo elaborate, an emergency is a specific situation, the emergency plan entails what individuals or institutions are expected\nto do in the event of diverse emergencies. On the other hand, when an event does not go forward as planned, related\nincidents or accidents occur that are beyond the control of stakeholders. Various research areas have examined the issue, such as disaster management, safety\nscience, medical science, transportation, tourism, physics, computer science, economics, laws, police science, geography, and others, each sharing their perspective on the matter and providing tailored management alternatives. However, cooperation based on communication among multiple stakeholders incurs significant costs and\nrealizing that coordination with multiple motivations and resources is stressful and challenging. Notwithstanding,\ncooperation can overcome differences among individuals, organizations, local cultures, and other politics.\nThe findings of this research contribute to expanding the scope of emergency management literature in KR. However, this research provides findings\nto include not only emergency planning for ordinary events but also contingency planning for special events in the\nresearch scope. If contingency planning is used not only to respond to/recover\nfrom various special events but also to prepare for them, it can save a significant amount of time, especially in cases of\nemergencies (IFRC, 2012: 5-12). Because contingency planning entails making multiple decisions beforehand regarding\nemergency resources, communication/coordination among stakeholders, and other technical responses, it will help to\nensure the provision of emergency support during special events quickly enough. Hence, the amount of time spent on\ncontingency planning is surely equal to the time saved in responding to disasters.\nStakeholders must fully utilize advanced technologies, including mobile technologies, Radio Frequency Identification\n(RFID), cloud computing, drone technology, smart cities, and others, to facilitate the supplementation process (Yamin,\n2019: 231-234). These can help in monitoring, managing, and controlling both special events and ordinary events.\nFurther, officials’ slack attitude should also be improved.\n\n\nOnce contingency plans are developed for special events in local communities, those should be regularly tested and\nupdated (UN, 2008: 19-22). In general, local communities need to review developed contingency plans once a year.\nHowever, since the emergency environment has rapidly changed in the 21st century regardless of national boundaries,\nstakeholders must gather together, evaluate contingency plans, and flexibly revise them as required. In addition, the\nupdated contingency plans must be consistently and widely distributed to local residents via public communication\nchannels. Further, the implementation of these plans must be kept very simple and realistic to avoid any delay in action\n(Choularton, 2007: 25-30). To this end, the contents of contingency planning for special events should not be overly\ndetailed but just appropriately detailed.\nThe level of public awareness of special events (or safety-first mindset) must be improved. The Itaewon tragedy happened\ndespite KR having a history of 67 deaths in Busan in 1959, 31 deaths in Seoul in 1960, 12 deaths in Gwangju in 1965, and\n11 deaths in Sangju in 2005 (Lee, 2022a). Since the frequency of crowd crushes is much less than that of typhoons\naccompanied by floods or building fires, the Korean government reports or other research papers rarely include crowd\ncrushes in the category of frequent disasters in KR. Hence, the level of disaster awareness on special events including\ncrowd crushes is very limited.\nUsing the Itaewon crowd crush incident to carry out supplementation of plans will increase the situation awareness for\ncrowd management (Martella et al., 2017: 377-378). The first responders did not know the danger of potential crowd\ncrush in Itaewon, reflecting the poor level of situation awareness (i.e., too optimistic a scenario). Since the stakeholders\nlacked advanced disaster information, they could not make appropriate decisions regarding crowd crush management in\ntime and thus failed to intervene appropriately. The level of situation awareness can be increased, in particular by utilizing\nhuman perception, as well as diverse scientific tools.\nSupplementation does not mean that reliance on contingency planning fully guarantees the successful management\nof special events.       "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nThe issue of crowd crushes has been not only very complicated but\nalso uncertain. This article aimed to evaluate how situations such as\nthe Itaewon Halloween crowd crush in South Korea in 2022 can be\nbetter managed to reduce human loss.\nMethods\nQualitative analysis was the key methodology used to compare\nemergency planning for ordinary events with contingency planning\nfor special events, focusing on four stakeholders, namely\ngovernments, businesses, voluntary organizations, and other local\ncommunities.\nResults\nThe key finding was that all stakeholders would need to supplement\nemergency planning for ordinary events with contingency planning\nfor special events for the nation. They must embody cooperation,\ncutting-edge technologies, routinized updates, situation awareness,\npolitical rationality, training and exercise, and others, based on\ninclusion.\nConclusions\nThis is a pioneer study that examined the Itaewon crowd crush more\ncomprehensively than others in particular by including many disaster\n\n\fmanagement principles.\n\n\nIntroduction\nCrowd crush is a catastrophe involving a body of moving people packed tightly within a limited space (or dangerously\novercrowded), resulting in mass deaths from compressive asphyxia. In other words, crowd crush occurs when a large\nnumber of people are crammed into a small space, causing them to push one another and many of them to fall down,\nresulting in human loss and casualties (Chen et al., 2023; Mao, 2023). It has been necessary to improve the issue of\nemergency management in order to prevent the occurrence of such a crowd crush. In particular, during coronavirus\ndisease of 2019 (COVID-19), many factors, including emergency operation plans, the varied effects of the pandemic,\npeople's mentality, the level of regional safety, social contexts, and so on, have been complicatedly implicated in the\noccurrence of crowd crush.\nThree kinds of group motivation have been suggested as the major cause of crowd crush: traffic interruption\n(e.g., passenger conveyor, passenger pressure point, crowd around sport stadium, etc.), flight (also known as panic\nvia perceived threats), and competitive craze (i.e., mass rush to obtain or buy valued products) (Raineri, 2004: 1-14).\nA few cases of crowd crush resulting from traffic interruption have been reported in the past. Other cases of crowd crush\nresult from the high extent of an individual’s tendency to choose flight, which includes the desire to run away, escape, or\navoid. The remaining incidents of crowd crush happen due to mass craze while rushing to obtain a common objective.\nThe Itaewon crowd crush occurred in South Korea (hereinafter KR) during the Halloween festivities on October 29, 2022.\nItaewon is located in the Yongsan-gu district of Seoul. It is known as a foreigner-friendly area owing to its trendy\nrestaurants, nightclubs, and nightlife. On October 29, 2022, many young adults gathered in a downhill alley measuring\n40 meters long and 4 meters wide, to celebrate Halloween. As the alley filled with people, they could not move and thus\nhad no choice but to stand shoulder-to-shoulder, slowly suffocating. This resulted in the death of 158 people (i.e., 132\nKoreans and 26 foreigners), with 196 others injured (Lee et al., 2022).\nThe vast majority of Koreans expressed their sadness at the national tragedy, but a few stakeholders attempted to take\ninappropriate actions. For example, some policymakers refused to use the term “Itaewon crowd crush” and instead called\nit the 10.29 disaster (Kim, 2022). In a sense, they refused to claim responsibility by hiding behind a new name. Also, by\ngiving it a name that does not include Itaewon, they have tried to decrease the extent of stigma attached to Itaewon as a\ntragic area. Another similar example is the case of the 9/11 terror attacks. Clapp (2010) suggests the name of the 9/11\nterror attacks was not intended to avoid the stigma attached to New York City; rather, 911 is the emergency phone number\nin the United States.\nMultiple experts have scientifically analyzed the Itaewon crowd crush from different perspectives. According to one of\nthem, although KR requires restrictive safety protocols for official events, the same requirement does not apply to public\nspaces where a large number of people gather informally (Lee, 2022b). Because of such ambiguous safety measures, not a\nsingle public agency promptly claimed related responsibilities to take charge of such a massive crowd. Similarly, highranking officials admitted that the nation did not have any emergency plan to manage such events without official\norganizers (Loh, 2022). Thus, a clear gap exists between the emergency plans and the emerging reality in KR.\nWhen focusing on emergency planning for ordinary events, the nation did not equally consider contingency planning for\nspecial events (Choi et al., 2022: 1-10). In fact, many professionals in the field of emergency management are not aware\nof the differences between the two types of planning. Further, the Korean research field has not delved into crowd crush as\na major research topic. Many Koreans have come to wonder what their governments have in place for managing crowd\ncrushes. Thus, the research question guiding this study is as follows: What is the most effective way to prevent situations\nsuch as the Itaewon Halloween crowd crush in KR?\nThe objective of this research has been to review how to deal with situations similar to the Itaewon crowd crush in order to\ndecrease human loss. This study compared emergency planning for ordinary events with contingency planning for special\nevents with a focus on four analytical units, namely governments, businesses, voluntary organizations, and other local\n\n\ncommunities. The key finding is that all stakeholders must try to supplement emergency planning for ordinary events with\ncontingency planning for special events. Further, they must address not only inclusion but also cooperation, timeefficiency, advanced technologies, public awareness, political rationality, education and training, and others.\nLiterature review\nThe subject of crowd crush and related management has been researched extensively in the international community\n(Almutairi et al., 2022: 1-13). Various research areas have examined the issue, such as disaster management, safety\nscience, medical science, transportation, tourism, physics, computer science, economics, laws, police science, geography, and others, each sharing their perspective on the matter and providing tailored management alternatives. For\ninstance, Tracy H. Pearl studied the legal aspects of crowd crush in the United States (Pearl, 2015: 1-45). According to\nPearl, although crowd crushes have occurred across a vast range of places and situations in the US, the nation has often\nfailed to protect its citizens from the aftermath. In other words, statutory laws in the United States left the majority of\nAmericans unprotected from crowd crush. In particular, crowd crush was hardly litigated, as being almost based on\ncommon law (or negligence claims). Hence, this paper suggests that the courts refer to jurisprudence of crowd crush by\nrelying on modern science.\nDuring the last decade, a number of researchers systematically examined crowd simulation, in particular as computational\nresources (including artificial intelligence) dramatically increased. A group of researchers delved into crowd behaviors,\nmovements, and psychology (Barr et al., 2023: 12-13), while the other group focused on various disasters including fires,\nfloods, and earthquakes. In particular, about crowd behavior and psychology, limited information around reasonable\ncrowd has been often pointed out as a cause of psychological process. George Sidiropoulos et al. also researched the\nsubject of crowd simulation (Sidiropoulos et al., 2020: 1-23).\nP.S. Karthika et al. examined the issue of accessibility to religious mass gatherings (Karthika et al., 2022: 1-18). They\nassumed that the level of crowd management in a certain situation was strongly influenced by the behavior of various\npedestrians. They confirmed that pedestrians would like to choose their favorite routes on the basis of not only distance to\na particular destination but also the direction. Among many, these researchers recommended the importance of short-term\nroad closures to prevent the occurrence of population influx into mass pilgrimages.\nResearch on crowd crush or related accident management in KR has not been quite robust (Kim et al., 2021: 8-10). Only a\nfew researchers have examined the subject, such as an investigation into the crowd crush at the Sangju music concert in\n2005, which was a study on evacuation velocity. In addition, while making all their efforts to deal with frequent disasters\nin the nation, such as typhoons, floods, and building fires, not only researchers but also government officials have failed to\nconsider how to wisely cope with crowd crush.\nFor effective crowd crush management, all stakeholders in the field of emergency management must respond to the needs\nof mass crowds with respect and sensitivity (Carter et al., 2018: 937-940). Without understanding the specific needs of\nmass crowds, it would be almost difficult to improve catastrophic situations in time. Emergency responders must openly\nand keenly communicate with mass crowds for their compliance, in particular before and during crowd crush. To this end,\nmajor stakeholders must sustain various guidelines and strategies for the management of potential crowd crushes.\nJohari’s window for interpersonal awareness includes four areas: the open area (i.e., not only you but also others know it.),\nthe hidden area (i.e., while only you know it, others do not know it.), the blind area (i.e., while you do not know it, others\nknow it), and the unknown area (i.e., nobody knows it.) (Luft, 1961: 6-7). Within this context, the Itaewon crowd crush\nshould be included in the blind area. As the Korean government showed its inefficiency in managing unexpected events\nsuch as the Itaewon crowd crush, many practitioners in foreign countries seriously discussed strategies related to crowd\nmanagement. In other words, situations such as the Itaewon crowd crush were not anticipated in KR, but other countries\nare aware of it and have strategies in place. Therefore, the tragedy falls in the blind area of the Johari window.\nSeveral similar tragedies have transpired in the past across the globe (Johnston, 2022). For instance, 11 young students\nlost their lives while trying to enter a The Who concert in Cincinnati, Ohio, in December 1979. Similarly, 97 soccer fans\nwere killed in Sheffield, the United Kingdom, in April 1989, due to a bottleneck phenomenon. About 2,400 pilgrims were\nkilled near the city of Mecca, Saudi Arabia, during the Hajj pilgrimage in September 2015. Approximately 130 soccer\nfans were killed in Malang, Indonesia, during a mass stampede in October 2022. In San Salvador, which is the capital of El\nSalvador, in May 2023, at least 12 people were killed due to a crowd crush caused by the overrunning of barricades during\nthe football match (The Gurdian, 2023).\n\n\n\nPotential crowd crush situations, in particular during the post-COVID period, were recently predicted by a few\nprofessionals (Morton and Power, 2022: 1-9). The pandemic has impacted the daily lives of almost all individuals\naround the world. Against the backdrop of social distancing, lockdown, travel constraints, and others, so many people\nhave been willing to gather together in public places. Despite COVID-19 variants or sub-variants still circulating in the\nair, people are now trying to relieve their mental stress or psychological impact by participating in crowd gatherings. This\nhas increased the likelihood of crowd crush.\nThere are two kinds of events according to the field of emergency management: ordinary events and special events.\nOrdinary events have four distinctive characteristics (Mair and Whitford, 2013: 6-8; Richards et al., 2022: 1-22). First,\nordinary events are organized and held in various local communities on a regular basis; in other words, they are routine\nevents. Second, ordinary events do not require many resources. Third, although ordinary events involve a number of\npeople, they are recurring events. Fourth, ordinary events do not require special permits. Examples include private parties,\nprofessional baseball games, political conventions, workshops, and others.\nOn the other hand, special events have four distinct elements that characterize them (FEMA, 2013). They are non-routine\nevents held in a local community. Special events put a substantial strain or pressure on local resources. Further, they\ngenerally involve a significant number of people. In addition, special events require local agencies to work on additional\nemergency mitigation, emergency preparedness, and emergency planning. Examples include spontaneous events,\ncontroversial political events, regional competitions, and others.\nHowever, it is not easy for even experts to determine if a specific event such as the Itaewon Halloween party must be\ncategorized as an ordinary event or a special event. As such, political rallies routinely occur in various democratic\ncountries, but nobody knows exactly when they will be held. A local community does not have the same number of\nresources as other communities. The number of people attending such events depends on several criteria. Also, additional\nemergency mitigation, preparedness, and planning usually entail complicated processes.\nPlans are a set of targeted actions carried out to achieve a goal; in other words, planning is a lasting process that leads to\ngoal fulfillment (HACCP Mentor, 2018). Also, while planning is the whole procedure of developing a series of plans,\nmany kinds of planning are available in the field of emergency management. Two kinds of planning are chosen and\ndiscussed in this research paper, namely emergency planning versus contingency planning, to analyze the relationship\nbetween ordinary events and special events.\nTo elaborate, an emergency is a specific situation, the emergency plan entails what individuals or institutions are expected\nto do in the event of diverse emergencies. On the other hand, when an event does not go forward as planned, related\nincidents or accidents occur that are beyond the control of stakeholders. At this time, a contingency plan is implemented\ndepending on the circumstances. A good example is the difference between traffic management (e.g., traffic plan,\nevacuation plan, traffic diversion, etc.) and comprehensive mobility plan (e.g., inclusion, improvement of user experience, etc.) (Kanaujiya and Tiwari, 2022: 267-268).\nIn particular, in terms of emergency preparedness, both emergency planning and contingency planning has substantially\nrequired emergency training and exercise (Russo and Rindone, 2014: 991-993). Training and exercise include discussion-oriented ones (e.g., orientation, seminar, workshop, and games, among others) and operation-oriented ones (e.g.,\nfunctional exercise, drill, full-scale exercise, or else). When reminding that training and exercise may contribute to risk\nreduction, appropriate evaluation methods have accordingly been supported. As such, to verify urban evacuation, it has\nbeen necessary to fully develop and utilize related evaluation methods such as analyzing disaster capabilities, resources,\nand plan adequacy for discussion-oriented exercise versus analyzing communication, cooperation, management, and\nequipment for operation-oriented exercise.\nIn summary, emergency planning is related to setting up a usual plan(s) for ordinary events, whereas contingency\nplanning includes the provision of a revised plan(s) for dealing with the worst-case scenario, or a Plan B for special events.\nIn other words, emergency planning is the process of increasing emergency preparedness to respond to different early\nalerts and warnings. Contingency planning, on the other hand, is the process of planning for and managing multiple\nunfolding emergencies.\nTaking the above all into consideration, this paper presents a systematic review of KR’s Itaewon crowd crush as a\npioneering study. This paper analyzed the subject more comprehensively than previous Korean studies on the matter.\nSimilarly, this study applied many disaster management principles suggested by international researchers, to the case in\npoint.\n\n\nMethods\nThe methodology used in this research work here is qualitative content analysis in systematic reviews. In general,\nsystematic reviews address answerable and specific questions, while trying to diagnose the key issue and then develop an\nalternative plan (Campbell et al., 2015: 154-160). Shortly, qualitative content analysis is to convert raw text data into\nthemes or categories via flexible interpretation with inference. The analysis was divided into four steps: planning the\nresearch structure, collecting qualitative text data, analyzing qualitative text data, and presenting qualitative text data\n(Qualtrics, 2022).\nFigure 1 shows the research structure to include a research question, units of analysis, thematic categories, the direction of\nresearch process, and others. The research has allocated four analytical units such as governments, businesses, voluntary\norganizations, and other local communities. These four units have been traditionally treated as key stakeholders or players\nin the field of emergency management (FEMA, 2021). Two thematic categories included are emergency planning for\nordinary events versus contingency planning for special events. In fact, those two thematic categories comprehensively\ncombine afore-mentioned four analytical units.\nInformation sources and search strategy\nFor collecting qualitative text data, several search engines were utilized, such as ScienceDirect, Oxford University Press,\nJSTOR, SCOPUS, PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest, EBSCO, and Google.com, among others. The keywords used\ninclude “contingency planning for special events,” “ordinary events vs. special events,” “crowd management,” “crowd\ncrush vs. stampede,” “emergency planning vs. contingency planning,” “crowd management in Korea,” “training and\nexercise,” and others. At any rate, reporting results\nbased on PRISMA is to increase not only transparency but also accuracy of systematic reviews (Page et al., 2022:\n156-159).\nEmergency planning for ordinary events\nGovernments\nThe Basic Plan on National Safety Management (BPNSM) has let the Ministry of the Interior and Safety (MOIS) take\ncharge of safety and emergency management at the national level. BPNSM has served KR as the most comprehensive\nemergency plan in the region. At the local level, local policemen around Itaewon are supposed to deal with mass crowds\nwith the support of local firefighters and other local government employees (MOIS, 2023). Nonetheless, only a few\nofficials were aware of the potential danger of public gatherings around Itaewon; thus, the local community failed to\ndecisively deal with it. According to the minister of MOIS, the police did not have enough emergency responders to deal\nwith the Itaewon crowd crush because they had to manage other political rallies at that time.\nBusinesses\nMany local businesses were located around the anonymous alley within Itaewon, while fully or partially providing\nemergency services and products for residents or visitors. Nonetheless, the alley was narrower than expected in terms of\n\n\nspace because of unlawful construction around those businesses to include multiple buildings. For instance, the Hamilton\nHotel had illegally extended its building toward the alley. Some other small businesses, such as cuisine restaurants,\ndancing clubs, drinking bars, and shops renovated or extended their buildings, without following the Korean building\ncode. These illegal activities partially but surely contributed to the bottleneck phenomenon in Itaewon.\nVoluntary organizations\nJust during or after the occurrence of the Itaewon crowd crush, quite a few spontaneous volunteers played an important\nrole in responding to it (Park, 2022). Spontaneous volunteers were not pre-registered volunteers but were those who\nresponded to urgent and temporary needs without being paid. Without their voluntary activities such as cardiopulmonary\nresuscitation (CPR), the casualty number would have been bigger. They also attempted to translate what foreign visitors\nasked for while cleaning up debris from the emergency spot. Similarly, some business owners volunteered to provide their\nbusiness spaces or services for carrying out disaster relief. Notwithstanding, voluntary activities were limited to the\nphases of emergency response/recovery.\nOther local communities\nOther local communities, including mass media, local residents, tourists, and others, did not pay much attention to the\nmass crowd gathering around Itaewon during Halloween parties. Almost nobody as other local communities would have\nexpected the occurrence of this level of tragedy. Although some mass media reported the popularity of Halloween parties\nin the nation, they did not seriously discuss the potential risks of mass crowds at all. Thus, not only local residents but even\ntourists were uninformed of the potential danger around Itaewon. In summary, other local communities did not have an\nunderstanding of personal safety during Itaewon Halloween parties.\nContingency planning for special events\nGovernments\nAll government employees should have approached the Itaewon crowd being similar to how they approach political\nrallies in the region. Even though the Itaewon even did not have any formal organizer, such incidents must be included in\nthe government’s emergency management protocol. In many senses, that should be the role of government in the 21st\ncentury. Therefore, the central government needs to set up the Korean version of the National Response Framework\n(NRF) beyond the BPNSM. Appropriate roles and responsibilities must be assigned to each stakeholder before the\noccurrence of special events. For example, local policemen being the front line of contingency defense must control\nunexpected crowds in time. As supporting institutions, not only local government officials but also local firefighters must\ndirectly or indirectly help local policemen around mass crowds.\nBusinesses\nVarious local businesses around Itaewon need to address the issue of structural safety (or load capacities) as much as is\nfeasible to prevent recurrence (Alkhadim et al., 2018: 26-28). They can no longer limit their activities to providing\nemergency products and services for the masses. As spectator safety is important during special events, local businesses\nmust consider the safety of their structures, including not only building structures but also temporary platform structures.\nSimilarly, businesses must continue to implement spot improvements to reduce localized bottleneck phenomena. Further,\nall structures around local businesses must entirely comply with the Korean building code.\nVoluntary organizations\nVoluntary organizations can recruit various volunteers to expand their activities into the phase of emergency preparedness, as well as the phases of emergency response/recovery regarding special events. This can continue to prevent the\noccurrence of another crowd crush tragedy. Thus, emergency training and exercises need to provide information to the\naffiliated volunteers on how to prepare for special events (e.g., crowd monitoring, emergency warning, etc.). Emergency\neducation should spread the knowledge on contingency preparedness to schools, community organizations, and potential\nspontaneous volunteers.\nOther local communities\nMass media must spread information on the danger of special events to residents, tourists and visitors, and others\nduring the phase of emergency preparedness, without mentioning reports of emergency prevention/mitigation/response/\n\n\nrecovery (Eriksson, 2018: 11-12). Mass media may create, disseminate, and even educate all individuals on the danger of\nmass crowds in advance while considering crowd personalities. Vice versa, by recognizing that personal safety is one of\nthe most important strategies in the field, all residents, tourists, and other visitors must open their communication\nchannels with media advisories, emergency alerts, and other safety issues.\nImplications\nEmergency planning for ordinary events does not have a unilateral relationship but a reciprocal relationship with\ncontingency planning for special events (Koenti, 2022: 1-9). Strictly speaking, the fundamental principles of emergency\nplanning for ordinary events are very similar (or even the same) to that of contingency planning for special events, but the\nlatter is slightly bigger than the former in terms of its scope. Thus, emergency planning for ordinary events should not be\nreplaced by contingency planning for special events but supplemented with the latter.\nThe goal of emergency management may not be smoothly achieved unless the needs of all people are considered when\nmanaging its scope (Sheehy et al., 2022: 1-17). When marginalized people suffer from the impact of multiple disasters far\nmore than prosperous people, the needs of marginalized people must be equally addressed in the name of diversity.\nWithout substantial inclusion, the field of emergency management cannot reduce the physical or social impacts of\ndisasters.\nThe fact that KR needs to supplement its emergency planning for ordinary events with contingency planning for special\nevents strongly indicates that the nation must embody inclusion to the fullest extent. Management of events involving\nunexpected crowds or mass crowds without organizers has not been equally included in the Korean field of emergency\nmanagement yet. In general, capacity assessment of organizing agency (whether it is a government agency or others\naround the Itaewon crush) has to be substantially carried out by calculating both its strengths and limits toward the event.\nTo date, the group of mass crowd used to belong to the group of marginalized people in KR. As Korean society develops,\nthe nation must address the concept of explicit and fair inclusion in the emergency management field.\nFurther, various stakeholders must cooperate to achieve successful supplementation (Persson and Granberg, 2021: 13361338). When a few stakeholders fail to manage crowd crush, cooperation with other stakeholders is strongly recommended. However, cooperation based on communication among multiple stakeholders incurs significant costs and\nrealizing that coordination with multiple motivations and resources is stressful and challenging. Notwithstanding,\ncooperation can overcome differences among individuals, organizations, local cultures, and other politics.\nIn particular, regarding coordination mechanism among four major stakeholders, those parties need to synchronize their\nactivities on the way to appropriate supplementation (McKing Consulting Corporation, 2008: 5-16). All they should\nfundamentally have the extent of willingness to achieve the final goal of emergency management (or not to repeat another\nItaewon Halloween tragedy). Besides, while further carrying out own roles and responsibilities under the big frame of\nnew NRF, each stakeholder will mutually adjust to the others’ efforts by extensively arranging a number of meetings,\ninformation exchange, feedback loops, policy change, and others.\nThe findings of this research contribute to expanding the scope of emergency management literature in KR. The Korean\nresearch field includes just emergency planning for ordinary events in its scope. However, this research provides findings\nto include not only emergency planning for ordinary events but also contingency planning for special events in the\nresearch scope. On a greater scale, the study adds to the literature on in-depth crowd management in international\nresearch.\nSupplementation efforts will have further implications. If contingency planning is used not only to respond to/recover\nfrom various special events but also to prepare for them, it can save a significant amount of time, especially in cases of\nemergencies (IFRC, 2012: 5-12). Because contingency planning entails making multiple decisions beforehand regarding\nemergency resources, communication/coordination among stakeholders, and other technical responses, it will help to\nensure the provision of emergency support during special events quickly enough. Hence, the amount of time spent on\ncontingency planning is surely equal to the time saved in responding to disasters.\nStakeholders must fully utilize advanced technologies, including information and communication technology, mobile\ntechnology, Radio Frequency Identification (RFID), cloud computing, drone technology, smart cities, and others, to\nfacilitate the supplementation process (Wang, 2023; Yamin, 2019: 231-234). In particular, drones and closed circuit\ntelevision (CCTV) have been in high demand in the field, particularly when it comes to real-time crowd management.\nThese can help in monitoring, managing, and controlling both special events and ordinary events. Further, officials’ slack\nattitude should also be improved, when admitting that many places have already set up cutting-edge monitoring structure.\n\n\nOnce contingency plans are developed for special events in local communities, those should be regularly tested and\nupdated (UN, 2008: 19-22). In general, local communities need to review developed contingency plans once a year.\nHowever, since the emergency environment has rapidly changed in the 21st century regardless of national boundaries,\nstakeholders must gather together, evaluate contingency plans, and flexibly revise them as required. In addition, the\nupdated contingency plans must be consistently and widely distributed to local residents via public communication\nchannels. Further, the implementation of these plans must be kept very simple and realistic to avoid any delay in action\n(Choularton, 2007: 25-30). To this end, the contents of contingency planning for special events should not be overly\ndetailed but just appropriately detailed.\nThe level of public awareness of special events (or safety-first mindset) must be improved. The Itaewon tragedy happened\ndespite KR having a history of 67 deaths in Busan in 1959, 31 deaths in Seoul in 1960, 12 deaths in Gwangju in 1965, and\n11 deaths in Sangju in 2005 (Lee, 2022a). Since the frequency of crowd crushes is much less than that of typhoons\naccompanied by floods or building fires, the Korean government reports or other research papers rarely include crowd\ncrushes in the category of frequent disasters in KR. Hence, the level of disaster awareness on special events including\ncrowd crushes is very limited.\nUsing the Itaewon crowd crush incident to carry out supplementation of plans will increase the situation awareness for\ncrowd management (Martella et al., 2017: 377-378). The first responders did not know the danger of potential crowd\ncrush in Itaewon, reflecting the poor level of situation awareness (i.e., too optimistic a scenario). Since the stakeholders\nlacked advanced disaster information, they could not make appropriate decisions regarding crowd crush management in\ntime and thus failed to intervene appropriately. The level of situation awareness can be increased, in particular by utilizing\nhuman perception, as well as diverse scientific tools.\nSupplementation does not mean that reliance on contingency planning fully guarantees the successful management\nof special events. Although sticking to contingency plans looks pretty effective for managing emergencies, the path to\nachieving them is still difficult. In a sense, even though contingency planning plays many roles in producing the extent of\nrigidity around special events (or allowing various participants to rigidly stick to the plan), it does not ensure the complete\nsuccess of future emergency management, which includes various thorny issues.\nNonetheless, the supplementation principle must embody political rationality on a big scale (Eriksson and McConnell,\n2011: 95-97). In fact, politics around contingency planning and appropriate plans helps the elected officials and decisionmakers to keep their reputation intact, delivers the image of controlled contingency for the public, and keeps the\nunhindered goal of national emergency management. Thus, by fully preparing for unexpected events, those high-ranking\nauthorities may boost their political success in particular not only by managing special events but also by taking care of\npublic fears of unforeseen catastrophes.\nEmergency education, training, and exercise have to be fully employed to facilitate supplementation in KR. Many\nindividuals and institutions fail to understand the exact differences between emergency planning for ordinary events and\ncontingency planning for special events. Similarly, they are unfamiliar with the supplementation of the former with the\nlatter. KR has to appropriately rely on these three ways for effective emergency management. While emergency education\nmust provide generalized fundamentals for supplementation pursuit, emergency training and exercise can let participants\npractice detailed actions.\nConclusion\nThis case study reviewed the Itaewon Halloween crowd crush in order to provide guidelines on how to prevent similar\ntragedies in KR or manage such a situation more efficiently to reduce human loss. The study identified previous studies,\nmanagement challenges, appropriate alternatives, and implications. In particular, emergency planning for ordinary events\nand contingency planning for special events were compared and contrasted.\nThis study recommends that all stakeholders, including governments, businesses, voluntary organizations, and other\nlocal communities, must try to supplement emergency planning for ordinary events with contingency planning for\nspecial events in KR. Each stakeholder must work toward the establishment of Korean NRF, structural safety, expansion\nof voluntary activities, personal safety, and others sequentially. At the same time, all stakeholders must comply with\ncoordination, time efficiency, regular updates, realistic approach, public awareness, political savvy, education and\ntraining, and others, to achieve full inclusion.\nThis paper is a pioneering study of Korean emergency management. In particular, the process of this research has been\nexamined and then supported by qualitative content analysis. As a strength of this article, qualitative content analysis has\n\n\nplayed a role in comprehensively reproducing the whole process of Itaewon Halloween crowd crush by interpreting a\nnumber of text data. On the other hand, a limitation of this research is that it is limited to KR. Since this study focuses on\nthe Korean crowd crush only, the results of this paper might not have equal impacts on international researchers, despite\nthe frequent reliance on principles of international emergency management.\nResearchers are anticipated to further evaluate the Itaewon crowd crush in the near future. They may diversely interpret\nthe national tragedy from the perspective of law, economy, psychology, computer science, road construction, and others.\nBesides, the impact of COVID-19 will continue to generate mass crowds and subsequent crowd crushes, irrespective of\nnational borders.
