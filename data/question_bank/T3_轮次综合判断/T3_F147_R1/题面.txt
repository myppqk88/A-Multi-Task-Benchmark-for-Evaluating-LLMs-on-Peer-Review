The history of writing\ninstruction in Pakistani universities has been rich in grammar precision and fixed modes of expression instead of\nrhetorical elegance and thinking (Mahmood et al., 2020). Students need help in planning, organizing, and revising their\nwork so that they can write coherently. Literature review\n2.1 Active learning theory: Foundations and principles\nThe active learning process is inherently a challenge to the traditional transmission model, which inculcates a student as\nan active knowledge builder, as opposed to a receiver of the knowledge. The working definition of student-centered\nlearning by Bonwell and Eison (1991) refers to any instructional approach that involves students in the process of\nlearning, stressing that students should do more than merely being asked to listen; they need to read, write, talk, or be\nasked to solve problems. The idea of this definition focused on interaction, participation, and thinking, not on the\nconsumption of content.\nPrince (2004) extended this framework by emphasizing the metacognitive dimension, noting that active learning requires\nstudents to engage in “meaningful learning activities and think about what they are doing” (p. 223). This metacognitive\nemphasis becomes particularly relevant in AI-mediated contexts where tools can facilitate reflective practice through\nfeedback and revision cycles.\nFreeman et al.’s (2014) comprehensive meta-analysis of 225 studies provided compelling empirical support, revealing\nthat active learning increased student performance by 6% and reduced failure rates by 36% compared to traditional\nlecturing. Their conclusion that active learning represents “the preferred, evidence-based choice for instructors” (p. 8410)\nhas been replicated across disciplines, establishing active learning’s superiority over instructor-centered methods and\nproviding the theoretical foundation for AI-enhanced pedagogical approaches.\n2.2 Theoretical frameworks of active learning\nActive learning theory draws from three foundational frameworks that inform AI integration in educational contexts.\nSocial constructivism emphasizes Vygotsky’s (1978) Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), where AI tools function as\n“more knowledgeable others” providing scaffolding through immediate feedback and collaborative dialogue. Johnson\nand Johnson (2009) extend this through collaborative learning theory, demonstrating that learning is enhanced when\nstudents work toward shared goals—processes readily facilitated through AI-mediated peer collaboration.\nThe main theory of AIs-enhanced active learning cycles is experiential learning theory. The four-step model offered by\nKolb (1984), concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation,\ncorresponds directly to collaborations with AI-writing: students take concrete steps in writing with AI assistance,\ncontemplate the results produced by AIs, theoretically abstract writing concepts based on the suggestions provided by the\nAI, and implement experimental approaches to revision.\n2.3 Active learning in writing instruction\nCollaborative and back and forth writing pedagogy inherently lends itself to active learning. In their meta-analysis,\nGraham and Perin (2007) also found that active learning strategies that show significant improvement in writing are\ncollaborative writing (effect size = 0.75), strategy instruction (effect size = 0.82), and peer assistance (effect size = 0.89).\nThese results indicate the usefulness of AI integration, which can provide more teamwork experiences and direction.\nFlower and Hayes (1981) redefined composition as a cycle of planning, translating, and reviewing-with metacognitive\nactivity and reflection on revision. AI-based solutions can facilitate every step with idea generation, vocabulary\nproposals, and automatic feedback. Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987) differentiate between the knowledge-transforming\nprocess of writing, which presupposes an active treatment of content and development of arguments, and the knowledgetelling paradigm in which AI can become an aid to critical reflection and perspective analysis.\n\n\n2.4 AI applications in writing instruction: Evolution and effectiveness\nAs writing applications have developed beyond rudimentary grammar checkers, this has also meant a shift toward active\nlearning pedagogies and a greater emphasis on student agency and collaboration (Nazari et al., 2021; Imran &\nAlmusharraf, 2024).\n2.4.1 Early systems and feedback tools\nEarly experiments were on automated feedback and grammatical markup. Purcell et al. (2013) discovered that Google\nDocs supported enhanced writing with the help of scaffolding and collaborative writing features. Nonetheless, tools\ndemanded organized instructional schemes that supported critical participation, which was the core of active learning\ntheory (Nobles & Paganucci, 2015).\n2.4.2 Advanced evaluation systems\nSophisticated automated writing evaluation (AWE) technology was an important step forward. Fitria (2021) has\ndocumented that AI-based applications such as Grammarly fostered metacognitive awareness and reflective practice\nthrough facilitating critical self-assessment. Chang et al. (2021) reported statistically significant gains when AI tools gave\ninstantaneous, domain-specific feedback in addition to increasing the independence of the learners-commonly referred to\nas active learning.\n2.4.3 Contemporary generative AI integration\nRecent generative AI research provides new active-learning opportunities. Su et al. (2023) showed how argumentative\nwriting is done through collaboration of students with the AI partner, ChatGPT, as a cognitive partner to support\ncollaborative learning and knowledge construction. Song and Song (2023) identified increases in coherence, order, and\ndrive when AI was incorporated into structured frameworks, with a call to employment in moderation to encourage\nagency over reliance. Huang et al. (2023) contrasted AI-aided and conventional settings. Under the AI setting, where\nstudents received personalized prompts and guidance to enhance revision tasks, academic performance was more\neffective, and engagement was higher than in the traditional setting.\n2.5 Ethical AI integration and active learning\nThe combination of AI technology and active learning pedagogy proposes significant ethical issues that need to be\ndiscussed to ensure that the educational outcomes of the integration of technology are productive, not destructive. Active\nlearning theory is based on the principles of student choice, critical thinking, and genuine engagement, which may be\nnegatively affected by the misuse of AI tools.\nRegarding ethical use of AI in higher education, policy guidelines were created by UNESCO (2021) and the Higher\nEducation Commission of Pakistan (2023), which were outlined not only as essentials to preserve academic integrity but\nalso to take advantage of the technological affordances. These rules reflect the active learning theory, encouraging\nresponsibility, critical thinking, and reflection of the students engaged in learning conditions provided by an AI system.\n2.6 Research gaps and theoretical synthesis\nAlthough research on AI in writing instruction is growing, there are noticeable gaps in how generative AI tools can be\nethically integrated into active learning approaches, especially in EFL/ESL settings where resources are limited. The\ncurrent state of research on AI effectiveness focuses on pedagogical curriculum, supporting student agency, critical\nthinking, and group learning insufficiently. Most studies have been carried out in high-resource learning environments\nand do not provide many insights into how AI tools can tackle systemic issues like excessive classroom sizes, poor quality\nteacher feedback, and the availability of resources to provide personalized learning. The present study fills these gaps by\nexploring how ethically scaffolded generative AI tools can be used to promote active learning in EFL/ESL writing\ninstruction.\n3. Discussion\n5.1 AI-mediated active learning: Theoretical validation and performance enhancement\nThe significant improvement in the experimental group (Z = -6.325, p < .001) provides strong evidence that AI tools\neffectively supported Kolb’s (1984) experiential learning cycle in EFL/ESL writing. Progressive improvement, evident\nfrom Test 5 (p < .001), aligns with Kolb’s proposition that learning deepens through repeated cycles of experience,\nreflection, conceptualization, and experimentation. This extends Freeman et al.’s (2014) meta-analysis showing active\nlearning’s superiority across disciplines, validating its principles in AI-enhanced EFL contexts. The 6–8 week development period suggests cognitive partnerships need sustained engagement, consistent with recursive learning models.\nThe control group’s modest gains (Z = -2.128, p = 0.033) reinforce Prince’s (2004) claim that passive instruction is less\neffective. Crucially, this study demonstrates AI as a mediator enabling active learning even in resource-limited contexts\nwhere traditional implementation is constrained.\nQualitative findings highlight a shift from viewing AI as a tool to treating it as a collaborator, supporting Luckin’s (2017)\ncognitive partnership model. This contrasts with Chang et al. (2021) and Fitria (2021), who treated AI mainly as feedback.\nEthical reflection and structured cycles appear to have transformed AI use into genuine collaboration. Student reflections\n—“I learned to combine its suggestions with my own thoughts”—demonstrate Kolb’s abstract conceptualization stage.\nUnlike Yan (2023), who reported dependency concerns, the ethical protocol here mitigated risks while preserving AI’s\ncognitive benefits.\n\n\n\n5.2 Active learning processes: Evidence and theoretical alignment\nMetacognitive awareness, reflected in comments like “I started to notice when I was depending too much on AI,”\nvalidates Bonwell and Eison’s (1991) assertion that active learning requires reflection. This distinguishes the study from\nprior AI writing research. Liu et al. (2021) also noted metacognitive improvements but lacked the systematic ethical\nframework used here. Structured prompts ensured AI use remained cognitively demanding, aligning with Prince’s (2004)\ndefinition of meaningful learning. AI tools enhanced rather than inhibited collaboration. Students reported increased peer\ninteraction through shared AI engagement and reflection tasks, extending Johnson and Johnson’s (2009) collaborative\nlearning theory into AI-mediated contexts. This contrasts with Anderson and Rainie’s (2023) concerns about AI reducing\ncollaboration, as the partnership model positioned AI as a facilitator rather than a replacement.\n5.3 Gender-differentiated engagement: Theoretical implications\nFemale students showed 19% higher engagement and greater improvement (p < .001) than males (p = .010) in\nAI-mediated active learning. In contrast, in the control group, males improved significantly (p < .001) while females’\ngains were insignificant (p = .062). This reversal suggests AI-mediated environments may align better with learning\npreferences associated with reflective and collaborative engagement.\nQualitative data point to stronger responsiveness among females to reflection and metacognition in Kolb’s cycle. This\nextends Al Mahmud’s (2023) findings by showing that pedagogical framing mediates gendered outcomes. These results\nsupport Vygotsky’s (1978) view that scaffolding must match learner characteristics. While both genders benefited, the\nframework appears particularly effective for those favoring reflective collaboration. The significant gains for males (p =\n.010) suggest the approach benefits all learners while amplifying certain preferences.\n5.4 Resource constraint mitigation: Theoretical and practical significance\nAI tools addressed challenges of large EFL classes, validating Blikstein’s (2016) proposition of AI as an equity tool.\nReports of receiving “personalized feedback whenever I needed it” illustrate how cognitive partnerships democratized\naccess to individualized instruction. This extends Popenici and Kerr (2017) and Renz et al. (2020), providing empirical\nevidence that AI can overcome barriers to active learning. The model resolved the tension between large class sizes and\npersonalized feedback, traditionally an obstacle to active pedagogy. By enabling active learning under constraints, the\nfindings support Godwin-Jones’s (2019) view that technology can enable pedagogy rather than replace it. Critics argue\ntechnology cannot solve pedagogical challenges, yet this study shows that AI integrated within grounded frameworks can\nsustain student-centered practices even in difficult contexts.\n5.5 Technology acceptance and active learning integration\nHigh perceived usefulness (79.7%) and intention for continued use (86%) validate the Technology Acceptance Model\n(TAM). Importantly, acceptance was strengthened by pedagogical integration rather than AI as a standalone tool. Student\nresponses such as “they help me think better, not think less” reveal critical evaluation rather than passive adoption,\naddressing Davis’s (1989) concerns about uncritical acceptance. Combined with ethical reflection, high adoption rates\nsuggest responsible use. Unlike Yan (2023), who reported dependency, this study shows that ethical protocols mediated\nsustainable adoption. Weekly reflections transformed acceptance into critical engagement, modifying TAM by highlighting ethics as a key factor in technology sustainability.\n5.6 Theoretical synthesis: Active learning–GenAI synergy framework\nFindings validate the Active Learning–GenAI Synergy Framework, showing AI can function as cognitive partners,\nmetacognitive mirrors, and equity tools. This extends theories of AI in education by embedding technology within active\nlearning, rather than as separate interventions. The achievement of the framework concerning the propensity to write and\nthe acquisition of digital literacies endorses the vision of cognitive partnerships proposed by Holmes et al. (2019) as a\ntangible illustration of resource-limited situations. It was established by carrying out an implementation using ethical\nprocedures and experiential learning processes. AI reduced cognitive load while enhancing higher-order thinking,\nchallenging assumptions that assistance undermines cognition. This suggests AI-mediated learning can both reduce\nroutine burdens and increase complex engagement. The finding extends Gayed et al.’s (2022) work on hybrid systems,\nshowing cognitive partnerships simultaneously lighten basic demands while fostering deeper processes. The framework\nachieved this duality by structuring AI use around progressive experiential cycles.\n5.7 Implications for EFL writing pedagogy\nThe shift from product- to process-focused instruction validates Flower and Hayes’s (1981) theory while extending it into\nAI-mediated contexts, supporting Graham and Perin’s (2007) process approach, highlighting AI-enabled individualized\nscaffolding in large classes, sustaining agency and critical thought. Improvements in coherence, structure, and revision\ndirectly address Pakistani EFL/ESL challenges noted by Mahmood et al. (2020). Multimodal integration expanded\n\n\nacademic writing to include digital composition, offering practical strategies for contemporary literacy in constrained\nsettings.\n5.8 Unexpected findings and theoretical implications\nHigh engagement combined with critical awareness challenges dependency concerns, showing students can maintain an\nauthorial voice while using AI. This supports the concept of “critical dependence”, where learners leverage AI while\nretaining evaluation and agency. The observed 6–8 week maturation of cognitive partnerships highlights sustained\nengagement needs, countering assumptions of immediate technological benefit while validating Kolb’s recursive model.\nProgressive improvement suggests long-term integration is preferable to short-term adoption, offering guidance for\nsustainable AI pedagogy.\n5.9 Limitations and future directions\nThe cultural and institutional specificity of Pakistani higher education may limit generalizability, requiring cross-cultural\nvalidation. While 15 weeks was sufficient to show improvements, long-term retention requires longitudinal studies.\nFuture research should test diverse AI tools and strategies across disciplines to determine optimal models and\ntransferability. Gender-based differences require deeper investigation to develop equitable integration strategies and\nuncover mechanisms driving participation patterns.\n6. Conclusion\nThese results add strong evidence that ethically enriched AI-based tools can become an effective method of active\nlearning in EFL/ESL writing training, especially when used in settings with limited resources. The effective use of the\nActive Learning-GenAI Synergy Framework showed that thinking in pairs involving students and AI tools has the\npotential to improve writing skills and support the development of digital literacy skills, as well as preserve academic\nintegrity. The theoretical contribution also moves the active learning theory into the arena of AI-admitted contexts and\nprovides practical NTS of implementation in response to the practical constraints EFL teachers experience globally. The\nfact that the tools of AI can be used as cognitive partners, metacognitive mirrors, and equity tools, as demonstrated in the\nstudy, gives a holistic approach to the sustainable integration of technology that will still retain humanistic educational\nvalues through exploiting the affordances of technology.\nMost significantly, the research establishes that the pedagogical framework mediating AI interaction—rather than the\ntechnology itself—determines educational outcomes. Research methodology\n3.1 Research design and theoretical alignment\nThe pragmatic mixed-methods action research design is employed by combining quantitative performance measures and\na qualitative phenomenological evaluation through which the full comprehension of AI-mediated active learning\nexperiences was understood. The action research framework’s emphasis on cyclical reflection, pedagogical innovation,\nand collaborative inquiry aligns with both active learning theory and ethical AI integration practices (Stringer, 2014;\nMcNiff & Whitehead, 2011).\nThe design entails pre-test/post-test experimental and control group comparisons supplemented with semi-structured\ninterviews, where methodological triangulation explored the relationship between the AI-enhanced active learning\nperformance (quantitative measure) and the quality of student engagement coupled with ethical awareness development\n(qualitative measure). Action research aspect facilitated ongoing pedagogical upgrades over the course of intervention\n(15 weeks) and made real-time alterations to AI integration plans through student feedback- in keeping with the iterative,\nreflective qualities of active learning and ethical AI usage.\n3.2 Theoretical framework integration: Active learning theory and Kolb’s experiential learning cycle\nThe methodological design is grounded in Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, which posits that “learning is a\ncontinuous, cyclical process involving four key stages: Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation” (Kolb, 1984). This theory states that “learning is the process whereby\nknowledge is created through the transformation of experience. Knowledge results from the combination of grasping\nand transforming experience”.\nThe AI-enhanced writing tasks were systematically designed to facilitate each stage of Kolb’s experiential learning cycle:\n1. Concrete Experience (CE): Students engaged in hands-on writing activities using AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude\nAI, Meta AI, Canva) to draft, revise, and create multimodal compositions\n2. Reflective Observation (RO): Weekly ethical reflection prompts encouraged students to critically analyze AI\noutputs, evaluate their own contributions, and observe the collaborative writing process\n3. Abstract Conceptualization (AC): Students developed an understanding of writing principles, ethical AI use,\nand metacognitive strategies through guided reflection and peer discussion\n4. Active Experimentation (AE): Students applied insights from reflection to subsequent writing tasks, experimenting with different AI collaboration strategies and testing ethical integration approaches\n\n\n\nActive learning principles integration\nThe methodology incorporated core active learning principles identified by Prince (2004) and Freeman et al. (2014):\n• Student Engagement: AI tools facilitated interactive writing processes that required continuous student\nparticipation and decision-making\n• Collaborative Learning: Peer review activities were enhanced through AI-assisted feedback and structured\ngroup reflection sessions\n• Higher-Order Thinking: Ethical reflection prompts (See Appendix 3) promoted critical evaluation, synthesis,\nand evaluation of AI-generated content\n• Immediate Feedback: AI tools provided real-time responses that enabled rapid iteration and experiential\nlearning cycles\n• Student Agency: Learners maintained control over AI collaboration, making autonomous decisions about\nwhen and how to integrate AI suggestions\nTechnology Acceptance Model (TAM) Integration\nFurthermore, Davis’s (1989) Technology Acceptance Model provided the theoretical framework (See Appendix 5) for\nunderstanding student acceptance and utilization of AI tools within active learning contexts. The study investigated four\nkey TAM constructs within the context of cognitive partnerships:\n• Perceived Usefulness (PU): The extent to which students believed AI tools enhanced their writing performance\nand learning outcomes\n• Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU): The degree to which students found AI collaboration effortless and intuitive\n• Attitude Toward Use (ATU): Students’ overall evaluative responses to AI-mediated active learning\nexperiences\n• Behavioral Intention to Use (BITU): Students’ commitment to continued AI collaboration in future academic\ncontexts\n3.3 Participants and sampling strategy\nThe study employed convenience sampling to recruit 148 undergraduate students from Bahauddin Zakariya University,\nMultan, Pakistan. Discussion\n5.1 AI-mediated active learning: Theoretical validation and performance enhancement\nThe substantial improvement in the experimental group (Z = -6.325, p < .001) indicates that AI tools facilitated the\nexperiential learning cycle proposed by Kolb (1984) in EFL/ESL writing. Although the quasi-experimental design\nrestricts causal inference, baseline equivalence, progressive patterns, interaction effects, and qualitative triangulation can\n\n\nbe used to attribute differences to the pedagogical approach. The progressive improvement, which is observed since Test\n5 (p = .001) is consistent with what Kolb proposed that learning will only intensify under repetition of experience,\nreflection, conceptualization, and experimentation. This builds the meta-analysis by Freeman et al. (2014) that has found\nthe benefits of active learning in all disciplines, verifying its concepts in AI-based EFL settings. The 6-8 weeks\ndevelopment time implies that cognitive partnerships are to be maintained over time, which is consistent with recursive\nmodels of learning. The large effect sizes (r = 0.68 overall; r = 0.72 at Test 5) and Bonferroni-adjusted significance will\nremove any doubts about Type I error inflation and trivial effects. The less impressive results of the control group\n(Z = -2.128, p = 0.033) support the statement of Prince (2004) that passive instruction is not as effective. Importantly, this\npaper shows that AI can be used as a facilitator to active learning in the resource-scarce situations where a conventional\nimplementation is restricted. It is supported by the interview evidence: The significance of Test 5-6 is consistent with the\nqualitative reports of partnership maturation and advanced AI evaluation strategies. Qualitative results describe the\ntransformation of the attitude toward AI as an instrument to the attitude toward it as a partner that justifies the model of\ncognitive partnership by Luckin (2017). This is opposed to Chang et al. (2021) and Fitria (2021), in which AI was viewed\nas feedback. Ethical reflections and cycles seem to have made the application of AI a true partnership. Student comments\nsuch as I learned to synthesize its recommendations with my own thoughts make evidence of the stage of abstract\nconceptualization in Kolb. In contrast to the case reported by Yan (2023), who raised dependent issues, the ethical\nprocedure in this case reduced risks at the expense of AI, maintaining its cognitive advantages.\nAlthough the quantitative evidence proves beneficial performance, to learn processes that AI facilitated active learning, it\nis essential to consider the metacognitive processes disclosed in qualitative data.\n5.2 Active learning processes: Evidence and theoretical alignment\nMetacognitive awareness, reflected in comments like “I started to notice when I was depending too much on AI,”\nvalidates Bonwell and Eison’s (1991) assertion that active learning requires reflection. This distinguishes the study from\nprior AI writing research. Liu et al. (2021) also noted metacognitive improvements but lacked the systematic ethical\nframework used here. Structured prompts ensured AI use remained cognitively demanding, aligning with Prince’s (2004)\ndefinition of meaningful learning. AI tools enhanced rather than inhibited collaboration. Students reported increased peer\ninteraction through shared AI engagement and reflection tasks, extending Johnson and Johnson’s (2009) collaborative\nlearning theory into AI-mediated contexts. This contrasts with Anderson and Rainie’s (2023) concerns about AI reducing\ncollaboration, as the partnership model positioned AI as a facilitator rather than a replacement.\n5.3 Gender-differentiated engagement: Theoretical implications\nFemale students showed 19% higher engagement and greater improvement (p < .001) than males (p = .010) in\nAI-mediated active learning. In contrast, in the control group, males improved significantly (p < .001) while females’\ngains were insignificant (p = .062). This reversal suggests AI-mediated environments may align better with learning\npreferences associated with reflective and collaborative engagement.\nQualitative data point to stronger responsiveness among females to reflection and metacognition in Kolb’s cycle. This\nextends Al Mahmud’s (2023) findings by showing that pedagogical framing mediates gendered outcomes. These results\nsupport Vygotsky’s (1978) view that scaffolding must match learner characteristics. While both genders benefited, the\nframework appears particularly effective for those favoring reflective collaboration. The significant gains for males\n(p = .010) suggest the approach benefits all learners while amplifying certain preferences.\n5.4 Resource constraint mitigation: Theoretical and practical significance\nThe AI tools were used to overcome the issue of high enrollment in EFL classes, which supported the idea of AI being an\nequity tool mentioned by Bliksten (2016). Access to individualized instruction was democratized through reports of\ngetting personalized feedback whenever they needed it through cognitive partnerships. This expands Popenici and Kerr\n(2017) and Renz et al. (2020) and offers solid empirical support that AI could be used to address the obstacles to active\nlearning. The model overcame the conflict between large classes and individual feedback, which is a barrier to active\npedagogy traditionally. The findings can substantiate the perspective of Godwin-Jones (2019), according to which\ntechnology can facilitate pedagogy but cannot substitute it, as it allows active learning to take place under limitations.\nAccording to critics, technology is not the solution to pedagogical problems, but this paper demonstrates that even in\nchallenging situations, AI that is incorporated into grounded structures can support student-centered practices.\n5.5 Technology acceptance and active learning integration\nThe Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is confirmed by high-perceived usefulness (79.7%) and intention to continue\nuse (86%). Notably, by engaging in pedagogical integration, but not AI as an isolated tool, acceptance was enhanced.\nSuch responses by students as they make me think better than think less are critical evaluations and not passive adoptions\n\n\nand represent the issue that Davis (1989) discusses about uncritical acceptance. The protocol (See Appendix 7) served as a\nnon-compliance governance framework. The alignment of EU AI Act (transparency through disclosure), (European\nCommission, 2024), UNESCO principles (critical literacy) (UNESCO, 2023) and Safe AI standards (dependency\nprevention) the international frameworks were converted into the classroom practice. Mitigation Risk register mitigation\nin the case of dishonesty, dependency, bias can be seen as a demonstration of mitigation in a scalable way. Ethics\nawareness (M = 3.4/4.0) is high, and 91 per cent disclosure confirms effectiveness.\nAll these ethical, pedagogical, and practical aspects are important to inform larger theoretical synthesis of AI-mediated\nactive learning.\n5.6 Theoretical synthesis: Active learning–GenAI synergy framework\nFindings validate the Active Learning–GenAI Synergy Framework, showing AI can function as cognitive partners,\nmetacognitive mirrors, and equity tools. This extends theories of AI in education by embedding technology within active\nlearning, rather than as separate interventions. The achievement of the framework concerning the propensity to write and\nthe acquisition of digital literacies endorses the vision of cognitive partnerships proposed by Holmes et al. (2019) as a\ntangible illustration of resource-limited situations. It was established by carrying out an implementation using ethical\nprocedures and experiential learning processes. AI reduced cognitive load while enhancing higher-order thinking,\nchallenging assumptions that assistance undermines cognition. This suggests AI-mediated learning can both reduce\nroutine burdens and increase complex engagement. The finding extends Gayed et al.’s (2022) work on hybrid systems,\nshowing cognitive partnerships simultaneously lighten basic demands while fostering deeper processes. The framework\nachieved this duality by structuring AI use around progressive experiential cycles.\n5.7 Implications for EFL writing pedagogy\nThe shift from product- to process-focused instruction validates Flower and Hayes’s (1981) theory while extending it into\nAI-mediated contexts, supporting Graham and Perin’s (2007) process approach, highlighting AI-enabled individualized\nscaffolding in large classes, sustaining agency and critical thought. Improvements in coherence, structure, and revision\ndirectly address Pakistani EFL/ESL challenges noted by Mahmood et al. (2020). Multimodal integration expanded\nacademic writing to include digital composition, offering practical strategies for contemporary literacy in constrained\nsettings.\n5.8 Unexpected findings and theoretical implications\nHigh engagement combined with critical awareness challenges dependency concerns, showing students can maintain an\nauthorial voice while using AI. This supports the concept of “critical dependence”, where learners leverage AI while\nretaining evaluation and agency. The observed 6–8 week maturation of cognitive partnerships highlights sustained\nengagement needs, countering assumptions of immediate technological benefit while validating Kolb’s recursive model.\nProgressive improvement suggests long-term integration is preferable to short-term adoption, offering guidance for\nsustainable AI pedagogy.\n5.9 Limitations and future directions\nThree major categories of limitation arise.\nMethodological: The quasi-experimental design in intact classes does not allow causal inference, although baseline\nequivalence was established and data triangulation was employed to strengthen internal validity.\nContextual: Higher education context peculiarities of Pakistan, specific AI tools and 15 weeks of interest limits the\ngeneralizability.\nMeasurement: Ethical awareness, which is prone to social desirability, is not tested through long-term retention.\nThe future research needs to focus on the following aspects, including randomized controlled trials, cross-cultural\nvalidation, longitudinal tracking, and systematic comparison of AI tools and pedagogical frameworks.\n6. Conclusion\nThis preliminary study offers some initial evidence that resource-constrained contexts may see the use of ethically\nscaffolded AI tools to improve active learning in EFL/ESL writing. The results that were measured were: better writing\nperformance (experimental r = 0.68 versus control r = 0.23), development of ethical awareness (M = 1.8 ! 3.4), and high\nlevels of technology acceptance (86% intention to continue use). The confounds at the programme level (intact classes\nacross various degree programs) and the specific nature of the context (Pakistani business students, ChatGPT. Claude and\n\n\nMeta AI, 15-week intervention) restrict the transferability and causal inference. Most importantly, the study confirms that\nthe educational outcomes depend on the pedagogical structure that is used to mediate the interaction with AI and not on\nthe technology itself. This result has far-reaching implications on AI integration tactics, indicating that funding on\npedagogical growth and business ethics is critical in achieving AI in education without undermining the student agency\nand critical reasoning necessary to achieve learning success in the 21 st century.\nMost significantly, the research establishes that the pedagogical framework mediating AI interaction—rather than the\ntechnology itself—determines educational outcomes. This finding has profound implications for AI integration strategies, suggesting that investment in pedagogical development and ethical frameworks is crucial for realizing AI’s\neducational potential while maintaining the student agency and critical thinking essential for 21st-century learning\nsuccess.\nAI usage statement\nArtificial Intelligence (AI) tools were used to support the preparation of this manuscript in limited ways.
