Furthermore, we used topic modelling to analyse\npatent activities related to weak/strong technology signals\nand compute regional topic weights.\n\nIntroduction\n\nThe European Union (EU) has introduced the Smart\nSpecialization Strategy (S3) to promote sustainable and\ninclusive economic growth in its regions by inducing them to\ndiscover and develop economic areas in which they can have\ncomparative and competitive advantage. S3 is a place-based and\nbottom-up innovation policy which allows the EU regions to tap\ninto their endogenous potential (Coffano & Foray, 2014; Foray\net al., 2009). As knowledge and competencies are dispersed and\ndivided locally, “entrepreneurs in the broadest sense (innovative\nfirms, research leaders in higher education institutions,\nindependent inventors and innovators) are in the best position to\ndiscover the domains of R&D and innovation in which a region\nis likely to excel given its existing capabilities and productive\nassets” (Foray & Goenaga, 2013). For this reason, S3 relies\non an entrepreneurial discovery process (EDP) to map\npromising areas for investment and specialization (Coffano &\nForay, 2014). The role of regional governments in S3 is to\nidentify potential entrepreneurial discovery projects and to develop\ncritical mass in these strategic priority areas in order to facilitate\nmicro-level discovery and experimentation processes (Foray\net al., 2011), but policymakers lack appropriate tools and\nmethods to identify and assess promising EDPs.\nPrevious research studies the distribution of knowledge claims\nby regional patents and explores co-occurrence of technology\nclasses to identify industry diversification and specialization\nopportunities across EU regions (Balland et al., 2019; Montresor\n& Quatraro, 2020). Another strand of literature utilizes an unsupervised text mining approach to explore latent topics and to map\ninnovation ecosystems based on start-up activities and scientific publications (Bzhalava et al., 2018; Moilanen et al., 2021).\nAlthough the prior research provides valuable insights for\n\nunderstanding specialization patterns across regions, we lack\nan understanding of how to identify and assess EDPs based\non technology weak signals. Weak signals are defined “as\nseemingly random or disconnected pieces of information that\nat first appear to be background noise, but which can be recognized as part of a larger pattern when viewed through a different frame or by connecting it with other pieces of information”\n(Schoemaker et al., 2013). In other words, they are the early\nsigns for future disruptions and discontinuities and being able\nto monitor and analyse weak signals can help decision-makers\nsubstantially in predicting impeding technological and business changes. Hence, incorporating weak signal technology in\nstudying and evaluating entrepreneurial discovery processes\ncan help regional governments to map promising areas of future\nspecialization across territories and to design European S3\n(Paliokaitė et al., 2015; Paliokaitė et al., 2016).\n\nTheoretical framework\n\nBy adopting S3, EU aims to avoid duplication and fragmentation\nof its resources and to increase effectiveness of its research\nand innovation activities in the face of increased global\ncompetition (Foray et al., 2011; McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 2016).\nIn contrast to traditional industrial and innovation policy in\nwhich a decision-making process was mainly centralized and\ntop-down, S3 is a bottom-up collective reflection process\nin which local actors from industry and academia discover\ntechnology and market opportunities and identify promising\nareas for specialization (Coffano & Foray, 2014). As knowledge\nare fragmented and distributed among local actors and setting\ninnovation-policy priorities involve many uncertainties,\npolicymakers usually lack sufficient information to identify\nfuture growth opportunities. For this reason, traditional industrial\npolicy and its top-down approach in setting innovation priorities\noften failed to promote local economic development (Barca\net al., 2012; McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 2016). In particular,\n“the evidence from numerous development policy examples\nworldwide demonstrates that regions have made many mistakes\nin terms of their policy choices, and often this was because policies\nwere chosen on the basis of criteria which were not appropriate\nor relevant for the local context” (McCann & Ortega-Argilés,\n2016). As old industrial policy relies ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy\nsolutions and advocates the replication of successful innovation\npolicies applied in very different contexts, it was ineffective to\nstimulate endogenous economic potential across regions and\noften caused underdevelopment (Barca et al., 2012; McCann &\nOrtega-Argilés, 2016). Moreover, traditional innovation and\ndevelopment policies mainly focus on securing continuity of\ntechnology and industrial structure, and for this reason, it placed\ngreat emphasis on interests of large established firms in\nsetting innovation priorities (Asheim, 2019). As incumbent\nfirms mainly rely on the dominant design in their production\nsystem and concentrate on generating incremental innovations\nthrough exploiting mature technologies and existing knowledge\n(Lindholm-Dahlstrand et al., 2019), the traditional industrial\npolicy constrains local innovation activities (Asheim, 2019;\nIsaksen & Trippl, 2016). In contrast, S3 focuses on altering and\nrenewing of technology and industrial structures across territories\nand relies on EDP in identifying future growth opportunities\n\n\n(Foray, 2015). EDP is an inclusive process and involves a\nwide range of local agents from business and academia to\ndiscover and produce information about new activities of future\nspecialization, whereas ‘the regional government assesses the\nactivities’ potential and empowers those actors most capable of\nrealizing that potential’ (Virkkala & Mariussen, 2018). Hence,\nentrepreneurs (in the broadest sense) explore new market\nopportunities and bring into existence novel ideas and technologies as well as reshape markets and value networks across\nindustries (Lindholm-Dahlstrand et al., 2019; Schumpeter, 1934).\nThis process is termed as “creative destruction” in the classic\ninnovation literature (Schumpeter, 1934). In “creative destruction” process entrepreneurial firms bring new technologies to\nthe market and out-compete established companies by cannibalizing their market profit (which based on existing technologies)\nand making the value of their accumulated knowledge obsolete.\nThis, in turn, leads shifting profit pools from incumbents\nto new firms, as well as rearranging industry structures and\nreplacing established businesses (Lindholm-Dahlstrand et al.,\n2019). Hence, by relying on EDP, European S3 aims to implement structural economic changes across regions through identifying new domains of future opportunities in which they can\nhave competitive advantages and concentrating resources on\nthose key limited areas (Foray, 2015).\nTo examine regional knowledge bases and to identify technological upgrading (diversification) opportunities, previous\nresearch uses co-occurrence analysis and explores patent technology classes in regional patent databases (Balland et al., 2019;\nMontresor & Quatraro, 2020), as well as studies trademark business classifications to detect future growth opportunities across\nEuropean regions (Drivas, 2020). In contrast, Moilanen et al.\n(2021) employ an unsupervised text mining method to detect\nlatent topics and thematic networks in scientific literature. Other\nstrand of literature utilizes network analysis to examine startup\ncompany sub-industry tags and business descriptions to explore\nthe structure of entrepreneurial ecosystems and to examine where\nentrepreneurs drive their businesses across different sectors\nand territories and to detect specialization patterns (Basole\net al., 2018; Losurdo et al., 2019). Furthermore, Papagiannidis\net al. (2018) use an unsupervised text mining approach to\ndiscover latent topics in websites of firms and visually identify\nareas of intense business activities across various places. Similarly,\nother scholars explore web pages of firms to map innovation\necosystems (Beaudry et al., 2016; Kinne & Axenbeck, 2020).\nIn particular, as web pages of companies are often used to\npublish information about innovative products/services, previous\nresearch uses web and text mining methods to survey business\nwebsites and to detect innovation activities across territories\n(Beaudry et al., 2016; Kinne & Axenbeck, 2020).\nTo understand future technology and market landscape and to\nidentify promising EDPs, foresight can be significantly helpful\nas an instrument to study the potential ways in which the\nfuture technology and market landscape can unfold. Specifically,\nforesight is defined ‘as a process which involves systematic\ninquiry into longer-term futures, including emerging and novel\nissues, which in turn enables present decision-making and action’\n(Minkkinen et al., 2019). In the process of strategic planning\n\nwith foresight, a well-known approach is to identify and analyze\nweak signals which are the early signs of future disruptions\nand discontinuities (Thorleuchter & Van den Poel, 2015). To\nprocess massive amounts of information and to analyze\ntechnology/business related weak signals, prior studies propose\ntext mining methods (Kim & Lee, 2017; Thorleuchter & Van den\nPoel, 2013; Thorleuchter & Van den Poel, 2015; Yoon, 2012).\nIn particular, Yoon (2012) develop a keyword-based weak\nsignal detection approach to assess the strength of the terms for\neach topic by measuring keyword frequency (visibility) and the\ndegree of diffusion-based on document frequency, as well as\ncalculates time-weighted increasing rates of keywords based\non their degree of visibility/diffusion and computes average\nterm (document) frequency to identify weak and strong signal related terms. Similarly, Kim & Lee (2017) propose the\nnovelty-focused weak signal detection approach by first applying\ntext mining to extract signals from documents and then employing\na local outlier factor to study the rarity and paradigm\nunrelatedness of weak signals. Although prior research develops\nbig data and text mining tools to identify weak signals of impeding\ntechnological and business changes, we lack an understanding of how to incorporate technology foresight in the\ndesign and planning of European S3. To study this issue, the\nresearch aims to identify and assess EDPs (e.g. invention activities) with technology weak and strong signals. It is important\nto apply Big Data analytics to understand EDPs.\n\nMethods\n\nIn this research, we used patent claim data from the European\nPatent Office (EPO). EPO provides access all European Patent\n(EP) publications from 1978 until the end of January 2019. EP\npublications are in XML, PDF and TIFF formats and this makes\nit difficult to extract only publication text data. To address\nthis issue, EPO created EP full-text data for text analytics and\nprovides free and easy access to EP publication text data, which\nincludes information about the publication authority, number,\npublication kind, date, the language of text component, text type\n(i.e. title, abstract, description of the invention, a set of claims)\nas well as the publication abstract and the full-text description of the invention. As a patent abstract text includes essential information about an invention and its technical details\n(Lee et al., 2019), we used patent claim abstract data in our\nresearch and extracted only abstracts with English language from\nEP full-text data (for further details, see Underlying data). In\nthe study, we aim to identify invention activities associated with\nweak/strong signals of technological changes and to incorporate\ntechnology foresight in the design and planning of S3. For\nthis purpose, in line with previous research, we first collected\nweak/strong technology signals from patents abstracts during\nten years period from 2000 to 2009 (Kwon et al., 2018; Yoon,\n2012), and then we linked weak/strong technology signals\nwith invention activities during 2010-2018 time period.\nTo identify regional dimension of patent abstracts (for 2010\nto 2018) and to measure their quality, we combined EP data\nwith the OECD-REGPAT and OECD Patent Quality Indicators\ndatabases by using the patent publication number. The OECDREGPAT database January 2020 allows to connect patent data\nwith regional dimension by using the addresses of the applicants\n\n\nand inventors. The OECD Patent Quality Indicators database\nJuly 2020 provides information about indicators of patent\ntechnological and economic values (e.g. number of citations\na patent received up to five years after publication, and if a\npatent belongs to the top 1% highly cited patents up to five\nyears after publication).\n\nIdentifying weak signals with keyword-based text\nmining\nWe then cleaned and pre-processed patent abstracts, which\ninvolved transforming all textual content into lower case keywords\nand deleting numbers, non-English and special characters, as\nwell as removing punctuations and stop-words. Moreover,\nkeywords are lemmatized, which refers to applying vocabulary\nand morphological analysis and transforming keywords into\ntheir root forms. We then extracted only noun and adjective\nkeywords as they represent technology concepts and, in line\nwith Yoon (2012), we calculated their Degree of Visibility\n(DoV) based on the frequency of their occurrence and Degree of\nDiffusion (DoD) based on their appearance in number of\npatent abstracts. Mathematically, DoV and DoD are expressed\nin the following way:\n TFij \n × {1 − tw × (n − j )}\nDoVij = \n NNij \n\n\n\n(1)\n\n DFij \n × {1 − tw × (n − j )}\nDoDij = \n NNij \n\n\n\n(2)\n\nwhere TFij refers to the total occurrence frequency of a term\ni in period j, and DFij denotes to the document frequency of\nterm i in period j. NNj stands for the total number of patent\nabstracts in period j, whereas n is the number of periods.\nFurthermore, to put more weight on recent appearances of\nkeywords and to give recent occurrences of keywords more\nimportance than past occurrences, Yoon (2012) introduces tw (a\ntime-weight) which is defined as 0.05. Moreover, the geometric mean is calculated based on DoV and DoD values to explore\nthe increasing rates of keyword occurrences. Terms associated\nwith weak signal topics usually have a low absolute occurrence\nfrequency and a high time-weighted increasing rates. On the\ncontrary, terms related to strong signal topics are likely to have\na high absolute occurrence frequency and a high time-weighted\nincreasing rates. In line with Yoon (2012), we extract keywords\nthat are in the top 30% in terms of growth rate. Finally, we\nconsider keywords that have less than average absolute yearly\nterm (document) frequency as weak signals, and those with more\nthan average absolute yearly term (document) frequency as strong\nsignals.\nBy analyzing patent abstract data for the period 2000–2009,\nwe identified weak and strong signal keywords related to\nhospitality/travel, education, telecommunication, healthcare,\nmedia and entertainment, environment, transportation and\nconstruction industries. Specifically, the following weak signal\nkeywords were identified for the hospitality/travel industry:\ncooking, accommodation, wine, leisure. For the education\nindustry – education; for the telecommunication industry – cell\n\nphone; for the healthcare industry – care, health, vaccine,\nsymptom, anticancer. For the media and entertainment industry –\nadvertisement, media, gaming; for the environment technologies/industries – planet, biosensor, biomass, greenhouse,\nsustainability; for the transportation industry – motorcycle, flight;\nfor the construction industry – cement, construct. Afterwards,\nwe utilised the word2vec algorithm to group weak signal\nkeywords (Mikolov et al., 2013). In particular, we first kept\nonly weak signal keywords in patent abstracts and removed\nother words. We then utilised word2vec to train text corpus and\nextract terms that have high correlation (0.70–0.99 range) with\nthe industry keywords; the word2vec algorithm uses a neural\nnetwork approach to create word embeddings by learning word\nassociations (co-occurrence) from text and locating words\nwith similar meaning close to one another in the vector space\n(Mikolov et al., 2013). Strong signal keywords were also classified into telecommunication, healthcare, media and entertainment,\nenvironment and transportation sectors. For this purpose, we first cleaned and pre-processed\ntextual context of patent abstracts and then utilised Correlation Explanation (CorEx) topic modelling, which is a semisupervised text mining approach that allows the defining of\n“anchor words” and guides learning topics in the direction of\nthose anchor words (Gallagher et al., 2017). In other words,\nwe use weak and strong signal keywords as anchor words and\nuse CorEx topic modelling to identify related invention activities across EU regions for the period 2010-2018. In total, we\nidentify 366,392 patents associated with weak and strong\nsignals. Moreover, we apply latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)\nto discover hidden topics in those patent abstracts. LDA is an\nunsupervised machine learning method which automatically\ncreates cluster terms that have a higher possibility of showing\nup together and reveals latent topics in a collection of\ndocuments (Blei et al., 2003). To decide the number of topics to\nsearch in patent abstracts and to measure the quality of the\ntopics discovered, we tested a range of topic numbers from\n2 to 50 and calculated coherence scores (ibid). Finally, we\nselected topics with the highest coherence score and ran LDA\nwith 7 topics.\nFurthermore, we employed a one-way ANOVA modelling to\nstudy the relationship between weak/strong signals and patent values. A patent is considered as breakthrough if it receives\na disproportionately large number of forward citations and\nhas a considerably large impact on subsequent technological\nprogress (Arts & Veugelers, 2015; Squicciarini et al., 2013). In\nline of prior research, we measured a patent quality by (1) number\nof citations it receives up to five years after publication and\nalso (2) if it belongs to the top 1% highly cited patents up to\n\n\nfive years after publication. The empirical study\ndemonstrates how Big Data sets can be analysed in different\nEuropean regions. Specifically, we analysed patent claim\n\ndatabase and extracted weak/strong signals. We then utilised\nCorEx topic modelling to identify invention activities with weak\nand strong signals, as well as employ the ANOVA statistical\nmethod to examine whether patent values can be assessed by the\nweak and strong signals of technological development. In the\nfinal stage, an unsupervised text mining approach was used to\nmap European patent activities related to weak/strong technology signals and compute regional topic weights. The results\nreveal in which areas different EU regions contribute and also\nreflect R&D priorities. The proposed approach can be used to\nenvision future innovation ecosystems and study weak signal\nrelated invention activities across territories, as well as to calculate regional topic weights and develop policy road mapping\nfor innovation ecosystem development in the EU.\nIn addition, this study shows that patents related to weak\ntechnology signals rather than strong, are more likely to be\nhigh-impact innovations and to serve as a basis for future\ntechnological developments, implying that weak signals\nmatter for detecting breakthrough inventions. As the quantity\nof information is growing rapidly in today’s digital economy,\ndecision-makers often lack appropriate tools and methods to\nprocess massive amounts of external information, interpret\nsignals of impeding technological changes and use them in\nstrategic and innovation management.
