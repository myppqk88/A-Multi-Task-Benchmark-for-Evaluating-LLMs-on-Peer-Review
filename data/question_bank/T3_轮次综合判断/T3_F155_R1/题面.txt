      "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nResearch comparing inclusive education implementation in nonWestern contexts, such as Indonesia and Oman, remains limited. Both\ncountries share cultural similarities yet differ significantly in their\neducation governance. Understanding these differences is crucial for\nimproving inclusive education policies and practices.\nMethods\nThis study employed qualitative document analysis, reviewing 33\npolicy and implementation documents from Indonesia and Oman. The\nanalysis focused on five dimensions: service models, teacher training,\ncurriculum adaptation, community involvement, and cultural\nattitudes.\n\n\fResults and Conclusions\nFindings reveal that Oman’s centralized system provides consistent\npolicy coordination but limits community participation and school\nflexibility. Indonesia’s decentralized system allows greater local\ninnovation and community involvement but leads to disparities in\nimplementation quality. Both countries struggle with teacher training,\ncurriculum adaptation, and cultural stigma against disabilities.\nEffective inclusive education requires a balanced approach combining\nadaptive centralization and equitable decentralization, ongoing\nteacher development, flexible curricula, active community\nengagement, and cultural transformation. These insights offer\npractical recommendations to strengthen inclusive education in\ndiverse socio-political contexts.\n\n\nIntroduction\nInclusive education is a global agenda that has gained increasing attention since the proclamation of The Salamanca\nStatement and Framework for Action1,2 and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.3\nBoth documents emphasize the importance of an education system that can accommodate all learners without discrimination, regardless of their abilities, socio-cultural background, or disability status. Thus, inclusive education is not\nmerely an additional service, but a fundamental right of every child that must be fulfilled by the state.4\nAmong the countries committed to inclusive education are Indonesia and Oman. Indonesia, as a major country in\nSoutheast Asia, has a progressive regulatory framework for inclusive education. This is reflected in the Minister of\nNational Education Regulation No. 70 of 2009 concerning Inclusive Education, as well as its strengthening thru Law\nNo. 8 of 20165 concerning Persons with Disabilities. However, its implementation still faces challenges such as limited\nteacher competence, school unpreparedness, and social stigma toward children with special needs.6–9\nMeanwhile, Oman, a small country in the Middle East region with peaceful and simple social characteristics, presents a\ndifferent dynamic. This country adopted formal inclusive policies and international initiatives relatively early, including\nChild-Friendly Education (CFE) in collaboration with UNICEF.10 Additionally, Oman has a long-term development\ndirection thru Oman Vision 2040, which emphasizes fair, innovative, and inclusive education.11 From a cultural\nperspective, Oman and Indonesia both share Eastern values and moderate Islam, which serve as the foundation for\nbuilding social and educational harmony.12 This context makes Oman and Indonesia interesting to compare, especially\nsince both manage cultural diversity while also striving to respond to global demands for inclusive education.\nFurthermore, fundamental differences in educational governance add another important reason for conducting this\ncomparative study. Indonesia implements a decentralized system, where local governments have significant authority in\neducation administration, including inclusive education. Conversely, Oman adopts a more centralized system, with\neducation policies directly controlled by the central government. Although this was not the focus of the research, these\ndifferences in governance patterns have implications for variations in implementation in the field, both in terms of\nplanning, community support, and teacher readiness.\nPrevious research on inclusive education has generally focused on the experiences of a single country or even a single\nschool.13,14 Moreover, Cross-country studies, particularly those comparing Southeast Asia and the Middle East, are still\nlimited. Additionally, while many studies highlight regulatory or policy aspects, few have connected global frameworks\nlike Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) with real-world classroom\npractices. Therefore, according to the researchers, this condition is important to examine, as there are often significant\ndifferences between ambitious regulations and everyday learning practices.\nAdditionally, cross-national research on inclusive education has largely been conducted involving Western countries,\nsuch as the United States, Canada, or European nations.15–17 These studies do enrich the literature, but the cultural, social,\nand religious contexts of Western countries are vastly different from those of Indonesia or Oman. Therefore, a\ncomparative study between countries that share both Eastern culture and moderate Islam is important to enrich a more\ndiverse global perspective.18\nTherefore, this study aims to analyze the implementation of inclusive education in Oman and Indonesia using a\ncomparative approach. The focus is on uncovering the similarities and differences in the practice of inclusive education\nin the field. Hence, this research is expected not only to enrich the academic discourse on inclusive education but also to\nprovide practical contributions for policymakers, educational leaders, and practitioners.\nLiterature review\nThe implementation of inclusive education, which emphasizes that regular schools with an inclusive orientation are the\nmost effective way to combat discrimination, build a friendly society, and improve the quality of education for all\nchildren, has become a global consensus. This agreement is outlined in the document The Salamanca Statement.1 This\ndocument became the foundation for the inclusion movement worldwide and shifted the educational paradigm from\nsegregation toward diversity as a strength. Furthermore, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with\nDisabilities3 affirms that inclusive education is a human right for all children in the world. All countries that have ratified\nthis convention, including Oman and Indonesia, have a legal and moral obligation to ensure equal education for children\nwith special needs and eliminate the barriers they face. Thus, inclusion is not only understood as a policy choice, but as a\nglobal mandate that must be translated into national educational practices.\nIn line with this mandate, the theory and implementation framework for inclusion emphasize that every child has the\npotential to learn. Florian and Black-Hawkins,15 thru their theory of inclusive pedagogy, emphasize the importance of\n\n\nteacher flexibility in adapting learning strategies, rather than forcing students to conform to a rigid system. The Universal\nDesign for Learning (UDL) framework reinforces this principle by promoting curriculum flexibility thru diverse ways of\nrepresenting, motivating, and expressing learning outcomes.19 On a practical level, implementing inclusion requires an\nIndividualized Education Program (IEP) or Individualized Learning Program (PPI: Program pembelajaran individual),\nwhich provides a measurable learning plan tailored to each child’s needs. Moreover, Gibson and Blandford,20\nemphasized the important role of the Special Educational Needs Coordinator (SENCO) in the UK to ensure the IEP is\nimplemented, while in Indonesia this role is carried out by the Special Guidance Teacher.21 Thus, personalized learning\nbecomes the core of inclusion implementation so that every student can develop their maximum potential.\nIn addition, the implementation of inclusive education also requires clear and integrated service models. The service\nmodel emphasizes the importance of collaboration between school elements, experts (such as therapists or psychologists), and the community thru multidisciplinary support teams. On the other hand, Gibson and Blandford20 emphasize\nthat cross-sectoral coordination is key to assessing student needs, designing accommodations, and providing ongoing\nsupport. Additionally, participatory school management is essential for building an inclusive culture. Kozikoglu and\nUca22 emphasize the role of the principal in fostering collaboration among teachers, students, and parents to create a\nschool climate that is welcoming to diversity. Without strong team support and a solid service model, inclusion efforts are\nvulnerable to being partial and unsustainable.\nFurthemore, teacher readiness thru training is also a determining factor for the success of inclusion. Pre-service and\nin-service training programs must emphasize differentiated pedagogical strategies, inclusive classroom management, the\nuse of the UDL framework, and the development and implementation of IEPs. Research shows that teaching and training\nexperience influences the effectiveness of inclusion practices. Emam and Al-Mahdy14 found that in Oman, gender and\nteacher experience significantly influenced inclusive practices, while Phytanza et al.23 in Indonesia confirmed that\nlimited teacher training and the absence of standardized IEPs were the main obstacles. This means that without\ncontinuous training programs, inclusion policies will only remain at the formal level without real transformation in\nthe classroom.\nAnother crucial aspect is curriculum adaptation and instructional accommodations. The principle of curriculum\ndiversification allows for the presentation of varied material according to student needs, including language simplification, the provision of alternative materials, and the use of assistive technology. The Indonesian Ministry of Education\nand Culture24 emphasizes that curriculum adaptation and multi-level support from the government, schools, community,\nand parents are necessary for inclusive learning to be effective. If this is ignored, students with different needs are at risk of\nfalling behind academically and widening the education gap.\nAdditionally, community involvement, parental support, and support networks are crucial factors for the sustainability of\ninclusive education. Gibson and Blandford20 showed that parental and community participation is not merely an\nadditional support, but rather an integral part of the inclusive service system. Community support strengthens school\nresources and helps create a cohesive learning environment. However, cultural factors often pose an obstacle. Mukhlis6\nnotes that despite Indonesia having a clear legal framework, social stigma against disability remains strong. Meanwhile,\nin Oman, the centralized system facilitates policy coordination, but community participation is often minimal.25 This\nshows that both centralistic and decentralistic models face their own challenges in implementing inclusion.\nMoreover, the literature also confirms that failure to implement inclusion theories has serious consequences. Children\nwith special needs may face academic and social marginalization, while teachers are locked in uniform approaches that\ndisregard difference, and formal policies lose their practical usefulness. On the structural level, highly centralized\nadministration encourages low community participation, whereas a decentralized system has the potential to produce\ninter-regional inequities due to differences in teacher capacity and resources.26 Thus, the realization of inclusion\nnecessitates a balance between adaptive and participative centralization and uniform and equal decentralization.\nOverall, this literature review confirms that effective implementation of inclusive education requires a synergistic\ninteraction between global normative foundations, inclusive pedagogy theories, teacher training, personalization of\nlearning through IEPs/PPIs according to children’s needs, curriculum adaptation, community involvement, and cultural\nattitude transformation. Considering all these elements, an inclusive education system can move from mere policy\nformality to real practice that empowers all children to learn fairly, appropriately, and with dignity.\nMethod\nThis study employed document analysis as described by Bowen,27 which involved scanning, reading, and interpreting\npolicy documents and implementation reports on inclusive education, conducted from May 2025 to August 2025. The\n\n\ndocument sources included twenty-seven national regulations, technical guidelines, and field implementation reports.\nThe Indonesian documents were obtained through the Ministry of Education and Culture,24 while the Oman documents28–30 were obtained directly through the study’s co-author, a Professor and Director of the Faculty of Sharia at the\nOmani Ministry of Education, who is also a co-author of this article. This strengthened the validity of the sources by\nproviding access to official and up-to-date data on the implementation of inclusive education in Oman.\nThe 33 documents included policy documents, guidelines, and implementation documents, as well as peer-reviewed\nresearch manuscripts, which were reviewed for context and depth. The documents were then reviewed and analyzed\nacross five main dimensions: 1) Service Models, Support Teams, and School Management; 2) Teacher Training; 3)\nCurriculum Adaptation and Learning Accommodations; 4) Community, Parent, and Support Networks; and 5) Cultural\nAttitudes. Information from academic research, international frameworks, and national policy documents was compared\nto ensure triangulation. This process helped communities in Oman and Indonesia understand how inclusive education is\nimplemented effectively and equitably.\nData analysis was conducted by collecting and reviewing various policy documents and reports on the implementation of\ninclusive education from Indonesia and Oman through online searches using keywords: “policies and implementation of\nInclusive education in Indonesia and Oman” and documents provided by co-authors in Oman. After collecting the\ndocuments, the researchers conducted in-depth readings to understand their content and context, then interpreted their\nmeaning. The information obtained was then classified into five analytical dimensions: service models and support\nteams; teacher training; curriculum adaptation; community and parent involvement; and cultural attitudes. In the next\nstage, the researchers analyzed and synthesized the document content to identify patterns and differences, while also\nverifying the validity and credibility of the sources.\nResults and discussion\n1. Service model, support teams, and school management\nDocument analysis shows that both Oman and Indonesia emphasize a service model oriented toward the diverse needs of\nstudents. In Oman, this approach is more centrally coordinated thru the ministry. Official documents such as Special\nEducation Services in the Sultanate of Oman28 ensuring that every school has a formal framework for identifying student\nneeds and providing professional support. However, community participation and school flexibility are still limited due\nto the centralized system.31,32 Conversely, in Indonesia, the service model is more varied due to the influence of\neducational decentralization. Schools have the flexibility to form support teams consisting of regular teachers, special\nguidance teachers (GPK: Guru Pembimbing Khusus), school principals, and in some cases involving psychologists or\nexperts.33–35 However, there is still a wide variation in quality between schools due to limited resources and differences in\nschool management commitment.36,37\n2. Teacher training\nThe findings indicate that teacher training is a critical factor for successful inclusion. In Oman, teacher training is\nsystematically implemented thru a national program designed by the ministry, although research by Emam &\nAl-Mahdy14 shows there are still gaps in teachers’ experience and perceptions of inclusion, for example: Teachers with\nmoderate experience seem to be “lagging behind” in some dimensions of efficacy compared to new and very experienced\nteachers, particularly in the aspect of collaboration. Meanwhile, in Indonesia, the presence of GPK is an important\ninstrument, but training is still uneven and depends on local government and partner support.38,39 This leads to\ninconsistency in teachers’ competence in implementing inclusive instructional strategies.23\n3. Curriculum adaptation and instructional accommodation\nBoth Oman and Indonesia recognize the importance of curriculum adaptation as an integral part of inclusion. Oman’s\npolicy documents explicitly emphasize the alignment between the national curriculum and students’ individual needs, but\nlimitations in implementation at the classroom level persist due to the dominance of standardized approaches.40\nIndonesia, thru the Ministry of Education and Culture24 guidelines, encourages curriculum diversification and the\nimplementation of Individualized Learning Programs (PPI/IEP). However, in practice, there are still obstacles due to the\nlack of standard IEP guidelines and limited teacher training in developing and implementing PPIs. For example, there are\nno uniform technical guidelines for IEPs/PPIs, and teachers, including classroom teachers and GPKs (Special Needs\nAssistants), have not received sufficient training to develop PPIs that meet children’s needs.26,41,42 As a result, learning\naccommodations in the classroom are often inconsistent.43\n4. Community, parent, and support network\nInvolvement Community and parental participation are distinguishing aspects of both countries. In Oman, the centralized\neducation system makes coordination between government levels easier, but the space for public participation is\n\n\nrelatively limited.25,31 The government relies on official data, such as the Statistical Report on Persons with Disabilities in\nthe Sultanate of Oman,29 for strategic planning. Furthermore, the commitment to improving services is outlined in policy\ndocuments like Inclusive Education Services for Persons with Disabilities in Oman,30 which serves as a guide for multisectoral support. Conversely, in Indonesia, the existence of a culture of mutual cooperation and the role of local\ncommunities provides greater opportunities for community participation.33,34 However, the level of engagement still\nvaries greatly between regions, depending on school initiatives and local government support. Parental involvement in\nIndonesia is also still hindered by low levels of inclusive education literacy, often remaining purely administrative.39\n5. Cultural attitudes and stigma\nCultural attitudes and stigma remain a serious challenge to the implementation of inclusive education. In Indonesia,\nresearch by Mukhlis6 shows that although the legal framework for inclusion is clear, social stigma against people with\ndisabilities is still strong, both among students and parents. This puts children with special needs at risk of discrimination\nand marginalization in regular schools. In Oman, although society is known to be moderate,12,31 public participation in\nsupporting inclusion remains low because the system’s orientation places more emphasis on top-down policies. As a\nresult, public awareness of the importance of inclusive education has not yet fully taken root at the community level.14\nTable 1 below shows a comparative overview of inclusive education implementation in Indonesia and Oman.\nAs shown in Table 1, there are significant differences in the implementation of inclusive education between Indonesia and\nOman, especially regarding governance, community participation, and teacher training.\nBased on Table 1, the research findings indicate that both Indonesia and Oman face challenges in implementing inclusive\neducation, although within different governance contexts. The difference between centralized and decentralized models\naffects service patterns, community involvement, and implementation consistency.\nIn Indonesia, decentralization enables innovation at the school level and opens up space for community participation.\nHowever, this condition creates disparities between regions. Some schools excel at building multidisciplinary support\nteams, while others still lack resources. This finding aligns with Phytanza et al.,23 who confirmed that the absence of IEP\nstandards leads to highly variable quality of implementation.\n\nTable 1. This confirms the urgency of implementing the UDL\nframework19 and IEP to bridge regulations and classroom practices.\nCultural factors present fundamental challenges. Indonesia still faces stereotypes and social discrimination against\nindividuals with disabilities.6 Meanwhile, in Oman, public awareness remains low due to a policy approach that is too\ngovernment-centric. Both conditions indicate that cultural transformation is a prerequisite for the sustainability of\ninclusion. Without a change in public attitude, formal policies will continue to face resistance in practice.\nOverall, the findings of this study demonstrate that the implementation of inclusive education in Oman and Indonesia is\nshaped by contrasting governance models, each with its advantages and constraints. Oman’s centralized system enables\nstrong coordination and consistency, yet it restricts opportunities for broad public participation. Indonesia’s decentralized\nmodel, on the other hand, provides more space for community involvement but struggles with uneven quality and\ndisparities across regions. This comparative perspective underscores that inclusive education cannot be secured through\ngovernance design alone; it requires an integrated effort that combines robust national policies, sustained teacher training,\nflexible curriculum adaptation, multidisciplinary team support, and a cultural transformation that normalizes inclusivity\nwithin society. Building on this insight, the study advances a novel proposition: a hybrid model that blends adaptive\ncentralization with equitable decentralization, offering a balanced strategy to ensure inclusive education operates\neffectively and sustainably across diverse contexts.\nBased on the discussion above, the strength of this study lies in weaving global frameworks such as UDL and IEP into a\nrarely studied cross-country comparative analysis, revealing how contrasting governance logics—centralization in Oman\nand decentralization in Indonesia—shape the way international commitments are translated into everyday learning\npractices. Beyond advancing scholarly debate, this research offers practical value by offering concrete recommendations\nto policymakers, educational leaders, and practitioners seeking to embed inclusive principles more deeply into their\nsystems. The recommendations presented here aim to guide both countries in strengthening inclusive practices while also\ncontributing to the broader global effort to ensure that education is recognized and realized as a right for every child in\nevery context.\nConclusion\nThe results of this study indicate that the implementation of inclusive education in Indonesia and Oman operates with\ndifferent dynamics depending on the adopted education governance system. Indonesia, with its decentralization model,\nprovides room for flexibility and innovation at the school level, as well as significant opportunities for community\nparticipation. However, this condition also creates disparities between regions due to differences in teacher capacity,\nresources, and school management commitment. Conversely, Oman, which implements a centralized system, is able to\nensure policy consistency and stronger national coordination, but community involvement and school flexibility are\nlimited. Both countries still face similar challenges, including limited teacher training, weak curriculum adaptation, and\nstrong cultural stigma against disabilities. Overall, this study confirms that effective inclusive education can only be\nachieved thru a combination of clear national policies, ongoing teacher training, a flexible UDL and IEP-based\ncurriculum, active community involvement, and a more inclusive cultural transformation.\nRecommendations\nBased on these findings, this study recommends that governments in both countries strengthen regulations by developing\nnational IEP or PPI standards that can be adapted to the local context, while also ensuring adequate allocation of resources\nand infrastructure for inclusive schools. At the school level, teachers need to receive continuous capacity development\nwith a focus on differentiated learning strategies, IEP development, and the implementation of the UDL framework.\nCollaboration in the form of multidisciplinary support teams involving regular teachers, special education teachers,\n\n\nprofessionals, and parents must be continuously improved. Additionally, the community and parents also need to be\nprovided with inclusive education literacy so that their role doesn’t stop at the administrative aspect, but extends to active\nparticipation in the learning and decision-making process. With this strategy, both countries are expected to strengthen\nthe integration of the principle of inclusion so that education truly becomes a right accessible to all children.\nLimitations & Further direction\nThis study has a number of limitations. First, the analysis is based solely on policy documents, implementation reports,\nand academic literature, so it does not fully capture the reality of practices on the ground. Second, data from Oman is\nlargely sourced from official government documents, so the potential for top-down bias remains. Third, this research\nfocuses on five main dimensions: service model, teacher training, curriculum adaptation, community involvement, and\ncultural attitudes, so other factors such as the role of digital technology in supporting inclusion or the direct perspectives\nof students have not been revealed. Fourth, the generalization of research findings is also limited because the social and\ncultural contexts of Indonesia and Oman have unique characteristics that may not be fully comparable to other countries.\nTherefore, further research needs to involve more in-depth field data, obtained thru interviews, observations, and case\nstudies, in order to provide a more comprehensive picture of inclusion implementation.\nData availability\nUnderlying data\nNo data are associated with this article.       "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nResearch comparing inclusive education implementation in nonWestern contexts, such as Indonesia and Oman, remains limited. Both\ncountries share cultural similarities yet differ significantly in their\neducation governance. Understanding these differences is crucial for\nimproving inclusive education policies and practices.\nMethods\nThis study employed qualitative document analysis, reviewing 33\npolicy and implementation documents from Indonesia and Oman. The\nanalysis focused on five dimensions: service models, teacher training,\ncurriculum adaptation, community involvement, and cultural\nattitudes.\n\n\fResults and Conclusions\nFindings reveal that Oman’s centralized system provides consistent\npolicy coordination but limits community participation and school\nflexibility. Indonesia’s decentralized system allows greater local\ninnovation and community involvement but leads to disparities in\nimplementation quality. Both countries struggle with teacher training,\ncurriculum adaptation, and cultural stigma against disabilities.\nEffective inclusive education requires a balanced approach combining\nadaptive centralization and equitable decentralization, ongoing\nteacher development, flexible curricula, active community\nengagement, and cultural transformation. These insights offer\npractical recommendations to strengthen inclusive education in\ndiverse socio-political contexts.\n\n\nIntroduction\nInclusive education is a global agenda that has gained increasing attention since the proclamation of The Salamanca\nStatement and Framework for Action1,2 and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.3\nBoth documents emphasize the importance of an education system that can accommodate all learners without discrimination, regardless of their abilities, socio-cultural background, or disability status. Thus, inclusive education is not\nmerely an additional service, but a fundamental right of every child that must be fulfilled by the state.4\nAmong the countries committed to inclusive education are Indonesia and Oman. Indonesia, as a major country in\nSoutheast Asia, has a progressive regulatory framework for inclusive education. This is reflected in the Minister of\nNational Education Regulation No. 70 of 2009 concerning Inclusive Education, as well as its strengthening thru Law\nNo. 8 of 20165 concerning Persons with Disabilities. However, its implementation still faces challenges such as limited\nteacher competence, school unpreparedness, and social stigma toward children with special needs.6–9\nMeanwhile, Oman, a small country in the Middle East region with peaceful and simple social characteristics, presents a\ndifferent dynamic. This country adopted formal inclusive policies and international initiatives relatively early, including\nChild-Friendly Education (CFE) in collaboration with UNICEF.10 Additionally, Oman has a long-term development\ndirection thru Oman Vision 2040, which emphasizes fair, innovative, and inclusive education.11 From a cultural\nperspective, Oman and Indonesia both share Eastern values and moderate Islam, which serve as the foundation for\nbuilding social and educational harmony.12 This context makes Oman and Indonesia interesting to compare, especially\nsince both manage cultural diversity while also striving to respond to global demands for inclusive education.\nFurthermore, fundamental differences in educational governance add another important reason for conducting this\ncomparative study. Indonesia implements a decentralized system, where local governments have significant authority in\neducation administration, including inclusive education. Conversely, Oman adopts a more centralized system, with\neducation policies directly controlled by the central government. Although this was not the focus of the research, these\ndifferences in governance patterns have implications for variations in implementation in the field, both in terms of\nplanning, community support, and teacher readiness.\nPrevious research on inclusive education has generally focused on the experiences of a single country or even a single\nschool.13,14 Moreover, Cross-country studies, particularly those comparing Southeast Asia and the Middle East, are still\nlimited. Additionally, while many studies highlight regulatory or policy aspects, few have connected global frameworks\nlike Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) with real-world classroom\npractices. Therefore, according to the researchers, this condition is important to examine, as there are often significant\ndifferences between ambitious regulations and everyday learning practices.\nAdditionally, cross-national research on inclusive education has largely been conducted involving Western countries,\nsuch as the United States, Canada, or European nations.15–17 These studies do enrich the literature, but the cultural, social,\nand religious contexts of Western countries are vastly different from those of Indonesia or Oman. Therefore, a\ncomparative study between countries that share both Eastern culture and moderate Islam is important to enrich a more\ndiverse global perspective.18\nThus, the above discussion demonstrates that although inclusive education research has progressed substantially, critical\ngaps remain in comparative understanding across non-Western contexts. In particular, limited attention has been paid to\nhow global inclusive education frameworks, such as Universal Learning Design (UDL) and Individualized Education\nPrograms (IEPs), are implemented in practice across different governance and cultural settings. To address this gap, this\nstudy uses a qualitative comparative approach to examine the implementation of inclusive education in Oman and\n\n\nIndonesia, focusing on similarities and differences in practice. This research aims to contribute to a more contextualized\nglobal discourse on inclusive education while offering practical insights for policymakers, educational leaders, and\npractitioners.\nLiterature review\nThe implementation of inclusive education, which emphasizes that regular schools with an inclusive orientation are the\nmost effective way to combat discrimination, build a friendly society, and improve the quality of education for all\nchildren, has become a global consensus. This agreement is outlined in the document The Salamanca Statement.1 This\ndocument became the foundation for the inclusion movement worldwide and shifted the educational paradigm from\nsegregation toward diversity as a strength. Furthermore, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with\nDisabilities3 affirms that inclusive education is a human right for all children in the world. All countries that have ratified\nthis convention, including Oman and Indonesia, have a legal and moral obligation to ensure equal education for children\nwith special needs and eliminate the barriers they face. Thus, inclusion is not only understood as a policy choice, but as a\nglobal mandate that must be translated into national educational practices.\nIn line with this mandate, the theory and implementation framework for inclusion emphasize that every child has the\npotential to learn. Florian and Black-Hawkins,15 thru their theory of inclusive pedagogy, emphasize the importance of\nteacher flexibility in adapting learning strategies, rather than forcing students to conform to a rigid system. The Universal\nDesign for Learning (UDL) framework reinforces this principle by promoting curriculum flexibility thru diverse ways of\nrepresenting, motivating, and expressing learning outcomes.19 On a practical level, implementing inclusion requires an\nIndividualized Education Program (IEP) or Individualized Learning Program (PPI: Program pembelajaran individual),\nwhich provides a measurable learning plan tailored to each child’s needs. Moreover, Gibson and Blandford,20\nemphasized the important role of the Special Educational Needs Coordinator (SENCO) in the UK to ensure the IEP is\nimplemented, while in Indonesia this role is carried out by the Special Guidance Teacher.21 Thus, personalized learning\nbecomes the core of inclusion implementation so that every student can develop their maximum potential.\nIn addition, the implementation of inclusive education also requires clear and integrated service models. The service\nmodel emphasizes the importance of collaboration between school elements, experts (such as therapists or psychologists), and the community thru multidisciplinary support teams. On the other hand, Gibson and Blandford20 emphasize\nthat cross-sectoral coordination is key to assessing student needs, designing accommodations, and providing ongoing\nsupport. Additionally, participatory school management is essential for building an inclusive culture. Kozikoglu and\nUca22 emphasize the role of the principal in fostering collaboration among teachers, students, and parents to create a\nschool climate that is welcoming to diversity. Without strong team support and a solid service model, inclusion efforts are\nvulnerable to being partial and unsustainable.\nFurthemore, teacher readiness thru training is also a determining factor for the success of inclusion. Pre-service and\nin-service training programs must emphasize differentiated pedagogical strategies, inclusive classroom management, the\nuse of the UDL framework, and the development and implementation of IEPs. Research shows that teaching and training\nexperience influences the effectiveness of inclusion practices. Emam and Al-Mahdy14 found that in Oman, gender and\nteacher experience significantly influenced inclusive practices, while Phytanza et al.23 in Indonesia confirmed that\nlimited teacher training and the absence of standardized IEPs were the main obstacles. This means that without\ncontinuous training programs, inclusion policies will only remain at the formal level without real transformation in\nthe classroom.\nAnother crucial aspect is curriculum adaptation and instructional accommodations. The principle of curriculum\ndiversification allows for the presentation of varied material according to student needs, including language simplification, the provision of alternative materials, and the use of assistive technology. The Indonesian Ministry of Education\nand Culture24 emphasizes that curriculum adaptation and multi-level support from the government, schools, community,\nand parents are necessary for inclusive learning to be effective. If this is ignored, students with different needs are at risk of\nfalling behind academically and widening the education gap.\nAdditionally, community involvement, parental support, and support networks are crucial factors for the sustainability of\ninclusive education. Gibson and Blandford20 showed that parental and community participation is not merely an\nadditional support, but rather an integral part of the inclusive service system. Community support strengthens school\nresources and helps create a cohesive learning environment. However, cultural factors often pose an obstacle. Mukhlis6\nnotes that despite Indonesia having a clear legal framework, social stigma against disability remains strong. Meanwhile,\nin Oman, the centralized system facilitates policy coordination, but community participation is often minimal.25 This\nshows that both centralistic and decentralistic models face their own challenges in implementing inclusion.\n\n\nMoreover, the literature also confirms that failure to implement inclusion theories has serious consequences. Children\nwith special needs may face academic and social marginalization, while teachers are locked in uniform approaches that\ndisregard difference, and formal policies lose their practical usefulness. On the structural level, highly centralized\nadministration encourages low community participation, whereas a decentralized system has the potential to produce\ninter-regional inequities due to differences in teacher capacity and resources.26 Thus, the realization of inclusion\nnecessitates a balance between adaptive and participative centralization and uniform and equal decentralization.\nOverall, this literature review confirms that effective implementation of inclusive education requires a synergistic\ninteraction between global normative foundations, inclusive pedagogy theories, teacher training, personalization of\nlearning through IEPs/PPIs according to children’s needs, curriculum adaptation, community involvement, and cultural\nattitude transformation. Considering all these elements, an inclusive education system can move from mere policy\nformality to real practice that empowers all children to learn fairly, appropriately, and with dignity.\nMethod\nThis study employed document analysis as described by Bowen,27 which involved scanning, reading, and interpreting\npolicy documents and implementation reports on inclusive education, conducted from May 2025 to August 2025.\nThe documents were selected based on explicit inclusion criteria: (1) official national or ministerial regulations related to\ninclusive education; (2) technical guidelines and implementation reports issued by authorized institutions; and (3) peerreviewed academic publications relevant to inclusive education in Indonesia and Oman. Documents that were outdated,\nduplicated, or not directly related to inclusive education policy or implementation were excluded from the analysis.\nThe document sources included twenty-seven national regulations, technical guidelines, and field implementation\nreports. The Indonesian documents were obtained through the Ministry of Education and Culture,24 while the Oman\ndocuments28–30 were obtained directly through the study's co-author, a Professor and Director of the Faculty of Sharia at\nthe Omani Ministry of Education, who is also a co-author of this article. This direct institutional access strengthened\nsource credibility by ensuring the authenticity and currency of official policy and implementation data from Oman.\nThe 33 documents included policy documents, guidelines, and implementation documents, as well as peer-reviewed\nresearch manuscripts, which were reviewed for context and depth. The credibility of the sources was ensured by\nprioritizing documents published by government ministries, international organizations, and peer-reviewed journals.\nThese documents were first read repeatedly to gain an understanding of their content and contextual background.\nData analysis was conducted through several sequential stages. First, selected documents were examined through\nin-depth reading to identify relevant information related to inclusive education policies and practices. The researchers\ncollected and reviewed various policy documents and reports on the implementation of inclusive education from\nIndonesia and Oman through online searches using the keywords \"inclusive education policies and implementation in\nIndonesia and Oman\" and documents provided by co-authors in Oman. Second, the extracted data were further coded and\ncategorized according to five main dimensions: (1) service models, support teams, and school management; (2) teacher\ntraining; (3) curriculum adaptations and instructional accommodations; (4) community, parents, and support networks;\nand (5) cultural attitudes. These dimensions are derived from inclusive education theories and global frameworks,\nincluding Universal Design for Learning (UDL), Inclusive Pedagogy, and IEP-based practices. Third, thematic\ncomparisons were conducted across documents to identify patterns, similarities, and differences between Indonesia\nand Oman across each analytical dimension. Finally, triangulation was applied by comparing information from policy\ndocuments, implementation reports, international frameworks, and academic literature to enhance analytical validity and\nreduce interpretive bias.\nResults and discussion\n1. Service model, support teams, and school management\nDocument analysis shows that both Oman and Indonesia emphasize a service model oriented toward the diverse needs of\nstudents. In Oman, this approach is more centrally coordinated thru the ministry. Official documents such as Special\nEducation Services in the Sultanate of Oman28 ensuring that every school has a formal framework for identifying student\nneeds and providing professional support. However, community participation and school flexibility are still limited due\nto the centralized system.31,32 Conversely, in Indonesia, the service model is more varied due to the influence of\neducational decentralization. Schools have the flexibility to form support teams consisting of regular teachers, special\nguidance teachers (GPK: Guru Pembimbing Khusus), school principals, and in some cases involving psychologists or\nexperts.33–35 However, there is still a wide variation in quality between schools due to limited resources and differences in\nschool management commitment.36,37\n\n\n\n2. Teacher training\nThe findings indicate that teacher training is a critical factor for successful inclusion. In Oman, teacher training is\nsystematically implemented thru a national program designed by the ministry, although research by Emam &\nAl-Mahdy14 shows there are still gaps in teachers’ experience and perceptions of inclusion, for example: Teachers with\nmoderate experience seem to be “lagging behind” in some dimensions of efficacy compared to new and very experienced\nteachers, particularly in the aspect of collaboration. Meanwhile, in Indonesia, the presence of GPK is an important\ninstrument, but training is still uneven and depends on local government and partner support.38,39 This leads to\ninconsistency in teachers’ competence in implementing inclusive instructional strategies.23\n3. Curriculum adaptation and instructional accommodation\nBoth Oman and Indonesia recognize the importance of curriculum adaptation as an integral part of inclusion. Oman’s\npolicy documents explicitly emphasize the alignment between the national curriculum and students’ individual needs, but\nlimitations in implementation at the classroom level persist due to the dominance of standardized approaches.40\nIndonesia, thru the Ministry of Education and Culture24 guidelines, encourages curriculum diversification and the\nimplementation of Individualized Learning Programs (PPI/IEP). However, in practice, there are still obstacles due to the\nlack of standard IEP guidelines and limited teacher training in developing and implementing PPIs. For example, there are\nno uniform technical guidelines for IEPs/PPIs, and teachers, including classroom teachers and GPKs (Special Needs\nAssistants), have not received sufficient training to develop PPIs that meet children’s needs.26,41,42 As a result, learning\naccommodations in the classroom are often inconsistent.43\n4. Community, parent, and support network\nInvolvement Community and parental participation are distinguishing aspects of both countries. In Oman, the centralized\neducation system makes coordination between government levels easier, but the space for public participation is\nrelatively limited.25,31 The government relies on official data, such as the Statistical Report on Persons with Disabilities\nin the Sultanate of Oman,29 for strategic planning. Furthermore, the commitment to improving services is outlined in\npolicy documents like Inclusive Education Services for Persons with Disabilities in Oman,30 which serves as a guide for\nmulti-sectoral support. Conversely, in Indonesia, the existence of a culture of mutual cooperation and the role of local\ncommunities provides greater opportunities for community participation.33,34 However, the level of engagement still\nvaries greatly between regions, depending on school initiatives and local government support. Parental involvement in\nIndonesia is also still hindered by low levels of inclusive education literacy, often remaining purely administrative.39\n5. This puts children with special needs at risk of discrimination\nand marginalization in regular schools. The difference between centralized and decentralized models\naffects service patterns, community involvement, and implementation consistency.\nIn Indonesia, decentralization enables innovation at the school level and opens up space for community participation.\nHowever, this condition creates disparities between regions. Some schools excel at building multidisciplinary support\nteams, while others still lack resources. Oman has a more established national\nsystem, but gaps in teacher experience are still evident.14 Indonesia has GPK as an important element, but the distribution\nand quality of training are uneven. Indonesia has already regulated this in the Ministry of Education and\nCulture24 guidelines, but in practice, it is often hampered by limitations in teacher competence. Oman also faces similar\nobstacles due to the dominance of the standard curriculum. This confirms the urgency of implementing the UDL\nframework19 and IEP to bridge regulations and classroom practices.\nCultural factors present fundamental challenges. Indonesia still faces stereotypes and social discrimination against\nindividuals with disabilities.6 Meanwhile, in Oman, public awareness remains low due to a policy approach that is too\ngovernment-centric. Both conditions indicate that cultural transformation is a prerequisite for the sustainability of\ninclusion. Without a change in public attitude, formal policies will continue to face resistance in practice.\nOverall, the findings of this study demonstrate that the implementation of inclusive education in Oman and Indonesia is\nshaped by contrasting governance models, each with its advantages and constraints. Oman’s centralized system enables\nstrong coordination and consistency, yet it restricts opportunities for broad public participation. Indonesia’s decentralized\nmodel, on the other hand, provides more space for community involvement but struggles with uneven quality and\ndisparities across regions. This comparative perspective underscores that inclusive education cannot be secured through\ngovernance design alone; it requires an integrated effort that combines robust national policies, sustained teacher training,\nflexible curriculum adaptation, multidisciplinary team support, and a cultural transformation that normalizes inclusivity\nwithin society. Building on this insight, the study advances a novel proposition: a hybrid model that blends adaptive\ncentralization with equitable decentralization, offering a balanced strategy to ensure inclusive education operates\neffectively and sustainably across diverse contexts.\n\n\n\nBased on the discussion above, the strength of this study lies in weaving global frameworks such as UDL and IEP into a\nrarely studied cross-country comparative analysis, revealing how contrasting governance logics—centralization in Oman\nand decentralization in Indonesia—shape the way international commitments are translated into everyday learning\npractices. Beyond advancing scholarly debate, this research offers practical value by offering concrete recommendations\nto policymakers, educational leaders, and practitioners seeking to embed inclusive principles more deeply into their\nsystems. The recommendations presented here aim to guide both countries in strengthening inclusive practices while also\ncontributing to the broader global effort to ensure that education is recognized and realized as a right for every child in\nevery context.\nConclusion\nThe results of this study indicate that the implementation of inclusive education in Indonesia and Oman operates with\ndifferent dynamics depending on the adopted education governance system. Indonesia, with its decentralization model,\nprovides room for flexibility and innovation at the school level, as well as significant opportunities for community\nparticipation. However, this condition also creates disparities between regions due to differences in teacher capacity,\nresources, and school management commitment. Conversely, Oman, which implements a centralized system, is able to\nensure policy consistency and stronger national coordination, but community involvement and school flexibility are\nlimited. Both countries still face similar challenges, including limited teacher training, weak curriculum adaptation, and\nstrong cultural stigma against disabilities. Overall, this study confirms that effective inclusive education can only be\nachieved thru a combination of clear national policies, ongoing teacher training, a flexible UDL and IEP-based\ncurriculum, active community involvement, and a more inclusive cultural transformation.\nRecommendations\nBased on these findings, this study recommends that governments in both countries strengthen regulations by developing\nnational IEP or PPI standards that can be adapted to the local context, while also ensuring adequate allocation of resources\nand infrastructure for inclusive schools. At the school level, teachers need to receive continuous capacity development\nwith a focus on differentiated learning strategies, IEP development, and the implementation of the UDL framework.\nCollaboration in the form of multidisciplinary support teams involving regular teachers, special education teachers,\nprofessionals, and parents must be continuously improved. Additionally, the community and parents also need to be\nprovided with inclusive education literacy so that their role doesn’t stop at the administrative aspect, but extends to active\nparticipation in the learning and decision-making process. With this strategy, both countries are expected to strengthen\nthe integration of the principle of inclusion so that education truly becomes a right accessible to all children.\nLimitations & Further direction\nThis study has a number of limitations. First, the analysis is based solely on policy documents, implementation reports,\nand academic literature, so it does not fully capture the reality of practices on the ground. Second, data from Oman is\nlargely sourced from official government documents, so the potential for top-down bias remains. Third, this research\nfocuses on five main dimensions: service model, teacher training, curriculum adaptation, community involvement, and\ncultural attitudes, so other factors such as the role of digital technology in supporting inclusion or the direct perspectives\nof students have not been revealed. Fourth, the generalization of research findings is also limited because the social and\ncultural contexts of Indonesia and Oman have unique characteristics that may not be fully comparable to other countries.\nTherefore, further research needs to involve more in-depth field data, obtained thru interviews, observations, and case\nstudies, in order to provide a more comprehensive picture of inclusion implementation.\nData availability\nUnderlying data\nNo data are associated with this article.
