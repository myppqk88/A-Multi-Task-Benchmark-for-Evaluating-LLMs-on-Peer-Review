Abstract
This paper examines the uneven geography of skill mismatches by
studying the regional allocation of highly educated workers in high,
medium or low skill-level occupations. To this end, a conceptual
framework is developed combining Geographical Political Economy
with theorizations around the optimal allocation of human capital. A
comparative empirical analysis is then conducted using the Location
Quotient Index for the period 2009-2021 for five countries (Greece,
Bulgaria, France, Germany and Denmark), where the universities
affiliated to the European Reform University Alliance (ERUA) are
located. The results of this analysis are then contextualized through
correlations with various macroeconomic, population density, and
demographic metrics, which represent factors underlying the uneven
geography of skill mismatches. The analysis stresses five critical
factors behind the regional and occupational allocation of skills:
economic dynamism at the national level, regional industrial
specializations, urbanisation levels, demographic structures, and
institutional endowments. Moreover, it is argued, these factors behind
human capital development tend to reproduce geographical
unevenness rather than ameliorating spatial inequalities. Based on
these observations, a set of policy actions is recommended based on
the five factors mentioned above and the EU’s New Skills Agenda. With
the above, the paper offers valuable insight into the negative
externalities associated with skills mismatches, taking into account the
different socio-economic models and school-to-work transition
systems across the EU. Moreover, it attempts to make a positive
contribution to the European Universities Initiative, as examining the
skills gaps in the regions associated with the ERUA strengthens the

Plain language summary
The economic downturn of the last fifteen years, beginning with the
2008/09 Global Crisis up to the currently unfolding energy crisis, have
shaped and accelerated the European Commission’s efforts towards
the digital and energy transitions (often referred to as twin
transitions), paying particular emphasis on workforce’s skills and
education. The EU Skills Agenda sets ambitious targets to ensure
access to education and lifelong learning in all regions of the Union.
Adopting a comprehensive approach which incorporates institutional,
evolutionary, and political economic influences, this paper examines
the uneven geography of skill mismatches by studying the regional
allocation of highly educated workers and whether they tend to be
employed in high, medium or low-skilled occupations.
To do so, a comparative empirical analysis is conducted using the
Location Quotient Index for the period 2009-2021 for five countries
(Greece, Bulgaria, France, Germany and Denmark) where the
universities affiliated to the European Reform University Alliance
(ERUA) are located. Specifically, the consortium implemented the
ReERUA Horizon Project (Grant agreement ID: 101035808), in the
frame of which the analysis at hand is conducted. The results of this
analysis are then correlated with various metrics that pertain to
factors that influence the spatial allocation of workforce skills.
The paper reveals five factors that are critical to the geographical and
functional allocation of skills: economic dynamism at the national
level, regional industrial specializations, urbanisation levels,
demographic structures, and institutional endowments. Finally, a set
of policy actions are recommended based on the five factors
mentioned above and the EU’s New Skills Agenda.

Introduction

During the past five decades, long processes of
de/re-industrialization brought the importance of workforce’s
skills to the forefront (Thrift, 2008). In the current unstable
conjuncture, which has been marked by successive crises,
including the 2008/09 Global Crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic,
and the subsequent energy crisis, governments and regulatory
bodies keep stressing the importance of new hard skills—often
associated with the digital and green transitions—as well as
soft skills related to problem-solving and adaptability to changing conditions (McGuinness et al., 2018). Indicatively, the
European Commission (EC) has published relevant agendas
(EC, 2016; EC, 2020), while it proclaimed 2023 as the
“European Year of Skills.” EC’s targets for up-/re-skilling
initiatives and its policy toolkits strongly resonate with the
basic tenets of Human Capital Theory (HCT from now on;
Becker, 1964; Mincer, 1958). In this context, investment in
education and VET (Vocational Education and Training) is
presented not only as a way for employees to improve their
skills and competencies but also as a means for upgrading
human capital overall, which can lead to economic growth
(EC, 2016; EC, 2020).
Despite the above, there are still significant skill mismatches in
many European regions, which the EU is trying to alleviate by
better linking education and employment, supporting training
programmes, and promoting labour mobility. Notably, a key
factor in the Europe 2020 Strategy for achieving dynamic labour

Human Capital and its uneven spatial distribution:
theoretical considerations and a tentative
conceptual framework

In general, the skills, competencies, and knowledge required
by the workforce are determined by the tasks each worker
must undertake for their position and the industry in which
they work (Giouli et al., 2021). There are two broad categories of skills. Hard skills are those required to perform a certain
job, including for instance language proficiency, expertise on
certain software, technical skills, etc. (McGuinness et al., 2018).
As these skills are acquired mostly through formal education,
(re)training, and on-the-job experience, they are measurable
through academic degrees and various types of certificates.
Given the latter, this is the type of skills this paper will mostly
focus on. Nevertheless, soft skills—also known as non-cognitive
skills or interpersonal and personal skills—complement
hard skills and are basic competencies needed in the workplace, even if they are not tied to a specific job. Under this
category, communication, teamwork, and organizational skills
are included, being actively sought after by employers regardless of the sector or occupation (McGuinness et al., ibid).
Overall, the development of both hard and soft skills is
closely linked to better career prospects, either in waged
positions or in self-employment (Vinayan et al., 2020).
To engage with the necessary skills required in the workplace, the relevant literature has developed numerous theories. Although having appeared early after WWII, HCT still
has a firm grasp on the public discourse and policy documents.
Postulating that wage income is a function of education and
work experience, HCT highlights the importance of individual
educational attainment (Mincer, 1958). Specifically, education
increases skills and thus productivity, which is then rewarded
with a higher income (Becker, 1964). In fact, the level of
education of a workforce remains crucial also on a broader
note, as it increases national income and ultimately generates
multiplier effects (Pritchett, 2001). Therefore, education is not
only crucial for the workers themselves, but also for economies (Tan, 2014). To maximize the effects of higher educational attainment, however, talent has to be allocated in an

uneven development (Viñuela-Jiménez et al., 2010). As such,
metropolitan areas with a diverse economic base concentrate
human capital, cutting-edge technology, and finance-related
activities, while economically backward and declining regions
become even more disconnected from knowledge and innovation (Feldman et al., 2021). Similarly, developed industrial
regions tend to attract new activities, workforce, and capital, as they further industrialise (Boudeville, 1968), although
in many cases they also spill over positive effects to their
surrounding areas (Perroux, 1955). However, in most cases,
while the latter regions enter virtuous cycles of regional growth,
others—mainly peripheral, rural and less developed regions
(MacKinnon et al., 2022)—are locked in vicious cycles due
to outdated technology, institutional weaknesses and other
factors (Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023). In the above, apart from
the dynamism of a regional industrial structure, the sectoral
specialization per se plays a key role. Characteristically, sectors like accommodation and catering, commerce and storage, and transportation, are reported to suffer greater skill
mismatches—both horizontal and vertical—than others
(Chłoń-Domińczak & Żurawski, 2017).
Having said the above, even within regions where significant
volumes of high-skilled workers are employed, labour markets
have become increasingly polarized, as different fractions of
capital seek for both cheap and skilled workers (CapsadaMunsech & Valiente, 2020; Teixeira, 2022). Such segmentation is crucially sustained by dual human capital development
mechanisms. Namely, next to a robust education system, a
training system that focuses more on vocational training and
apprenticeship initiatives is formed, serving as a channel for
precarious labour (Teixeira, 2019). Nevertheless, dual educational systems such as Germany’s have also been praised for
their positive effects in school-to-work transitions, albeit having been oversimplified in the relevant literature (Deissinger,
2015). Similarly praised is the universalistic regime in Nordic
countries like Denmark, which invest in human capital and
match high levels of educational attainment with demand for
such workers (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019). Beyond educational
systems, a number of policy and governance factors can shape
youth transition regimes. Indicatively, the role of institutions
and stakeholders in counselling and supporting graduates ameliorates or exacerbates spatial disparities in skill mismatches
(Ali, 2014); in statist regimes, for instance, employers are not
sufficiently involved in matching skills’ demand and supply
(Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020). As such, in the
sub-protective regime of the Mediterranean EU, in countries
like Greece, youth transitions to adulthood are long and problematic (Emmanouil et al., 2023). This leads to a large pool
of highly qualified people who either cannot find a job, have
a profession for which they are overqualified, or have a profession in a different sector from the one in which they were
trained, while at the same time many companies are struggling
to fill their vacancies (Biagi et al., 2020). Similar phenomena
of weak linkages between education and labor market, high
levels of temporary employment, extensive skill mismatches
and low labor demand characterize the post-socialist regime
in countries like Bulgaria (Hadjivassiliou et al., 2019).

Methodological remarks

The aim of this paper is to study and contextualize the uneven
geography of skill mismatches, focusing on highly educated
workers. To do so, it first calculates the concentrations of workers with high levels of education in low, medium, and high
skill-level occupations. The focus on the workers with high
education levels serves in examining the contested issue of
overskilling (Brunello & Wruuck, 2021). The severe short- and
long-term consequences of overskilling, as indicated in the literature, underline the high scientific importance and policy
relevance of this objective. Then, to contextualize the observed
uneven geography of skill mismatches, the paper employs
a simple correlation analysis, considering various economic,
demographic, and spatial metrics that represent crucial
underlying factors.
The analysis is conducted at the regional level (NUTS-2) and
its geographical scope covers five countries: Greece, Bulgaria,

Apart from calculating the relative regional concentrations
using the above formulas (as shown in Figure 2–Figure 4), the

To distinguish occupations by skill level, Eurostat follows the
International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08)
of the International Labour Organization (ILO, 2025). Specifically, occupations are classified as of high skill-level if they
correspond to ISCO occupational groups 1 to 3 (managerial
and scientific positions), of medium skill-level when they correspond to ISCO levels 4 and 8 (intermediate level manual and
non-manual positions), and of low skill-level if they correspond
to ISCO level 9 (entry level manual and non-manual positions;
see Annex 1). The classification of skill levels was carried out
at our request by Eurostat itself, to eliminate the risk of misjudging skill levels and to increase the reliability of the mismatch index. Also, as the focus of this paper is on the regional
level, our analysis cannot include skill mismatches by sector
and region, given that this would lead to extensive data fragmentation and therefore to issues of reliability and missing
values.
To distinguish education levels, Eurostat follows UNESCO’s
(2012) classification. Specifically, the education level is designated as high if it corresponds to ISCED levels 6 to 8 (tertiary
education: from bachelor’s degree to doctorate or equivalent),
medium if it corresponds to ISCED levels 3 to 5 (upper secondary to post-secondary education, including short tertiary
education), and low if it corresponds to ISCED levels 0 to 2
(pre-school to lower secondary).

To better contextualise our findings, we also conducted a
correlation analysis between the concentrations of all types of
skill (mis)matches and various metrics that the relevant literature has indicated as significant. Drawing upon our main findings, here we discuss the most crucial factors underlying the
uneven geography of skill mismatches. In this framework, we put
particular emphasis on the economic dynamism of national
economies, regional industrial structures, urbanization, local
institutional structures and practices, and demographic structures, drawing from the main findings of the relevant literature
(Alamá-Sabater et al., 2024; Cedefop, 2010).

allocation is reflected in the negative correlation between
national GDP and LQM. Indeed, robust national economies,

Within a value chain, a higher value added is typically attributed to the initial-stage and the later-stage activities, whereas
those in the middle are lower value added (forming a “smiley
face” curve; Mohiuddin & Su, 2014). Specifically, activities
which come first in production chains relate to the standardization, innovation, and design of a product, activities coming
afterwards include the manufacturing and assembly of a product, and activities that come last relate to logistics, marketing,
and branding (Fróes de Borja Reis & Barroso de Souza, 2018).
As such, it is commonly accepted that already advanced economies achieve a better positioning within these complex structures. This brings upon them a series of positive outcomes,
including being able to improve productivity through attracting
high-skilled labour (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011). Moreover,
this positioning is clearly reflected in country-level differences in education systems, which allows benefited countries
to better develop high-skilled labour in the first place. As mentioned above, the countries that we study follow different educational paths that have a direct impact on the labour market.
For example, the universalistic (e.g.Denmark) regime with high
investments in education and fast and stable school-to-work
transitions is completely different from countries of the
sub-protective regime (e.g. Greece), which display severe skills
mismatches and low labour demand in general (Hadjivassiliou
et al., 2019).
Beyond the national scale, positioning within GVCs translates in profoundly different ways at the regional level as
well (Fernandez-Stark et al., 2011). Characteristically, stark
inequalities among regions regarding skill and education matching were found in Germany and France despite being strong
national economies overall, while instances of a great matching
were identified within lagging national economies, as in the
case of Bulgaria’s and Greece’s capital regions (Yugozapaden
& Attica respectively). For one, this observation underlines
how robust regions within a less robust national context
over-concentrate capital and activities at the expense of their
surrounding regions. Such a case is Yugozapaden in Bulgaria,
with an LQH score of over 1.31 in 2021. Interestingly, the same
applies even in Germany, France, and Denmark, although to
a lesser extent. Indicatively, LQH was found to be particularly high in Berlin (Germany) and Hovedstaden (Denmark),
while many regions in these countries did not perform as well.
The above helps us to—at least partially—refute theoretical
and empirical postulations around the spillover effect of
growth poles (see Florida et al., 2008).
Overall, the above highlights the importance of regional industrial specializations and innovation capacity, which are
deeply interconnected. Specifically, beyond the correlation
between LQH and regional GDP, our analysis documented
the statistical significance of the Innovation Index’s scores.

In further examining the determinants of skill (mis)matches,
closely related to industrial specializations is the extent of
urbanization. As the analysis revealed, there is a positive
moderate correlation between the LQH and the population density, showing that urbanization is important in human capital
allocation. Regions with dynamic metropolitan areas typically
abound in occupations requiring higher skill levels, thus,
demand for skilled labour is high there (Psycharis et al., 2014);
on the other end, urban environments often constitute more
attractive environments for a high skilled and creative labour
force as they are characterized by more amenities—cultural,
transportations-wise, choices for education, etc.—and lower
entry barriers for newcomers (Florida et al., 2008). This is
rather expected, since large urban agglomerations act as dominant poles, accumulating population and capital, concentrating the better part of domestic infrastructure, and attracting
foreign investments (Perroux, 1955), eventually fueling processes of self-sustained growth (Polyzos, 2015). Nevertheless,
our analysis highlighted a mild positive correlation between
population density and perfect skill matching (LQH), as well
as no negative correlation between density and LQL and LQM.
This indicates that, instead of metropolitan regions in general, it was mostly the capital regions within each country that
exhibited a good performance in skill matching. In fact, many
metropolitan regions were found to simultaneously display
over-concentrations of highly educated people in low-skill occupations, as for instance Île-de-France. This is a particularly
important observation, as it implies deep cutting inequalities
and fierce competition even within the most dynamic regional
labour markets, which have in fact exacerbated during the
2010s recession (Herod et al., 2021). Indeed, the relevant literature has noted that urbanized regions exhibit highly polarized labour markets (Capsada-Munsech & Valiente, 2020).
Namely, besides the core of well-qualified people working in
high skill occupations, there is a periphery of precarious workers undertaking menial tasks (Teixeira, 2022). Moreover, with
super-star cities within robust national economies and capital
cities within smaller national economies concentrating a larger
highly educated workforce than what their labour markets can
absorb, and thus channeling it to lower skill-level occupations,
the simultaneous high scores in LQH and LQL cannot be seen as a
paradox.
Nevertheless, it is not central metropolitan areas that were
found to exhibit the starkest overskilling (LQL) but those around
them. Aside from some robust regions then, overskilling is so
widespread that one out of four university graduates in the
EU works in jobs requiring lesser skills than what they have
acquired through their education (Cedefop, 2018). Moreover,
this phenomenon is persisting, as skill mismatches in most
peripheral regions do not solely emerge in times of economic
turbulence, but they constitute diachronic traits (Iammarino
et al., 2019); this is despite approaches such as HCT postulating that overskilling cannot persist in the long run and

(in terms of education attainment level) and demand (in terms
of demanded skill-level in occupations), especially when it
comes to upper-level skills. To further decipher this remark,
we discussed the role of five interrelated factors: economic
dynamism at the national level, regional sectoral specializations, urbanization, institutional structures, and demographic
trends. In this framework, we briefly discussed the role of
regions’ positioning within broader structures such as GVCs.
Importantly, our analysis pinpointed that human capital is
shaped by multiple forces at the same time, most of which
relate with each other in a dialectical way. Furthermore, we
identified a series of self-sustained, regenerating processes of
resilience and vulnerability, i.e., virtuous and vicious cycles.
Ultimately, we found human capital to be of the utmost importance in regional development. Although it can generate spatial growth, its concentration in certain areas leaves lagging
regions out of growth pathways.
To break this pattern of uneven development, policy interventions are required. Thus, here we recommend a series of actions
drawing upon the five factors discussed above, while taking into account the EU’s “New Skills Agenda”. Our recommendations have been tailored for the regions associated with
the ERUA but can be applied to other contexts as well, given
that these regions represent Europe’s key employment regimes.
Looking at the local institutional structures and practices, in
the regions with strong research and academic activity like the
ones related to the consortium, it is important that their universities work together with stakeholders from industry to spill
over knowledge and cover the research and innovation
needs of the labour market. In this frame, learning events for
reskilling and upskilling in emerging sectors—e.g., green
skills—are necessary. In addition, high quality career counselling is key, especially for vulnerable groups such as the youth,
women, and migrants, for not only making sensible early career
decisions but also in overcoming career challenges throughout their lives (Papakota, 2016). In fact, as noted above, such
career counseling is more than necessary in peripheral regions,
where career choices are less informed due to “information
asymmetries” (McGuinness et al., 2018). In this context,
educational structures such as schools, universities or vocational training centres should respond to the needs of students
at different stages of career development by identifying skills,
experiences, values and personality traits of graduates, technological trends and employment opportunities, employers’
expectations and career planning (Litoiu & Oproiu, 2012).
Lastly, tighter networks of social, scientific, and policy-related
stakeholders should be encouraged, so that through joint
actions they engage in labour market forecasting and early
identification of skill shortages.
Regarding the spatially uneven distribution of human capital due
to national economies’ dynamism (or lack thereof) or regional
industrial specializations, it can be addressed through targeted investment incentives and guided industrial reorientation.
