When an affordance is of shared relevance to\ntwo or more individuals, these agents will then be ready to act\ntogether, to cooperate with each other and to pool their individual abilities, so as to improve grip on the overall situation\nin which the affordances are nested. Moreover, the result of\nthis pooling of their individual abilities is that actions become\npossible (like the lifting of the long plank) that were not\npossible for each of the participants acting as individuals9.\nConsider for instance a dyad using a two-handed saw to cut\nthrough a fallen tree. Person A takes control of the left side of\nthe saw, while person B controls the right side of the saw. The\naffordances offered by the saw are to use a technical term, ’nested’\nin the wider context of the activity of sawing the piece of wood\nin order to build something together. Possibilities for action are\noften nested within other affordances (Van Dijk & Rietveld,\n2021). The affordances of the saw in this example are embedded\nwithin the affordances of the shed where the building activity is\ntaking place.\nDealing adequately with matters of shared relevance (like\nthe liftability of the long plank) will require multiple individuals to combine their abilities. However, this is not something that can ordinarily be\nso readily assumed. If I am to embark on some joint endeavour with another agent, I must know that they are willing to do\nwhat it takes to accomplish some end that is of mutual interest\nto both of us, and they must know the same about me. It might\nthen be argued that for both of us to know this about each other\nwill require each of us to first identify and ascribe intentions to\nthe other, and perhaps even to know that we have done so.\nHow is it that the two agents managed to act in coordinated\nways if they do not represent who is doing what, when and\nhow? The two\nindividuals act in coordinated ways because both are responding to a situation of shared relevance (on the basis of abilities\nthat they have acquired thanks to a history of interactions in the\nsame form of life). We can see this in the temporal\ndynamics of the individual’s skilled behaviour. Each\naffordance-related state of action-readiness operates over different time scales. For instance, some states of action-readiness\nrelate to the instructions of the experimenters, operating over\nlonger time scales of the duration of the whole experiment. Instead of moving planks now imagine that a\ngroup of people are engaged in moving a heavy piano down\nsome stairs. The movements of each person will have to\ncomplement each other if they are to avoid losing grip on the\npiano. Each person will however be moving in a slightly\ndifferent way, some providing more of a supporting role\nfor the piano while other will take more of a responsibility for moving the piano downwards. It is crucial that all the\nindividual movements that are being made are carefully and\ntightly adjusted to each other. The affordances of the heavy\npiano play a crucial role in this process, as of course do those\nof the stairs the people are descending. The agents are continuously adjusting their movements to each other, but only in\nrelation to the effect their respective movements are having\non the moving of the piano in the wider context of the stairs.\nIn joint action multiple individuals succeed in coordinating with\neach other because they form what we have called an interpersonal synergy. The movement scientist Nikolai Bernstein\n\nobserved that the number of muscles and joints in the human\nbody creates a problem of control (Bernstein, 1967; Kelso, 2009;\nTurvey, 1990). Each individual muscle and joint has multiple\ndegrees of freedom. For each action the agent is ready to perform, there are very many different constellations of muscle and\njoint dynamics that can bring about the same action. Yet when\nwe move our body, all of our muscles and joints work together\nin concert in ways that are somehow adjusted and fine-tuned\nto the affordances to which the agent is responding. If the\nindividual is writing with a pencil for instance, this activity\nconstrains the timing and force of contraction and relaxation\nof the individual muscles and joints in his hands, fingers, and\narms. The joints and muscles appear not to act independently\nbut interdependently, self-organising through their coupling,\nconstraining each other’s degrees of freedom, and thereby\nreducing the need for control. Similarly, when several people\nare moving a piano, it is the constraints that derive from\npeople being coupled to the piano that reduce the degrees of\nfreedom of their individual movements allowing them to\nfunction together as a single coordinated whole. The mutual\nadaptation makes the behaviour of the individuals interdependent in a way that makes it possible for them to pool their skills,\nso as to coordinate their responsiveness to multiple affordances\nof shared relevance. It is the affordances of the heavy piano in\nthe situation of descending the steep stairs that invites the individuals carrying the piano together to coordinate their actions\nin a particular way. There is no need for them to plan who\ndoes what and when. Instead, this is settled on the fly through\nthe interpersonal synergy they form as they couple to the piano\nin the particular setting of the stairs.\nIn the final section, we pull together the two elements we\nhave laid out so far of a field of relevant affordances containing affordances of shared relevance and interpersonal synergies\nto propose an account of we-intentionality in terms of skilled\nintentionality. We will see that interpersonal synergies form\nthrough engagement with affordances of shared relevance.\nThese affordances constrain and limit the degrees of freedom in\nthe behaviour of the interlocutors. The coordination that is\nachieved is best accounted for in terms of the behaviour of a\nsingle integrated system that is jointly shaped by the co-actors\nas they respond to the affordances of shared relevance.\n\nSkilled we-intentionality\n\nIn the previous section, we saw how it is the affordances of the\njoint liftability of the piano nested in the setting of the stairs\naround which the interpersonal synergy self-organises. The\nmultiple nested affordances that make up the situation in which\nthe piano is being moved together function as constraints on\nthe self-organising process. Each relevant affordance generates\nmicro-states of action readiness. Multiple simultaneous states\nof action readiness self-organise to form a macro-pattern of\naction readiness across the two individuals (an interpersonal\nsynergy) in ways that are constrained by affordances of\nshared relevance. The affordances of the piano moving situation provide constraints on the behaviour of the individuals.\nThe individuals because of their skills are responsive to these\n\n\n\nconstraints. Their skills attune them to the dynamically changing\nand evolving situation as they move the piano allowing them to\nmaintain a good grip on the piano.\nThe central question for a theory of joint action seen from this\necological dynamical perspective is under what conditions\naffordances stand out as a shared invitation to be acted on by\nmultiple individuals. We’ve seen (in section two with the example of the football player) how affordances stand out as inviting to an individual when they improve grip. One is ready to\nact on those relevant affordances that allow one to tend\ntowards a better grip. The same is true of shared invitations to\nact. Affordances stand out as of shared relevance when it is\nonly by acting in ways that are mutually adapted that each of\nthe individuals is able to improve grip. Suppose one is using a\ntwo-handed saw. This is the kind of saw that you can only\nuse to cut through a fallen tree if when one agent pushes on\nthe saw, the other agent pulls. The rhythm the two agents\nestablish is thus crucial to using the saw skilfully. The two\nagents need to mutually adapt to each other producing complementary actions, just like in the case of the piano we just\ndiscussed. If they do not adapt to each other, they will have\nthe sense as a dyad that what they are doing is not going well.\nSo long as they mutually adapt each allowing their actions\nto be drawn from them by the affordances of the two-handed\nsaw, they will succeed in improving grip as a dyad. Thus,\naffordances will be experienced as shared invitations to multiple\nindividuals when each of the individuals has the sense that a\nway to improve grip is by joining forces with other agents. We\ncan suppose that this is a sense the individuals develop often\nthrough interaction with others. We learn that there are certain\naffordances such as those of two-handed saws that can only\nbe engaged with skilfully by doing things together with other\npeople.\nWe-intentionality that is typically taken to be required for acting jointly can therefore be explained in terms of inviting\naffordances that act as constraints on the self-organising states of\naction readiness. The agents that participate in a joint action\ndo not need to have predefined intentions or goals that specify who is to do what and when, in order to coordinate with\neach other. Multiple agents can have control over an action\nthe states of action readiness self-organise to form interpersonal synergies in response to affordances of shared\nrelevance.\nIt might be objected that people often do act based on explicit\ngoals and intentions that they set for themselves in advance\nof acting. The rich literature on planning of which Michael\nBratman for instance has made important contributions is all\nabout the cognitive dynamics of goal-setting (Bratman, 1987;\ncf. Castelfranchi & Paglieri, 2007; Pacherie, 2006). We would\nrespond that even cases of long-term planning can naturally\nbe described in terms of skilled intentionality. Suppose that\nI have agreed with my partner that I will cook tonight, and\nI am thinking about the ingredients I will need. The dish is one\nI’ve made many times before and because of my extensive\n\npractice I know exactly what to do to make it taste its\nbest. I know in particular which vegetables and spices are\nneeded. Yet, I don’t have what I need and this situation makes\nthe visit to the supermarket an attractive course of action.\nA supermarket visit is a possibility for action (a large-scale\naffordance) available in our human form of life and one with\nwhich I am very familiar. All of this thinking and planning\ncan be readily made sense of in terms of my skilled engagement with this large-scale affordance over a long time-scale\n(Van Dijk & Rietveld, 2021). Understanding action in terms\nof skilled intentionality allows for an explanation of how the\nindividual is always ready for multiple possibilities that matter to them even when some of these possibilities are some way\noff in the future.\nConsider, as a final example ostensive communication through\npointing. Suppose we are out collecting fruit together and\nI point at some berries but all you can see are leaves. You\ncan readily infer that I am not pointing at the leaves but most\nlikely at some hidden berries. One might suppose that you can\nmake this inference because the shared goal of finding berries is part of our common ground (Tomasello, 2014). There are\nmany possible features of the current perceptual scene I might\nbe attempting to direct your attention towards. To identify\nwhich of these features is actually of relevance to you, you\nmust first determine what it is that I am intending to make\nyou believe. You must think something along the following\nlines: his pointing in the direction of those leaves would make\nsense if he intends to make me believe there are berries hidden over there behind those leaves. Similarly, when I make\nthe pointing gesture, I am also imagining how the gesture\nis likely to be received by you, and whether it is likely to be\nunderstood. I must think about what my partner will infer\nabout my intentions, and the beliefs I intend to bring about\nin her. We’ve argued that in perceiving the pointing gesture,\nyou will be able to immediately recognise that I mean to draw\nyour attention to the hidden berries, not to the leaves. It is the\nhidden berries that are of shared relevance to us in this situation. We are after all, engaged in the shared activity of hunting for berries. The pointing gesture is made in a situation\nthat is of shared relevance because we are undertaking this\nactivity together.\n\nConclusion\n\nOur aim in this paper has been to situate we-intentionality in\nrelation to the living environment. We have shown how people can succeed in acting together in coordinated ways without reasoning about each other’s intentions. We have proposed\nan account of we-intentionality in terms of skilled intentionality, the coordination with multiple affordances simultaneously.\nWhen people coordinate with each other, they are responsive\nto (multiple) affordances of shared relevance. We have explained\nthis responsiveness in terms of multiple states of bodily\naction readiness that self-organise in ways that are scaffolded\nand constrained by the affordances of shared relevance. Typically, people are able to coordinate with each other on the basis\nof skills for responding to affordances of shared relevance.\n\n\n\nWe’ve asked the concept of shared relevance to do a good\ndeal of explanatory work in the account of joint action we have\nproposed. The key question in the account of joint action we\npropose is why it should be that an affordance (like the liftability of the plank) should stand out from the landscape of\naffordances as inviting or calling for the coordinated action of\nmultiple individuals. Why should it be that individuals sometimes find themselves ready to join forces and collaborate in\nresponding to affordances of shared relevance? The answer\nto this question is just the same as the answer we would give in\nthe case of skilled intentionality for the actions of individuals. Affordances stand out as relevant for an individual agent\nbecause of disattunement in the agent-environment system as\na whole, which the individual then acts to reduce. Affordances\nare of shared relevance, we have argued, when the only\nway to improve grip is by forming an interpersonal synergy\nwith other individuals, like in the example of the two-handed\nsaw. Affordances more generally stand out from the landscape\n\nas being of shared relevance to two or more individuals\nbecause of the sociomaterial practice of which the individuals\nare members10. The berries stand out as relevant for the people that are foraging together for instance, because foraging\nis part of their shared way of life, and the berries are significant in the context of this shared activity. There is something\nthat the individuals care about because of the practices in\nwhich they take part, and they are ready to join forces with relevant affordances and act together based on what matters to\nthem as members of a practice.\n\nData availability\n\nNo data are associated with this article.\n10\n\nWe borrow the term “sociomaterial practice” from anthropology and the\nfield of organizational studies in which it is used to refer to the entanglement of social practice with the material forms of the places in which people\nact and interact (Mol, 2002; Orlikowski, 2007).\n\nReferences\nAbramova K, Slors M: Social cognition in simple action coordination: a case\nfor direct perception.       "R1": "Comments on Kiverstein and Rietveld, “Skilled We-intentionality”, 2021\nThis paper is a valuable notification for philosophers who work on social and group cognition that\necological and dynamical psychologists have been building up a substantial body of work that is\ndirectly relevant to their concerns. These psychologists have built up an expanding body of\nempirical results and theoretical arguments that explain joint action without any reference to\nmental representations—whether individual, joint, or aligned. Kiverstein and Rietveld argue that\nshared affordances structure joint actions, without representations. This is in direct contradiction\nto the assumptions that have structured the philosophical debate. Because Kiverstein and Rietveld\ncite all the key players in the philosophical debate, those key players are much more likely to pay\nsome attention than they have. Most of us in the ecological and dynamical communities typically\nignore these philosophers; and they ignore us right back. (I have been guilty of this in my own\nwriting on joint action.) This paper is a worthwhile contribution and worth publishing for that\nreason alone.\nThat said, I do have one major concern about the role of the Skilled Intentionality Framework in\nthe argument. Let me preface the concern by saying that I am a fan of the Skilled Intentionality\nFramework and am even an author on one of the papers promoting and elaborating it. However,\nsome of my colleagues in the ecological and dynamical psychology community complain that the\nSkilled Intentionality Framework is primarily a re-branding of ideas that were already in the\nscientific literature, and that its only innovations are terminological (and so unnecessary). I do not\nshare this complaint, and defend the Skilled Intentionality Framework when it is raised. But it\ndoes align with my main concern with this paper. How much argumentative work is the Skilled\nIntentionality Framework actually doing in this paper?\nThe main argumentative structure of the paper goes from affordances, via development, to\nshared affordances to social coordination dynamics to interpersonal synergies to self-organized\njoint action on shared affordances, which obviates the need for mental representations in the\nexplanation of joint action. This is a compelling argument. (In fact, I am finishing a book that\n\n\n\n\nfollows some of the same path.) But notice that I did not mention the Skilled Intentionality\nFramework.       "R2": "Skilled we-intentionality: Situating joint action in the living Environment” argues that the literature on\njoint action, mainly devoted to understanding different activities in which two or more individuals\nact together, rather than alone or in parallel, has so far underplayed the role of environmental\naffordances in accounting for joint activities. To address this omission, the paper proposes a\ndevelopment of the already existing framework of skilled intentionality to study cases of weintentionality in which individuals are engaged with others in joint activities.\nThe paper is a good contribution to the literature and develops an interesting account of skilled\naction that incorporates the key role of the living environment in understanding joint action. Yet,\nthe main claims in the paper are not entirely clear, and some of the key concepts proposed in the\npaper are ambiguous (in particular, that of ‘grip’ and ‘shared relevance’). This is because the paper\nis covering a variety of cases of joint activity that seem to work differently. Consequently, the key\nconcepts put forward in the paper appear to apply differently to different cases of joint activity, if\nthey apply at all. The paper needs to state its ambitions and main aim more clearly, as well as\nrefine some of its central claims and conclusions. In what follows, I substantiate these points in\nmore detail, while also commenting on the claims in the paper that invite clarification or\nexpansion, for doing so can improve an already interesting and worthwhile paper.\n1.The first observation concerns the key concepts advanced in the paper.\nIn the following passage we are given a general characterization of the view:\n“People coordinate and join forces in acting together by responding to affordances of shared\nrelevance in the living environment. When an affordance is of shared relevance to two or more\nindividuals, these agents will then be ready to act together, to cooperate with each other and to\npool their individual abilities, so as to improve grip on the overall situation in which the\naffordances are nested.” (p.7, my emphasis)\nAccording to this characterization, joint action coordination takes place when the environment\noffers the opportunity/invitation for individuals to act together. This is, when affordances are of\n‘shared relevance’ for the individuals (ready to be) involved in the action. ‘Shared relevance’ is\ndefined in terms of ‘improvement of grip”, and ‘improvement of grip’ for a group of agents is\ndefined in analogy with the individual case, that is, in terms of the reduction of disattunement in\nthe agent-environment system:\n\n\n\n\n“Why should it be that individuals sometimes find themselves ready to join forces and collaborate\nin responding to affordances of shared relevance? The answer to this question is just the same\nas the answer we would give in the case of skilled intentionality for the actions of\nindividuals. Affordances stand out as relevant for an individual agent because of disattunement\nin the agent-environment system, which the individual then acts to reduce. […].” (p.10, my\nemphasis)\nCoordination is then characterized as a form of “interpersonal synergy” (pp. 7-8), in terms of which\n“improvement of grip” is jointly obtained. Thus, circumstances in which affordances stand out as\nof ‘shared relevance’ are those in which “the only way to improve grip is by forming an\ninterpersonal synergy with other individuals, like in the example of the two-handed saw.” (p.10, my\nemphasis).\nIt is important to note that the authors define a situation where there is shared relevance of\naffordances when “the only” way of improving grip is by forming an interpersonal synergy. Yet this\ndescription applies to certain forms of shared activity and not others.\nSome of the examples the authors give such as moving a heavy piano together down the stairs\n(p.8), or joining another agent to use a two-handed saw to cut through a tree (p.7), are cases\nwhere the definitions just discussed seem to apply cogently. Yet, in many cases of shared activity,\nthere is no clear ‘grip’ improvement that is taking place. For example, imagine a crowd in a\nstadium joining in a ‘Mexican wave’ to celebrate the victory of their soccer team and someone\nsitting in the same stand as these winning team supporters, but who is not a supporter of that\nteam and does not join in the wave. Later, her own team scores a goal and she celebrates by\nsinging a song that the supporters of her team are singing at the other side of the stadium. How\nshould we think of her not joining the wave in terms of the view proposed? Should we say that she\nis not reducing the disattunment in the agent-environment system by not joining in or should we\nsay the opposite? What about the singing in the other side of the field? There is no obvious\nexplanation in terms of disattunement here, for there seem to be different dimensions of\nexplanation involved. The person’s relation to her own team, and her determination to act as a\nmember of that team, might say more about her motivations and actions than her bodily situation\nin the current context.\nOther sets of cases that seem very different from the two-handed saw are cases that involve\nacting with others, but that are not cases of bodily interaction. For example, we all paint the house\ntogether, but we came to paint on different days with explicit instructions distributed to us in\nadvance. In this case, it seems that we need other elements apart from interpersonal synergy to\nexplain coordination between agents. Though, in this case, the environment plays a key role, there\nis much more to be said about how these relations work - between agents, between agents and\nenvironment, in relation to plans, etc. - compared to the double hand-saw case.\nAnother interesting kind of case is the following: I am about to cross the street and I see an old\nlady struggling to step down the sidewalk. I step in to help her. Maybe I do not clearly decide in\nthe sense of making a conscious decision to help her, rather I jump in and help her cross the\nstreet. This action of mine arguably reduces my attunement with the environment for I am now\npart of a dyad with the old lady, and we, crossing together the street, are much clumsier and\nslower as a joint agential system that I would be if crossing alone (though, arguably, she is doing\nbetter in this joint action scenario than alone). It is not clear what the affordances of shared\n\n\n\n\nrelevance are in this case, for I do not improve my grip on the situation by joining her, though\nwhen we are in fact crossing the street some degree of reduction of disattunment might help\nexplain - in part - our bodily coordination. It may be argued that, in this case, it is my values and\nthe prevailing social norms by which I orient my action that explain what I am doing and why I am\ndoing it, and perhaps even how do it (since I am gentle, I wait longer than I would with a younger\nperson, I do this because of respect and kindness, even if perhaps this is not the most efficient\nway for us to cross the street (could I carry her in my arms instead?)). The authors come close to\nthis sort of consideration when they say:\n“Affordances more generally stand out from the landscape as being of shared relevance to two or\nmore individuals because of the socio-material practice of which the individuals are members\n(There is something that the individuals care about because of the practices in which they take\npart, and they are ready to join forces with relevant affordances and act together based on what\nmatters to them as members of a practice.)” (p.9)\nThis might be true. Incidentally this explanation seems to fit better not only with my three own\ncases here, but also with their own examples of the ‘silent carriage in the train’ (p.7) and those of\n‘shopping for a home-renovation’ (p.8) and ‘hunting for berries together’ (p.9), in which a shared\nactivity is explain in terms of other shared activities and practices. But it is to be noted how these\nexamples, as much as mine above, are hardly explainable in terms of the notions of improvement\nof grip and reducing disattunement in the agent-environment system, as defined in the paper.\nThe disanalogy here runs deep, for if we were to keep the same terminology to explain how\nindividuals are motivated to act and coordinate in all these cases, the terms would become highly\nmetaphorical. Can my sitting silent in a silent carriage of a train be completely explained in terms\nof improvement of grip, independently of my aim of respecting the social norm of ‘keeping quiet’\nthat prevails in that setting? Can we explain Mexican waves or helping an old lady cross the street\nas agents’ ways of improving their grip in the agent-environment system or do we need in\naddition, and crucially, reference to the agents’ intentions, values or group memberships? These\nare cases in which socio-cultural norms and values in place partly explain what the agents are\ndoing and why. In this, these cases are quite different than handling a piano together or a doublehanded saw. In the latter, there is a clear bodily sense in which the action involves two agents, and\nits success requires bodily coordination that does indeed improve grip in the agents-environment\nsystem – the interpersonal synergy the authors speak about. Moreover, these same cases seem\nexplainable in terms of coordination, independently of any further aims that the agents may have,\nexcept those that the coordination brings about, i.e. cutting the tree or moving the piano down\nthe stairs. In the examples I provided though, whereas in some cases there is bodily coordination\n(e.g., the stadium wave; helping an old lady cross the street), this is either not a straightforward\ncase of improving grip, and/or the motivations of the agents are not completely explainable\nindependently of further values, intentions and aims. For these kinds of cases, we cannot explain\nwhat the affordances of shared relevance are merely in terms of improvement of grip, where this\nis understood in terms of reduction of disattunment of the agent-environment system by the sole\nmeans of forming interpersonal synergies. To explain all these cases in that way would turn terms\nlike “relevance”, “disattunement”, and “grip” highly metaphorical and vague, for they would explain\nbodily coordination and sparse time-space coordination, cases where there is face-to-face\ninteraction and cases where agents are anonymous to each other, cases of joint action motivated\nby group membership, and cases motivated by emotional contagion, etc., in exactly in the same\nway. It looks as if we do need more nuances here.\n\n\n\n\n2. Another key issue that results from the different cases that the authors aim to explain by\nthe same means, is that depending on what case we focus on, the dialectics with the current\naccounts of joint action changes.\nIn p.9, the authors claim:\n“The agents that participate in a joint action do not need to have predefined intentions or goals\nthat specify who is to do what and when, in order to coordinate with each other. Multiple\nagents can have control over an action the states of action readiness self-organise to form\ninterpersonal synergies in response to affordances of shared relevance.”\nWhile this is true in the case of the two-handed saw, it is not true of the case of helping the old\nlady, or in their own examples of “hunting for berries” or “shopping for a home renovation”. In\nthese cases, it seems that we need further explanation of what the agents are doing and those\ninvolve reference to something more: some goals, intentions, values, or social norms that the\nagents share. In this respect, these cases are sufficiently different from cases which can be\nunderstood in terms of “interpersonal synergies in response of affordances of shared relevance”.\n(NB: Some authors have attempted systematic characterizations and classifications of the different cases\nof joint action (see e.g. Knoblich et al. 2011 and Satne 2020a). According to these classifications, some\njoint/shared activities involve shared planes and/or shared intentions while others can be explained\nwithout making reference to any prior intentions, goals or plans (see Satne 2020b and Butterfill 2012)\nfor alternative accounts of how to characterize each case). The latter are described as forms of\n“emergent coordination” (Knoblich et al. 2011), “minimalist” forms of joint action, (Butterfill 2016) or\n“basic joint intentional activities” (Satne 2020a, b; Fiebich & Gallagher 2013).)\nThis important difference between different kinds of joint activity has a direct impact on the claim\nthat participants in joint action “do not need to have predefined intentions that specify who is to\ndo what and when, in order to coordinate with each other”. (p.9). When we focus on cases of\njoint action that are not defined by previous intentions or goals, in which bodily coordination plays\na key role, i.e. the two-handed saw, or moving a piano together up or down the stairs, the authors’\nargument runs smoothly, and the claim that their account can do without attributing intentions to\nexplain coordination is sound (for a similar claim see Satne 2020a and Knoblich 2011). Thus, for\nthese cases the concept of ‘interpersonal synergy’ and that of affordances of ‘shared relevance’ do\nindeed explain the kind of bodily coordination between different agents that takes place and how\nagents might be motivated to participate in joint action without having any predetermined\nintentions or goals. But when we consider the larger set of cases that they use to illustrate the\nview, this simply does not follow. ‘Shopping for a home-renovation’, ‘being in the silent carriage’,\n‘painting a house together at different times’, they all involve some pre-determined goals,\nintentions or norms in terms of which who takes part in a joint action and when are determined.\nEven if the authors would want to resist this claim, much more argument would be needed.\nCrucially this depends on how one understands what intentions, goals, or norms are, and how\nthey work. Thus, much more argument would be required to substantiate the claim that\n“interpersonal synergies in response of affordances of shared relevance” could explain such cases\n- and every possible case of shared activity - in the absence of any intentions, plans, goals, or\nnorms.\n\n\n\n\nIn sum, there are key issues that the authors need to address to clarify their proposal and make it\ncogent. Depending on how they address these, they could follow two alternative paths:\n1. circumscribe the focus of the paper to explain bodily coordination in joint action. This can\ninclude cases of ‘emergent coordination’ (Knoblich et al. 2011), that is, cases of bodily\ncoordination where there are no previous plans or preceding intentions or goals. This study\ncan also include as one kind of case of bodily coordination, instances of acting together that\ninvolve bodily coordination but also further aims or goals (e.g. helping the old lady, riding\non the silent carriage), in as far as it is clearly stated that the paper’s aim is not to explain all\naspects of these joint actions, but only bodily coordination in relation to the environment,\nand in this the explanation works as a complement rather that a rival to other views. It is\nclear from the paper’s arguments how the proposal applies to such cases, explaining them\nin terms of interpersonal synergies, shared relevance of affordances, and improvement of\ngrip.\n2. aim at an encompassing account, that covers all different cases of skilled we-intentionality.\nIf the authors were to follow the second route, they would need to have a different focus for\nthe paper and make correlative adjustments in the main arguments. This might require that\nsome of characterisations of joint action in terms of ‘shared relevance’ and ‘improvement of\ngrip’ as applying to all cases, be dropped, for broadening the scope of these terms too\nmuch, risks turning them vague or empty. A good strategy here would be to analyse the\ndifferent kinds of cases of joint action in more detail, explaining in what sense different\ncases of joint action can be accounted for with those same concepts, or nuances thereof,\nand in what sense any of these do involve any goals or intentions on the part of agents, that\nspecify further aims, intentions, or goals that the agents aim to fulfil by acting in that way.\nWhile both alternatives require refinements in the paper’s claims, option (1) seems more feasible\nin the light of the materials discussed in the paper. Furthermore, as explained in more detail in\nSection 3, the arguments against rival theories of joint action are straightforward in this case,\nwhile they are not if the scope is broadened to cover all sorts of different cases. In this version of\nthe paper, the aim of broadening the scope and applying the skilled intentionality tools to all cases\nof joint action could be mentioned but left for detailed discussion in a different paper (or several),\nentirely devoted to making those points.\n3. Accounts of joint action have several different aims, some of which the account presented\nby the authors addresses, while others not.\nIt would be good for the authors to clarify what exactly their account aims to do with regards to\nthese different debates and be explicit about it.\na) One of the main aims of theories of joint action is to explain what differentiates joint action and\nan action done by two individuals but in parallel.\nThe paper does not explicitly address this point. Yet, it might be that some ideas in the paper - e.g.\ninterpersonal synergy - could account for this difference at least for cases of bodily coordination.\nHowever, if the paper aims to cover other cases, such as the ‘painting the house’ where joint\naction is remote in space and time, more argument would be required to support this claim.\nb) Another aim that many views on joint action have is to position themselves on whether we can\n\n\n\n\ngive an account of joint action in terms of individual states plus other conditions, or not. This\namounts to asking whether ‘jointness’ in ‘joint action’ can be accounted for in terms that do not\npresuppose jointness/sharedness or not. Reductionist accounts (e.g. Bratman, Ludwig) claim that\nwe can, antireductionist accounts (e.g. Gilbert, Searle, Schmid) deny it.\nThe paper only briefly refers to this debate, in its discussion of Searle’s view that mainly concerns,\nas with Bratman’s, (c) below (i.e. whether inter-agential coordination can/should be explained in\nterms intentions). Now, one can wonder whether the ‘Skilled-intentionality’ view, in its own terms,\nis anti-reductionist with respect to its ‘jointness’.\nThe position is not clear in the paper, and there are no arguments explicitly addressing this\nquestion. On the one hand, in as far as ‘shared relevance’ and ‘improvement of grip’ explain joint\naction in terms of reducing disattunment of the agents-environment systems, the view could be\nseen as reductionist. Yet, this changes when we consider the cases that require framing in terms\nof further shared activities, like the case of shopping for a home-renovation. In those cases, it\nseems that ‘shared activities’ – decorating the house – explain ‘shared relevance’ – looking at this\nparticular tool in the DIY-store – and these shared activities in turn are explained by ‘a history of\ninteractions’ – the couple plans to renovate their house, etc. In this line of argument, ‘sharedness’\nmight be seen as a primitive concept, for it leads to other forms of sharedness that together might\nbe thought to form an explanatory circle.\nThus, here again we find an internal tension and an ambiguity in the view, for different cases seem\nto require different accounts. Even if one thought, as the paper also claims, that affordances are\nalways nested in other affordances, and thus that all cases are like cases of the second kind, this\nrequires further explaining. In any case, whether this is the authors’ view is speculative, for this\nissue is not explicitly discussed in the paper.\nc) Some views on joint action attempt to explain ‘coordination in joint action’ and discuss what\nelements are required to explain this, e.g. whether intentions or plans are required.\nThe paper gives an account of how coordination might work in many cases, relying on affordances\nprovided by the living environment without the need of agents having plans and following up on\nthem. In this, the paper is successful. In this respect, the proposal is indeed different from\nBratman’s, as the paper claims. Yet, it is important that the paper clarifies explicitly that these are\nnot rival accounts, as Bratman states explicitly that his view is meant only as offering sufficient\nconditions – not necessary ones – for shared intentional activity, and the authors do not disagree\nthat there might be cases of joint action where there are such explicit plans. In p.4, they explicitly\nsay this: “The account of interlocking intentions Bratman provides is perhaps well-suited to\naccount for the negotiations that might unfold between people when they plan to perform an\naction together (e.g., Bratman, 2014). However, it does not explain how multiple agents succeed in\nputting the plans they have made into effect, coordinating what they are doing when they act\ntogether in a particular situation.” While this last point might be true, Bratman does not claim to\nbe explaining this, for Bratmanian joint action might involve no bodily coordination at all. A\nBratmanian case of joint action may be like the example of agents coming to paint the house at\ndifferent times, mentioned above, where there is no face-to-face interaction, and agents have\nexplicit plans and common knowledge of those. As long as there are some cases in which there\nare individual intentions and common plans, Bratman’s account is not challenged.\n\n\n\n\nIn any case, beyond Bratman, the paper shows how intentions need not be considered necessary\nfor inter-agential action coordination to occur. Yet the authors claim that they are offering an\naccount of joint action or joint activity that does away with intentions or goals altogether (see e.g.\n“Our aim […]is to show how the work that shared goals and intentions are asked to do in\nphilosophical accounts of joint action can instead be performed by affordances of shared\nrelevance.”, p.5). This - I think - is not shown in the paper. While the arguments might show this is\nthe case for spontaneous actions that involve bodily coordination between individuals, it is not\nclear from the considerations in the paper that this is so in all cases that the paper mentions.\nd) Finally, some accounts are interested in arguing that the “sharedness” of joint action is\ndependent on the presence of a particular subject, a first-person plural subject or a ‘we’, where\n‘we’ is understood as a plural agent not reducible to a mere sum of individuals (see Schmid and\nGilbert for paradigmatic views).\nThe paper does not take a position on this aspect of the discussion. This is in part due to the fact\nthat the paper does not clarify what is meant by ‘we’ in “we-intentionality”. (See (4) below for some\nremarks on this issue.)\nIn sum, in relation to the current scholarly discussion on joint action, the account presented in the\npaper clearly addresses (c) by making the case that affordances in the living environment, that are\nof shared relevance for the individuals, can account for inter-agential action coordination in the\nabsence of intentions directed at common plans (or subplans that fit a shared plan). As said, the\npaper’s criticism of the literature on this issue is partly successful, that is, in as far as it focuses on\nthe explanation of action coordination cases that do not require a further specification of the\nagents’ activities in terms of further joint aims, shared activities or shared goals of the agents. Yet\nit is not clear that the view succeeds as a general alternative to Searle’s or Bratman’s accounts\nwhen all cases of shared activity are included. As said above, this will require more and different\nkinds of argument.\nThus, while the paper could succeed in explaining coordination in action, it should clearly state its\nscope and aim as explaining how coordination is possible without intentions, rather than claiming\nthat this is the case for all possible cases of shared activity, and attempting to cover a wide range\nof very different cases with the concepts of “shared relevance” and “improvement of grip”.
