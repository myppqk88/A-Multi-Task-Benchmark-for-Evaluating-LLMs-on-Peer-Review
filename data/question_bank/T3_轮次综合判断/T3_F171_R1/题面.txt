      "text": "Abstract\nBackground: Academic success of international medical students\nenrolled in Chinese universities is of great significance, because it\ndirectly influences their performance in the license exam and in\nobtaining a job. Insufficient research has been conducted on\nacademics’ awareness of factors related to teaching that affect their\nstudents’ academic success.\nMethods: Semi-structured interviews were conducted with academics\n(N=36) from November 2020 to January 2021 at two medical\nuniversities in China. Each interview, lasting between 30 to 70-min,\nwas audiotaped, transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic\nanalysis.\nResults: The important teaching factors that academics perceived to\ninfluence the success of students are: (i) style of pedagogy, (ii)\naddressing students’ language difficulties and differences, (iii)\nteaching resources management, (iv) attributes of the academics, (v)\nsupervision and guidance by the academics, (vi) rapport between the\nacademic and student, (vii) linking teaching content to license exams,\n(viii) classroom discipline management, and (ix) assessment style.\nConclusions: University faculties and departments that are involved in\nteaching international medical students need to ensure that academic\nstaff are provided with ongoing professional development and\nresources to enhance teaching quality. The nine areas identified\nabove should provide priority topics for such staff training.\n\n\nIntroduction\nOver the past two decades, the internationalization of China’s universities (Dang et al., 2021; Jin & Horta, 2018) has seen\nmedicine emerge as the most popular program after Chinese language for international students to pursue in Chinese\nuniversities (Zhou, 2016). The Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Medicine (MBBS) program taught in English medium\nis supported and supervised by China’s Ministry of Education (MOE) as well as being designed and implemented by\nuniversities (Huang, 2014). An example of this is the release in 2020 of the document Quality Control Standards of Basic\nMedical Education (in English Medium) for International Students in China. This is from the China Education Association\nfor International Exchange and the International Medical Education Committee, entrusted by the International Department of\nthe MOE. The objectives of this standards document were to further standardize MBBS education across Chinese\nuniversities, effectively improve training quality and promoting a sustainable development of MBBS education.\n45 universities are authorized by the MOE to recruit international students for medical education (Xie, 2021). The World\nHealth Organization (WHO) recognizes all these universities in the Directory of World Medical Schools. It means\ninternational graduates holding a Chinese MBBS degree are eligible to attend license exams in their home country.\nExamples include the Medical Council of India Screening Test, or the United States Medical Licensing Examination\n(USMLE), or the UK Professional and Linguistic Assessment Board (PLAB). This is perceived by students as one of the\ngreat advantages of studying MBBS in China—in addition to other perceived benefits that include less demanding\nadmission requirements, affordable cost, and a safe, stable society (Zhou, 2016).\nTypically, the MBBS program takes six years to complete, with five years devoted to courses in natural science, Chinese\nlanguage, basic science medicine, and clinic subjects, and one year of internship in Chinese hospitals or in students’ home\ncountry.\nMost of the MBBS students originate from low-income countries from Asia and Africa, where the demand for health\nprofessionals is high (Liu et al., 2017). The majority of the students choose to return to their home country for career\ndevelopment after completing the MBBS program—except for some students who continue further studies (Huang,\n2019; Fan et al., 2020). Jiang and Sun (2007) found that 87.4% of MBBS students planned to engage in medicine-related\ncareers after graduation to make a significant addition to the health workforce in their home country, or they would\nmigrate to other high-income countries (Li & Sun, 2019; Han & Guo, 2009). The quality of MBBS education in China\nwill likely influence their future license exam performance and the employment position they obtain. The reputation\ngained by these courses is the key to sustainable development of medical education for international students (Zhou,\n2016).\nMedical license exams are fundamental to evaluate the learning of medical schools and their graduates (Han & Guo,\n2009). To some extent, the later success rate of MBBS graduates reflects whether China’s MBBS education meets\ninternational standards (Mao et al., 2012). In this respect, the results to date have been sub-standard when compared to\nthose in several countries. For example, China has the most Indian students compared to other countries, who are required\nto pass the Foreign Medical Graduate Examination (FMGE) to practice medicine in India (Muthyanolla, 2022). From\n2015 to 2020, the total number of students sitting the FMGE from China was 43,632, but the average passing rate was\nonly 12.51% (Muthyanolla, 2022). Similarly, the passing rate of Nepalese students from China in Nepal Medical Council\n(NMC) Examination from 2016-2018 was only 34.3%, behind that of Nepalese graduates from Nepal (80.3%),\nPhilippines (74.8%), Bangladesh (60.9%), Pakistan (58.4%), India (49.2%), Russia (44.6%), and Ukraine (40.9%)\n(Aryal, 2018). These results indicate that the majority of China-educated MBBS graduates do not have the expected level\nof professional knowledge and skills.\nRecent studies, while acknowledging the accomplishments of medical education for international students in China, have\npointed out there are still several problems to be addressed. These problems are mainly identified as: lack of a unified\ntraining program for international medical students (Liu et al., 2021b), language barriers between lecturers and students\n(Ding, 2016; Huang, 2019), ineffective teaching pedagogy (Yang et al., 2019a), insufficient well-qualified and\nexperienced academics, and poor teaching resources (Ding, 2016; Huang, 2019; Wang et al., 2018), absence of robust\nquality assurance system (Liang & Liu, 2019), classroom discipline issues (Zeng & Sheng, 2019), and poor student\nquality (Ma et al., 2018; Huang, 2019). It should be noted that many of these studies were based on the authors’ own\nworking experience and subjective reflection, so lacked empirical evidence. A few Chinese studies adopted a more\nobjective quantitative method by surveying international students’ opinions, but the views of academic staff were rarely\nsolicited. The study reported here was designed to address this important issue by interviewing academics.\nTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in China delving into the beliefs of academic staff concerning\npedagogical factors that influence success of international medical students. The findings may contribute to a better\nunderstanding of challenges faced by international medical students in China, and offer a direction for improving the\n\n\nquality of teaching in these universities. This is important for ensuring that graduates have the necessary expertise—and is\nvery important for the reputation of the universities.\nMethods\nEthics and consent\nThis study was approved by Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (XZMU20200028) and Institutional\nReview Board of Nanjing Medical University (NJMUIRB774) in [September] 2020. All participants gave written and\noral informed consent to participate in the study.\nResearch sites\nThe study was performed in two medical universities (A and B) located in Jiangsu Province, eastern China. Both\nuniversities had over 15 years’ experience in teaching international MBBS students, so it was reasonable to regard them as\nrelevant sites from which to gather perspectives of academics engaged in teaching. A similar MBBS curriculum was\nadopted at these two universities, in line with the requirement by the MOE. MBBS students and Chinese students took\nseparate courses and did not mix in the classroom. The courses for international students were taught principally through\nthe English medium. The majority of their international students were from developing countries in Asia and Africa, and\nsome students were far from proficient in English.\nParticipants\nPurposive sampling was adopted in this study. Criteria for selecting interviewees were: academic titles, years of teaching,\ngender, and course taught. The aim was to obtain a heterogeneous sample from the medical teaching body at the two\nuniversities. Academics were approached individually by phone call and WeChat invitation with the help of two\n“gatekeepers”. The target sample size before recruitment was around 30, but had to be increased until data saturation\nwas reached (Morse, 1991). A total of 40 academics were approached, but 2 of them declined the interview invitation and\nanother 2 initially agreed but later withdrew by not responding to the researcher’s text messages.\nIn total, 36 academics (17 males, 19 females) were recruited, including 2 teaching Chinese, 3 teaching nature science,\n11 teaching clinical subjects, and 20 basic medical science. The participants had between 2 to 13+ years of experience teaching\ninternational students; and all were responsible for teaching the same subject to both Chinese and international students.\nData collection\nSemi-structured interviews were conducted with each participant from November 2021 to January 2022. Only the first\nauthor and participants were present for each individual interview. Academics from University A participated in face-toto-face interviews in their office or a small meeting room, where it was perceived to be comfortable and quiet, while\nonline interviews were conducted with academics from University B (due to the coronavirus pandemic). Both face-toface and online interviews were audio-recorded. Field notes were taken during the interviews. The interview questions,\nwhich were pilot-tested, addressed participants’ views on factors affecting student success, and their recommendations\nfor improving teaching quality at classroom level and organizational level.\nBefore the interview, academics were debriefed concerning the purpose and significance of the study, and the likely\nlength of the interview. Interviews were conducted in a quiet and comfortable environment. In conformity with ethical\nrequirements, all interviewees were assured of anonymity and agreed to audio recording and dissemination of findings.\nEach interview, conducted in Chinese, lasted for around 30 to 70 minutes.\nData analysis\nA thematic approach was used to analyze data (Braun & Clarke, 2006), with recorded audios later transcribed verbatim in\nChinese. The Chinese transcripts were coded manually by the primary researcher, who immersed herself in the data and\nnoted down her initial ideas on emergent themes. Initial codes were generated based on identified key words and phrases.\nNext, the codes were summarized into potential themes for each participant. The primary researcher translated the codes\nand themes into English. Then the researcher team discussed, adjusted and reached a consensus on final relevant\ncategories. Trustworthiness was guaranteed by member checking, peer auditing and self-reflection. During the whole\nresearch process, the primary researcher kept a self-reflective journal to minimize the effect of her own background and\nwork experience on the interpretation of the data.\nResults\nTheme 1: Teaching pedagogy\nAn overwhelming majority (86%) of academics perceived their teaching methods and practices to be a major influential\nfactor on international students’ learning. They perceived a mismatch between their teaching approach and the learning\n\n\ncharacteristics of students. Most academics still relied on traditional teacher-centered lecturing, and tended to read\nverbatim from slides without properly explaining further or adding practical examples that could enhance the students’\ncomprehension of the subject. Some academics had noticed that lectures as a learning approach may not be suitable to\ninternational students’ learning needs. One academic teaching clinical medicine commented that in a lecture on clinical\nmanifestations and diagnosis of diseases, the students were more concerned about the treatment and recovery from\ndiseases. The academic suggested:\nStudents enjoyed learning about clinical treatments because they may think that it would be useful for their clinic\nwork in the future. However, treatment is only briefly introduced in this class.\n(Participant 25)\nUnlike Chinese students, international students asked more questions and would interrupt the class to express opinions.\nThis made the academics think more about how to communicate with them, and how to improve teaching quality. The\nmajority of academics felt that much of the current teaching was spoon-feeding information and lacking interaction.\nLecturers were failing to grasp students’ attention and hold their interest in class, which often affected their exam\nperformance. Interviewees felt that teaching innovations such as case-based learning (CBL) and problem-based learning\n(PBL) should be used to teach international students, because this type of teaching mode was more heuristic, which would\nencourage students to learn more actively. They suggested CBL and PBL would increase the involvement of international\nstudents in learning activities. They could also make more frequent use of individual and group presentations and\ndiscussions, encourage asking questions, guiding students to think, and trying flipped class teaching (setting preparation\nassignments on a lecture topic before presenting the lecture). However, academics also felt that implementing these\nchanges would be a challenge for them, because it would place more demands on their language ability, subject\nknowledge, ability to listen and respond to students, and control the class. It was pleasing to note that one of the few\nacademics who already involved international students in interactive class learning activities reported positive changes in\nperformance of these students:\nInternational students are not interested in the traditional lecturing we use to teach Chinese students. Very few\nstudents listen carefully in class. I usually ask international students to summarize the content of the previous\nlesson in front of the whole class. They would become very active to participate and prepare well. I think\ninternational students like to show themselves in public. If I take advantage of this characteristic of theirs, I can\nstimulate their passion for learning.\n(Participant 31)\nCompared to Chinese students, international students were relatively weaker in basic knowledge and learning. Academics acknowledged the diverse academic abilities of international students and their different culture backgrounds and\nagreed that teaching approaches should be adjusted to these student characteristics. They also believed that it would be\nimportant for them to give culture-relevant examples in class that students were familiar with, make timely summaries of\nkey knowledge points, give practice assignments after class, cover exam points, and provide timely feedback on\nassignments. An academic teaching radiology gave the example below:\nFor international students, you have to start from the basics. For example, in my [radiology] class, there is a CT\nvalue, high or low. High is bright, low is dark. You have to explain this foundation setting first; otherwise, they\nwould become confused. Doing this sort of thing could help them, but would require academics to take more time\nto prepare for class.\n(Participant 19)\nInterviewees reported that real clinical cases needed to be integrated into basic medicine lectures, to help international\nstudents understand them better. They added that international students learn better with case examples. Academics\nwithout deep clinical experience but who are teaching biochemistry, microbiology, immunology, and parasitology found\nthis to be a challenge. They suggested it was necessary for them to provide more than theory and facts by including\nrelevant clinical knowledge to facilitate the learning of their students. An academic teaching biochemistry, with a\nbackground in biology, stated in this regard:\nStudents have asked me some clinical questions, which shows that they have this demand. This demand actually\nreminds me that I should include clinical knowledge in my teaching. This could make my class more attractive and\nmake them realize that my course is very helpful to their clinical study in the future and can help solve clinical\n\n\n\nproblems. In addition, it can make students realize the importance of my course, at least for now, and improve their\nlearning enthusiasm. I’ve already started learning relevant clinical knowledge.\n(Participant 6)\nAcademics also stated that their lecture slides probably need to be more interesting to attract students’ attention. The\nacademics stated that there may be a need to use different teaching aids beyond writing on blackboard—such as videos,\naudios, and animations to make the classes more dynamic and understandable for students. An academic teaching\npharmacology mentioned how he utilized online technology to make the learning in his class more effective.\nI like to go to YouTube to find some videos about the mechanism of drugs to show in the class. Maybe my\nexplanations are not very good, but the videos explain very well, which can help deepen students’ understanding.\nMoreover, I feel that playing videos when students are sleepy can increase their attention.\n(Participant 22)\nTheme 2: Language barriers\nAfter teaching pedagogy, the language barrier was identified as a significant challenge in improving the learning of\ninternational students. In discussion, 81% of the interviewees indicated that language differences and difficulties are\nnegatively influencing teaching and learning quality. They also offered potential solutions. These Chinese academics\nspoke English as a foreign language, while international students spoke English as their first or second language (albeit far\nfrom perfectly). This is not a harsh criticism of either group, but merely highlights the actual situation. Mutual\ncommunication was constrained because the academics were not necessarily fluent in English, and the students were\nnot necessarily fluent either. Academics found it difficult to understand students’ English accents and expressions, which\nmay not be standard American or British English, particularly with students from India, Nepal, and Pakistan.\nAcademics revealed that while they could generally command medical English for in their own field after years of\nteaching, they still struggled with daily-life English and cross-disciplinary English. Limited English vocabulary outside\ntheir field affected their teaching, and was one of the main reasons why they often resorted to reading verbatim from slides\nrather than explaining concepts in their own words, and why they avoided engaging interactively with students.\nConsequently, students become bored during class, and sometimes were annoyed by poor communication. An academic\nteaching surgery compared teaching Chinese and international students concerning language in theory lectures.\nIf it is a Chinese class, the academic has many methods to teach. He has eloquence and rich experiences; he can\nsolicit and quote from others when giving lectures. However, in case of teaching foreign students, due to the\nlanguage barrier, academics cannot express themselves 100% smoothly. They can only communicate with\nstudents in the simplest way, so they cannot always respond appropriately to students. If they cannot attract\nstudents, students will lack interest in the class. Naturally, they do not devote themselves to learning.\n(Participant 35)\nAcademics teaching clinical medicine who were also responsible for clerkship and internship at hospitals, indicated that it\nwas hard to translate some Chinese words concerning a patient’s symptoms to English for the benefit of the students.\nSometimes the translation made by an academic would not exactly match the symptoms. This situation was further\nexacerbated when international students could not comprehend explanations delivered in daily-life Chinese. Therefore,\nthe effectiveness of clerkship and internship learning was adversely affected because of language barriers, as this\nacademic teaching internal medicine explains:\nTake the symptoms of angina pectoris for example. When I talk about angina pectoris to Chinese interns, I will use\nXuzhou dialect, which is very easy to understand. However, it is difficult for me to convey the clinical symptoms\nreflected by most patients to foreign students through a more appropriate word. Because the pain reflected by the\npatient is not the oppressive feeling mentioned in the textbook. We cannot simply transfer the knowledge from\nbooks to students, but we have to combine it with our clinical practice. However, sometimes, it is very difficult to\ntranslate properly.\n(Participant 25)\nTheme 3: Teaching resources management\nOver half of the interviewees (58%) stated that issues with teaching resources also affected teaching and learning. Four\nsub-issues emerged as part of this theme.\n\n\n\n3.1 Demand of textbook and teaching materials\nTwelve academics complained that they lacked easy access to necessary textbooks, English question banks, and other\nrelevant teaching materials such as reference books and videos. Universities did not mandate or provide any particular\ntextbook for academics to use, and academics had to search for textbooks, or create their own materials by drawing on\ndifferent English and Chinese sources. When the textbook of the academic was different from students’ own books, they\ncould have difficulty understanding lectures. Sometimes, students were not assigned any textbook or relevant learning\nmaterials. Moreover, academics responsible for different modules or units in a course would often use different textbooks,\nwhich led to inconsistencies. Academics felt it is necessary for experts to develop good textbooks to help students\nunderstand and review lecture knowledge; and they suggested universities should purchase specialized English books,\nmedical videos, and question banks for use by staff and students. One academic teaching gynecology commented on her\ndifficulty with textbooks:\nOur Clinical College once gave us some textbooks, but I think the versions of those books are relatively old. I have\nnot seen updated books later. In those books, old methods of screening gynecological cervicitis, which were used\none or two decades ago, were even introduced.\n(Participant 27)\n3.2 Need for collaborative lesson planning\nSeven academics felt that it was important to engage in collective lesson planning for intellectual communication. This is\nlacking at present, but may be an important procedure for a teaching team to share experiences and discuss which\nknowledge points are the most important for students to master. In these planning sessions, application of flipped class\nteaching could be debated, and how to properly create synergies among different topics taught by different academics.\nMoreover, with collective lesson planning, new academics could be given a demonstration on how to teach international\nstudents by more experienced academics. Academics inexperienced in teaching international students expressed their\nneeds to receive teaching tips on pedagogies, and have feedback from more experienced lecturers. However, in most\ndepartments, this collective lesson planning and guidance was lacking, particularly clinical medicine subjects because the\nteachers were busy with their own clinical and research work:\nTheoretically, there should be collective lesson planning, but it cannot be done in practice … Teachers have their\nown understandings of knowledge and lecturing methods, and collective lesson preparation has little impact on\nthem … Basically, teaching has been weakened and become a mere formality. There is no teaching demonstration\nfor new teachers any more. The supervision mechanism has gradually disappeared. Now the overall orientation no\nlonger takes teaching as the main evaluation index, but mainly takes SCI publications.\n(Participant 24)\n3.3 Weak teaching supervision system\nSeven academics reported that universities lacked a sound supervision system for teaching quality, so academics might\nwell conclude that it makes no difference if one teaches well or not. Without a system that has rewards for good\nperformance and penalties for poor performance, academics would have no incentive to teach well. Strengthening\nsupervision of teaching by including regular observation by experts and spot checking of lecture quality was something\nthat some academics desired.\nThe School of International Education needs to establish a system or assessment standard, and implement rewards\nand punishments in each teaching and research department. If there is no difference between good and bad\nteaching, teachers will not be motivated.\n(Participant 33)\n3.4 Inadequate clinical teaching resources\nFive senior clinical academics mentioned there was a shortage of well-qualified persons to teach theory, clerkship and\nmentoring internships in hospitals. In some departments, due to high turnover of staff, there was lack of a stable\nteaching team responsible for international students. Recently hired academics and new doctors who had minimal or\nno prior teaching experience were asked to teach these students. Their teaching would be limited to reading their notes\nor slides aloud, without explanation or discussion. Academics with teaching experience hoped universities and\nhospitals would attach more importance to consistency in teaching international students, and to introduce incentives\n\n\n\nto encourage young, qualified doctors to participate in teaching. There was a perceived need to train new academics\nfor future teaching work:\nDuring the clerkship, we had two teachers to teach 25 international students. The venue space and teachers were\nnot enough … Internship was more like clerkship learning just for a visit in the case of foreign interns, because\nsome teachers could not speak English and they were very busy. Foreign interns could not operate due to doctorpatient relationship concern. They could not receive much guidance … You know, this is a problem.\n(Participant 24)\nThree academics also mentioned that their course syllabus was obsolete. A senior academic who teaches pediatrics said it\nwas imperative to discuss with international students to know which diseases they needed to learn about for work in their\nown country. The course syllabus could then be updated accordingly. This would make it easier for students to relate to\nthe content being taught in class. In this regard, academics suggested their departments should evaluate their existing\nsyllabus, and improve it where necessary by making it more relevant.\n\nTheme 4: Attributes of academics\nApproximately 50% of interviewees disclosed that the attributes of academics, such as their dedication, passion for\nteaching, attitude, sense of responsibility, and subject knowledge played a pivotal role in ensuring teaching quality that\nhelps students learn. The academics interviewed commented that much more time and effort is needed to prepare lessons\nfor international students because of the modifications required. Some perceived the workload of preparing a lesson in\nEnglish was three to ten times greater than preparing a lesson for Chinese students. For instance, they would have to write\na lesson script beforehand to read in aloud in class, because it was too challenging to improvise their English during the\nsession. This type of language barrier would not occur in the Chinese class.\nThe interviewees admitted that many academics were not motivated to engage in teaching international students because\nuniversities prioritized research over teaching. There were no incentives for academics to devote time and effort to areas\nthat would not advance their careers. Therefore, they felt more inspired to do research and were much less driven to teach\ninternational students, even though they were qualified. Moreover, some academics might become apathetic about\nteaching, which would affect the quality of their contribution. A typical observation was:\nAll materials for lesson preparation must be in English. It is already very difficult to prepare lessons in Chinese. I\nhave to prepare lessons in English and practice my manuscript … In this current university environment, which\ngenerally attaches importance to scientific research rather than teaching, everyone is willing to focus on their own\nresearch rather than pay attention to teaching. Research awards are more tempting… When it comes to\nprofessional rank evaluation, papers are more useful than a teaching competition award.\n(Participant 3)\nDespite the common perception of heavy workload, challenges, and dissatisfaction with a lack of incentives, some\nacademics maintained a personal commitment to their teaching out of a sense of responsibility and self-improvement.\nTo maintain the enthusiasm of academics in teaching, it is not about monetary stimulation, but a sense of value and\nhonor. Academics are not unsentimental people … Many academics are dissatisfied, but they continue to teach\neven if they are not satisfied. This is dedication, awareness and sentiment … I don’t want to talk about interests, but\nthe school should give me some other aspects that emotionally make me feel honored to teach international\nstudents.\n(Participant 4)\nWhen asked about what incentives universities could provide to motivate academics, the interviewees claimed that\ninstead of placing MBBS education in a peripheral position, university management should attach more importance to\nMBBS education and to the academics who provide it. They expressed strong need for continuous professional learning\nto improve pedagogies, and assistance with oral English through formal or informal learning opportunities at home or\nabroad. Several academics claimed that a university should count their teaching performance as part of any evaluation for\npromotion, rather than solely basing it on number of publications. This would ensure that academics would become more\nmotivated and enthusiastic to teach. A few academics also desired funding opportunities to implement teaching\ninnovations in the MBBS program, but it was not possible. Two academics hoped the university could count their class\nhours as double or three times more than Chinese class hours, because they spent much more time on lesson preparation\nand had a need of class hours for academic promotion.\n\n\n\nTheme 5: Teacher supervision and guidance\nWhen asked what academics could do to help students improve their academic performance, nearly 50% of the\ninterviewees indicated that there was a need for them monitor their students’ progress more closely, and to push\nunderperforming students harder to be responsible for their study. Students could be lazy and lack self-discipline.\nAcademics felt that if they could devote more time into supervising low-achieving students and talking with them after\nclass to encourage them, the students may feel they are being cared about and be more motivated to study. An academic\nteaching cell biology highlighted the importance of encouraging and caring about students:\nStudents are still children. If academics communicate with them more, they will feel pressure and study under\npressure … In the first year of my teaching, I dedicated all my energy to push them. I tried my best to memorize all\nthe students' names and faces. They felt my care. Several students still have been in contact with me since\ngraduation … I was busy with my own life and family, but if I had spent more time at that time, I could have saved a\nfew more students from failing.\n(Participant 21)\nMoreover, six academics felt it was not only important to impart knowledge and skills to students, but also moral and\nethical education, values and responsibility in class. They stated that it was critical to educate students to cherish learning\nopportunities in China, correct their learning attitude, and be responsible for medicine. By doing so, students’ intrinsic\nmotivation could be stimulated with a sense of respect and responsibility, as stated by an academic teaching anatomy:\nIn anatomy class, the specimens are donated by others. No matter the country in the world, the donors are the\nworthiest of respect. As a medical student, you should respect them and patients, because they, ordinary people,\nhave given us the opportunity to learn and explore medicine. You should treat patients well in the future. Why did\nthey donate their bodies? Because they hoped you could become a good doctor.\n(Participant 28)\nTheme 6: Teacher-student rapport\nApproximately 31% of the interviewees mentioned that getting to know about students and their cultures and establishing\ngood teacher-student rapport would be conducive to better communication and teaching. In particular, communication\nbetween academics and students could help build emotional connections and understanding in class. For example,\nwithout knowledge of students’ lives and cultures, any academic may accidentally say culturally insensitive statements\nthat can cause discomfort. An academic teaching orthopedics with more than ten years’ teaching experience with\ninternational students shared how he successfully developed a good relationship with students by incorporating students’\ncultures into class.\nOnce, as soon as I finished watching the Indian film Dangal, I quoted 10 classic sentences into my slides to\nmotivate my students in class. They became very interested when they saw these quotes. “Wow, this is our Indian\nmovie.” Sometimes, I talk about my feelings about Nepalese festivals in class. They feel excited and think the\nteacher acknowledges their culture… If students like you, they will learn your course well.\n(Participant 18)\nAn academic teaching anesthesiology mentioned that academics teaching clinical subjects generally knew less about their\nstudents, and thus they failed to establish good rapport. This made their lectures seem rigid and lacking in attention to the\nstudents as individuals. The academics teaching clinical medicine suggested that universities should arrange activities to\nincrease communication between staff and students. This is particularly important for younger academics.\n\nTheme 7: Detachment of teaching content from license exams\nOver a quarter (28%) of the interviewees pointed out that one of the primary reasons why many international students lack\ninterest in learning is probably because the current course content and resources do not correspond closely enough to the\ndemands made in the license exams. Academics usually teach the knowledge that they think is important, and this may not\nprecisely match a curriculum based on the license exam. What is required is a blending of both academics’ specialist\nknowledge and a prescribed curriculum. This issue was critical, because students would be more motivated to learn things\nif they perceived them to be useful to them in passing examinations, and in their working life. However, some academics\nargued that it would be difficult to address license exam needs of students from diverse countries, where exam content\nmay vary. In this situation, it seemed essential to develop a more contextualized curriculum, as explained by an academic\nteaching anatomy:\n\n\n\nOne of the key points of our teaching is to help students pass license examination. If a student does not pass the\nexam, he/she will not even qualify for matching hospitals. This is true of both Chinese students and foreign\nstudents. Teaching and license examination cannot be separated. If we want to help students pass the license exam,\nteaching activities should play a guiding role … The passing rate of license exam is the most important measure\nstandard of a medical university. Practice is the sole criterion for testing truth.\n(Participant 2)\nTheme 8: Classroom discipline management\nChinese academics felt frustrated and perplexed by international students’ certain behaviors in class. International\nstudents were often late for class, left class in the middle for no reasons, listened to music, used smartphones, or would\nchat with others and made noise. These behaviors negatively influenced the mood and positivity of academics towards\ninternational students, as by comparison such discipline problems would rarely occur in Chinese students’ class. Seven\nacademics (19%) stated that it might be students’ own cultural habits, but they should follow Chinese rules. These\nacademics dedicated certain time to disciplining students in class to make students more serious about study. However,\nmore academics emphasized that it was the student administrators’ responsibility to strengthen student discipline\nmanagement. One academic teaching hygiene explained how she managed class discipline to push students learn.\nIn sharp contrast to Chinese students, foreign students pay too little attention to class discipline… For example, for\nmorning class at 8 a.m., some students cannot come until 9 a.m. I wondered why. Later, I went to Pakistan for a\nvisit. When I went to a university, I understood that this was their habit. However, in China, if you want to obtain a\nChinese degree, you have to follow Chinese rules … I count the check-in and lateness into class participation score,\nand I will call the roll five minutes in advance. Sometimes I close the back door in class for fear that they might\nsneak out. Sometimes I put roll call on the second quarter of class to prevent someone from sneaking out after\nsigning in … It is like fighting a battle of wits and courage with them.\n(Participant 30)\nTheme 9: Assessment styles\nSix academics (17%) stated that the current assessment system was problematic, mentioning the exam evaluation system\nand the final elimination system.\n9.1 Exam evaluation system\nThere is no formative assessment but only a summative assessment based on final exam performance. Academics\nsuggested that the university add formative assessments such as a mid-term exam or in-class quizzes, because it could\nhelp academics to follow students’ progress and difficulties, and push students to study harder, and decrease the final\nexam pressure on students.\nOne academic teaching pathophysiology mentioned that the evaluation of international students’ academic performance should\ntake into account of their characteristics rather strictly follow the same evaluation system of Chinese students. This academic felt\nthat international students were more interested in practical learning in the laboratory and generally outperformed Chinese\nstudents, so the university could consider increasing the weight of practical score into their final course score.\nAcademics also mentioned they wanted to change the assessment methods, but in most cases, it was beyond their control\nunless the changes were approved at the organizational level after going through complicated application procedures.\nOne academic teaching parasitology exemplified how their department improved their assessment method based on\nstudents’ feedback.\nStudents came to complain that the exam was unfair because some students did not attend class, just memorized\nkey points the week before the exam and got higher marks. After receiving their feedback, we improved. We took\ninto their formative performance as a part, and the final exam accounted for only 50%. Answering questions and\nsigning in to class are all part of the formative performance. I think we should add formative assessment and tell\nstudents to listen to the whole class. Our purpose is not to let them fail in the exam, but to guide them to learn\nsomething. Students understand this. Finally, almost all students passed my course.\n(Participant 29)\n9.2 Final elimination system\nSeven academics pointed out university assessment and evaluation systems were less strict with international students. It\nwas perceived as a loose entry and exit to the course, which would have no pressure on students to study. These academics\n\n\nsuggested the university should implement a final elimination system to delay graduation or fail those who are\ndisqualified for graduation and a MBBS degree.\nThe university must strictly enforce the examination system and cannot deliberately make exceptions in\ninternational students’ favor. Schools should dare to weed out unqualified students, so that they will be afraid.\nOnce they are afraid, most of them will study hard.\n(Participant 10)\nDiscussion\nFrom the standpoint of academics, this study revealed critical factors that are worthy of attention with respect to academic\nsuccess of international students in China. Nine key themes emerged covering challenges that include teaching pedagogy,\nEnglish language, teaching resources management, teacher attributes, teacher supervision and guidance, teacher-student\nrapport, detachment of teaching content from license exams, class discipline management, and assessment styles.\nIt is evident that teaching methods and language barrier were the most frequently reported factors that impacted Chinese\nacademics’ teaching effectiveness and international medical students’ academic success. This finding is in keeping with\nthat reported in the systematic review by Dang et al., (2021), in that these are the most common challenges facing Chinese\nacademics in English-medium-instruction (EMI) programs. The finding of teaching method as the most dominant factor\nperceived relevant to student academic success, is aligned with the findings of Helm and Guarda (2015). They reported\nthat in an Italian university, most lecturers in programs where English is the medium instruction needed to improve their\nteaching competences. The literature also indicates that the pedagogical abilities of academics seem to be greatly\ninfluenced by language barriers (Ding, 2016; Huang, 2019; Zhan, 2017). Yang et al. (2019b) found EMI teachers in a\nChinese university, more than non-EMI teachers, became more didactic and less interactive in class, which then hindered\nteaching and learning. Despite this, improved English skill does not necessarily equate to improved pedagogical changes\n(Dafouz, 2018; Dang et al., 2021; Helm & Guarda, 2015). The finding of this study suggests that while it is important for\nthe university to address the oral English abilities of academics in both English related to a specific discipline and English\nin daily life, it is more imperative to focus on improving the pedagogy of these academics as key to academic success of\ninternational medical students. Universities should offer ongoing professional learning in teaching approaches such as\nPBL and CBL for pre-service and in-service academics, taking full consideration of the needs, ability and interests of\ninternational medical students (Liu et al., 2021b; Saleh et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2019a). Meanwhile, universities should\nstrongly encourage and support teaching innovations with sufficient funding.\nThis study uncovers that limited access to teaching resources largely inhibits teaching and learning. The lack of uniform\nEnglish textbooks and relevant teaching materials for international students has also been identified in many universities\nin China (Huang; 2019; Jiang et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2019a; Zeng & Sheng, 2019), and in other developing countries,\nsuch as in a Ukrainian university where EMI programs were taught to domestic students (Goodman, 2014). It is\nacknowledged that textbooks are still the main carrier of subject content, therefore, the selection and use of textbooks will\ndirectly affect the teaching and students’ knowledge attainment (Li & Cui, 2010; Li et al. 2013). Compared with the rich\ncurriculum integrated teaching resources in developed countries in Europe and the United States, China still lacks a set of\nEnglish medical teaching materials with complete independent intellectual property rights and in line with the\ncharacteristics of its medical education in terms of pertinence and adaptability (Wang et al., 2018). The dire shortage\nof English teaching resources has limited the sustainable development of medical education for international students in\nChina (Wang et al., 2016). Although some Chinese universities have started to write teaching textbooks on their own, and\nhave made progress, there are also some problems. For example, the English level of Chinese teachers is uneven, and the\nwriting style cannot be unified (Wang et al., 2018).\nIn alignment with existing literature (Ding, 2016; Huang 2019; Yang et al., 2019a; Zeng & Sheng, 2019), the findings of\nthis study show that lack of collaborative lesson planning for MBBS classes, shortage of clinical teaching resources, and\nweakened supervision mechanisms are prevailing in Chinese universities. With regard to the issue of perceived obsolete\nsyllabus, Zhou (2016) shows that Tianjin Medical University has added relevant teaching content according to changes of\nthe disease spectrum in the country of origin. For instance, contents such as Schistosoma aegypti and Schistosoma\nmansoni which are prevalent in Africa were added to the course of Human Parasites. The case of Tianjin Medical\nUniversity implies that organizational support plays a critical role in guaranteeing the quality of MBBS education.\nBlömeke et al. (2016) found the quality of academics in terms of their experience, education background, motivations,\nprofessional knowledge and self-efficacy to predict student achievement. Similarly, the finding of this study suggests that\nthe attributes of academics such as passion for teaching, professional responsibility, dedication, attitude, and content\nknowledge are perceived to have an effect on teaching quality. As indicated by the interviewees, academics are often\n\n\n\ndemotivated to teach international students because of a lack of emotional, material, and career incentives. Lacking\nincentives seems a prevalent phenomenon, as reported by other universities in different provinces in China—to name a\nfew, Liaoning Medical College (Ding, 2016), Three Gorges University (Zeng & Sheng, 2019), and Zhengzhou\nUniversity (Zhang, 2016). This problem was also found in a study in Sweden (Stenfors-Hayes, 2010). This suggests\nthat university policy makers need to be attentive to the opinions and needs of academics if they aim to formulate an\nappropriate reward system that can promote teaching quality for student success.\nThe finding concerning supervision and guidance for students and its effect on student success is new to the literature.\nChinese academics subconsciously assumed a parental role to discipline international students, which implies their\ncommitment to education. Moreover, moral and ethical education for international students during and outside lectures\nhas been recognized in Chinese studies (Liu et al., 2021a; Wang & Huang, 2019). This may be more relevant to a Chinese\ncontext because of the influence of Confucian ideology in Chinese education that academics should not only impart\nknowledge and skills but also bear an obligation to cultivate the mind (Ye, 2001).\nSimilar to the findings of previous studies in China (Yang & Lei, 2021; Zhao, 2021) and Iraq (Saleh et al., 2012), teacherstudent rapport is an overlooked dimension in MBBS teaching. Lack of good teacher-student rapport, due to differences\nin language, cultural background, and religious beliefs, could make the dynamics within a classroom dull and rigid (Yang\n& Lei, 2021). Building a good relationship between academics and their students could help foster a classroom\nenvironment that is favorable to learning. This requires academics to learn more about the life and learning characteristics\nof international students, and to communicate with them more after class to establish a harmonious and supportive\nteacher-student relationship.\nThe issue of teaching course content that is detached from license exam requirements has been recognized in some\nChinese studies (Han & Guo, 2009; Huang, 2019). The universities with more capabilities have taken action to integrate\nlicense exam content in their teaching. For example, the MBBS curriculum at Zhejiang University is internationalized by\nreference to UK medical education standards and clinical executive standards in Tomorrow’s Doctors (Fan et al., 2020),\nUnited States Medical Licensing Examination, and Professional and Linguistic Assessment Board. Li and Cui (2010)\nfrom Dalian Medical University suggest introducing license exam tutoring by inviting foreign academics to teach\nChinese academics by studying exam questions and content; but this was seen as separate from regular teaching. This\ndifference between the two cases implies that university staff should certainly prepare students for license exams, but also\nshape the curriculum to include topics in curricula tested in license exams (e.g. FMGE).\nClassroom disciplinary issues with international students are common and can negatively influence the lecturing style of\nacademics. This was also identified in other studies (Jin et al., 2009; Zeng & Sheng, 2019). Some academics take\ndisciplinary action with problem students while others ignore the behavior but feel uncomfortable with the lack of control.\nIn a survey of learning experience in a medical university in Southwest China, half the MBBS students felt academics\nwere not strict enough with class discipline (Yang & Lei, 2021), probably because of language barriers. Yet, the finding of\nthis study suggests that academics should try to ensure good disciplinary management for the sake of well-behaved\nstudents. Action needs to be taken with misbehaving students to focus their attention on attendance and listening during\nlectures.\nThe finding on the effect of assessment style on international medical students’ academic achievement has scarcely been\ndiscussed in the literature. In one study conducted in Ningxia Medical University, Zhang et al. (2020) focus on how the\nimplementation of formative assessment in their MBBS microbiology course helped improve class attendance and\nlearning attitude, develop creativity and self-directed learning habits, increase teacher-student communication, and make\nevaluation fairer and more objective. This case shows the advantages of revising assessment styles. Notably, Zhao et al.\n(2020) claim that the current assessment of students in medical colleges and universities in China is generally based on\nsummative assessment (at the end of the course), and that there is a lack of understanding of and attention to formative\n(ongoing) assessment and evaluation during the course. Summative assessment is seen by the interviewed academics as a\nrelatively single and weak assessment method that could reduce students’ enthusiasm for learning. Assessment only at the\nend of a course makes it difficult to monitor progress in students’ growth in professional knowledge (Zhan, 2017).\nTherefore, it is important for universities to transform assessment styles appropriately to help international medical\nstudents learn.\nIn congruence with other studies (Wang, 2013; Zhang & Hu, 2019), it was noted that there was often a tendency in\nChinese universities to graduate international students by lowering exam difficulty or pass mark, or modifying the\nstudents’ marks to help them graduate. These behaviors not only severely undermine student quality but also affect the\ninternational reputation and status of Chinese higher education. There needs to be a system that deliberately holds back\nany student who is not mastering the knowledge and skills taught in the MBBS program.\n\n\nConclusion and limitations\nThe findings of this study show that teaching-related factors play a critical role in influencing international medical\nstudents’ academic success. The problems identified here, when associated with other literature, suggest that the same\nproblems are prevalent across other Chinese universities that host international medical students. It seems that although\nChina MOE attaches great importance to development of MBBS education quality, there are still many issues in\nimplementing its policies at the university level. These may be related to the importance and support each university\nattaches to the MBBS program, as well its own capabilities to teach the program effectively. The findings highlight that\nuniversity policy-makers should devote more attention to the quality and development of MBBS education. Academics\nare the implementers of internationalization of education and they are the developers of educational resources (Ding,\n2016). Universities have the responsibility to support and meet the needs of academics in terms of professional learning\nand in provision of incentives, as well as being attentive to their recommendations for how to improve teaching quality.\nEfforts at individual level, department level, and university level should be jointly made to ensure high teaching quality,\nwhich is the key factor to promote student academic success.\nThis study was conducted at two sites, but caution with generalization of the findings is needed. They may be relevant to\nother Chinese universities offering courses to international students, but not to other settings. In future studies, this\nresearch will be extended to investigate the perceptions of academics on the salient student-related factors that may affect\ntheir academic success.\nData availability\nUnderlying data\nThe datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to privacy agreements\nbetween the researchers and the interviewees. The selection of\ninformants was based on the subject they taught and their gender,\nexperience of teaching international medical students, educational\nbackground and career stage. Semi-structured interviews were\nconducted with academics (N = 36) between November 2020 and\nJanuary 2021 at two medical universities in China. Each interview\nlasted between 30 and 70 minutes and was audiotaped, transcribed\nverbatim and thematically analysed.\nResults\nThe teaching factors that academics perceived to have an important\ninfluence on the success of students were (i) pedagogy and content\nalignment; (ii) language barriers; (iii) resource management and the\n\n\flearning environment; and (iv) educator attributes and guidance.\nConclusions\nUniversity faculties and departments involved in teaching\ninternational medical students should ensure that their academic staff\nare supported in their ongoing professional development and\nprovided with resources to enhance their teaching quality. The four\nfactors (and their sub-factors) listed above should be prioritised in\nsuch staff training.\n\n\nIntroduction\nWith the internationalisation of China’s universities over the past two decades (Dang et al., 2023; Jin & Horta, 2018),\nmedicine has emerged as the most popular programme, after the Chinese language, for international students to pursue\n(Zhou, 2016). The Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) programme, taught in English, is designed\nand implemented by universities with the support and supervision of China’s Ministry of Education (MOE) (Huang,\n2014). An example is the document Quality Control Standards of Basic Medical Education (in English Medium) for\nInternational Students in China, which was published in 2020 by the China Education Association for International\nExchange and the International Medical Education Committee, with the support of the International Department of the\nMOE. This document was aimed at further standardising MBBS education across Chinese universities and effectively\nimproving training quality, thus promoting its sustainable development.\nThe MOE has authorised 45 universities to recruit international medical students (Xie, 2021). The World Health\nOrganization (WHO) recognises these universities in its Directory of World Medical Schools, which means that\ninternational graduates holding a Chinese MBBS degree are eligible to attend licence exams in their home countries.\nExamples include the Medical Council of India Screening Test, the United States Medical Licensing Examination, and\nthe UK Professional and Linguistic Assessment Board. Students regard this as one of the greatest advantages of studying\nMBBS in China, in addition to other benefits such as less demanding admission requirements, lower costs, and a safe and\nstable society (Zhou, 2016).\nThe MBBS programme typically takes six years to complete, with five years devoted to courses in natural science, the\nChinese language, basic science medicine, and clinical subjects, and a one-year internship in a Chinese hospital or a\nhospital in the student’s home country. Most MBBS students are from low-income Asian and African countries, where\nthe demand for health professionals is high (Liu et al., 2017). The majority choose to return home to establish their careers\nafter completing the MBBS programme, but some remain to pursue further studies (Fan et al., 2020; Huang, 2019). Jiang\nand Sun (2007) found that 87.4% of MBBS students planned to develop medicine-related careers after graduation and\nhoped to make a significant contribution to the health workforce of their home countries, or planned to migrate to other\nhigh-income countries (Han & Guo, 2009; Li & Sun, 2019). The quality of the Chinese MBBS education they receive is\nlikely to influence their licence exam performance and their future employment positions. The reputation of these courses\nis therefore key to the sustainable development of China’s medical education for international students (Zhou, 2016).\nMedical licence exams are necessary to evaluate the teaching provided by medical schools and the learning outcomes of\nthe graduates they produce (Han & Guo, 2009). The success rate of MBBS graduates in these exams reflects the extent to\nwhich China’s MBBS education meets international standards (Mao et al., 2012). However, the results have to date fallen\nbeen below those of several other countries. For example, more Indian students study in China than in any other country,\nand they are required to pass the Foreign Medical Graduate Examination (FMGE) to practise medicine in India\n(Muthyanolla, 2022). The total number of Indian students who studied in China and sat the FMGE between 2015 and\n2020 was 43,632, but the average pass rate was only 12.51% (Muthyanolla, 2022). Similarly, for Nepalese students who\nstudied in China, the pass rate in the Nepal Medical Council (NMC) Examination from 2016 to 2018 was only 34.3%,\nbehind that of Nepalese graduates from Nepal (80.3%), the Philippines (74.8%), Bangladesh (60.9%), Pakistan (58.4%),\nIndia (49.2%), Russia (44.6%), and Ukraine (40.9%) (Aryal, 2018). These results indicate that the majority of Chinaeducated MBBS graduates do not have the expected level of professional knowledge or skills.\n\n\n\nThe selection of academics to teach international MBBS students is an important consideration. Priority is usually given\nto those with overseas educational or other experience, as this group of teachers are considered to be proficient in English\nand competent in intercultural communication (Yu et al., 2022). To elevate teaching quality, Chinese universities also\ntake measures such as sending academics for training in other countries (Zhang et al., 2014). China’s MOE also\nestablished a Teacher Training Centre for studying medicine at Tianjing Medical University in 2010 (Liu et al., 2021).\nThis centre aims to improve the teaching standards and English teaching skills of medical teachers of international\nstudents in China and to improve the quality of training of international students and the level of education management\nservices (Liu et al., 2021). The centre takes a mixed-mode approach consisting of face-to-face training such as\ndemonstration teaching, oral English training for foreign teachers, and online training such as lectures by domestic\nand foreign experts and teaching seminars (Yu et al., 2023).\nAlthough the successes of medical education for international students in China have been acknowledged in recent\nstudies, several problems remain. All of the informants gave\nwritten and oral informed consent to participate in the study.\nResearch sites\nThe study was conducted in two medical universities (A and B) located in Jiangsu Province in eastern China, which has a\nwell-developed economy and offers high-quality education. Both had accepted international MBBS students for over 15 years, so it was reasonable to regard them as\nrelevant sites at which to gather the perspectives of academics engaged in teaching.\nBoth universities applied similar MBBS curricula, which followed the requirements of the MOE. International and\nChinese MBBS students took separate courses and did not attend the same classes. English was the principal teaching\nmedium of the courses for international students. Most of these students were from developing Asian and African\ncountries, and some were far from proficient in English.\nInformants\nWe took a purposive sampling approach in this study. We based our selection of interviewees on the subjects they\ntaught and their gender, years of teaching international medical students, educational background, and career stage. Our\naim was to obtain a heterogeneous sample of medical teaching staff from the two universities. Academics were\napproached individually via phone calls and WeChat invitation with the help of two ‘gatekeepers’. Our target sample\nsize before recruitment was approximately 30, but we increased this to 40 to achieve data saturation (Morse, 1991).\nOf these, two declined the interview invitation and another two initially agreed but later withdrew by failing to respond to\nthe researcher’s follow-up text messages.\nThus, a total of 36 academic teaching staff (17 men, 19 women) were recruited, of whom 2 taught Chinese, 3 taught nature\nscience, 11 taught clinical subjects, and 20 taught basic medical science. Their experience of teaching international\nstudents ranged from 2 to over 13 years, and all were responsible for teaching the same subject to both Chinese and\ninternational students (Table 1).\n\n\nTable 1. collection\nA semi-structured interview was conducted with each participant between November 2021 and January 2022. Only the\nfirst author and one participant were present in each interview. Academics from University A participated in face-toto-face interviews in their offices or a small meeting room that was perceived by the participants to be comfortable and\nquiet, while the interviews with academics from University B were conducted online (due to the COVID-19 pandemic).\nBoth the face-to-face interviews and the online interviews were audio-recorded and notes were taken throughout. The\ninterview questions, which were pilot-tested, addressed the participants’ views on factors affecting student success, and\ntheir recommendations for improving teaching quality at the classroom and organisational levels.\nThe academics were initially briefed about the probable length of the interviews and the purpose and significance of the\nstudy. To conform with ethical requirements, all interviewees were assured of their anonymity and agreed to the audio\nrecording and dissemination of the findings. Each interview was conducted in Chinese and lasted for around 30 to\n70 minutes.\nData analysis\nA thematic approach was taken to the data analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), with the audio recordings later transcribed\nverbatim. These Chinese transcripts were coded manually by the primary researcher, who immersed herself in the data\nand noted down her initial ideas on emergent themes. Initial codes were generated based on identified key words and\nphrases. These codes were then summarised into potential themes. The primary researcher translated the codes and\nthemes into English. The research team then discussed, adjusted, and reached a consensus on a final set of categories. The\nintegrity of the process was ensured through member checking, peer auditing and self-reflection. The primary researcher\nkept a self-reflection journal throughout the process to minimise the effects of her own background and work experience\non the interpretation of the data.\nResults\nTheme 1: Pedagogy and content alignment\n1.1 Challenges in teaching pedagogy\nMost of the academics perceived their teaching methods and practices to have a major influence on international students’\nlearning. However, they perceived a mismatch between their teaching approaches and the learning characteristics of these\nstudents. They mainly relied on traditional teacher-centred lecturing and tended to read verbatim from slides, without\ngiving adequate further explanations or offering practical examples that could enhance the students’ comprehension of\nthe subject. Some noted that as a learning approach, lectures may not be suitable to meet international students’ needs.\nA teacher of clinical medicine commented that in a lecture on clinical manifestations and diagnoses of diseases, the\nstudents were primarily concerned about the treatment and recovery aspects. The teacher suggested:\nThe students enjoyed learning about clinical treatments maybe because they thought that it would be useful for\ntheir clinical work in the future. However, treatment is only briefly introduced in this class. (P25)\nInternational students asked more questions than Chinese students and were willing to interrupt the class to express their\nopinions. This led the academics to consider how they could better communicate with them and how to improve the\nquality of their teaching. Most felt that much of the current teaching involved spoon-feeding information and lacked\ninteraction. Lecturers were failing to grasp students’ attention and hold their interest in class, which often affected\ntheir exam performance. Interviewees felt that teaching innovations such as case-based learning (CBL) and problembased learning (PBL) should be used to teach international students, because this type of teaching mode is more\nheuristic and thus encourages students to learn more actively. They suggested that CBL and PBL would increase the\ninvolvement of international students in learning activities. They could also make more frequent use of individual and\ngroup presentations and discussions, encourage students to ask questions, guide students to think, and try flipped class\nteaching (setting preparation assignments on a lecture topic before presenting the lecture). However, the academics also\nfelt that implementing these changes would be a challenge for them, because it would place more demands on their\nlanguage skills, subject knowledge, ability to listen and respond to students, and control the class. One of the few\nacademics who already involved international students in interactive class learning activities reported positive changes in\nthe performance of these students:\nInternational students are not interested in the traditional lecturing we use to teach Chinese students. Very few\nstudents listen carefully in class. I usually ask international students to summarise the content of the previous\nlesson in front of the whole class. They participate very actively and prepare well. I think international students\nlike to show themselves in public. If I take advantage of this characteristic of theirs, I can stimulate their passion\nfor learning. (P31)\n\n\nInternational students’ basic knowledge and learning were poorer than those of Chinese students. The lecturers\nacknowledged the diverse academic abilities of international students and their different cultural backgrounds and\nagreed that they should adjust their teaching approaches accordingly. They also believed that they should give culturally\nrelevant examples in class that the students were familiar with, make timely summaries of key knowledge points, give\nthem practice assignments to complete after class, cover exam points, and provide timely feedback on their assignments.\nAn academic teaching radiology gave the following example:\nFor international students, you have to start from the basics. For example, in my [radiology] class, there is a CT\nvalue, high or low. High is bright, low is dark. You have to explain this foundation setting first; otherwise, they\nwould become confused. Doing this sort of thing could help them but would require academics to take more time to\nprepare for class. (P19)\nThe interviewees reported that real clinical cases should be integrated into basic medicine lectures to help international\nstudents understand them, and they thought that the students would learn better with case examples. Academics without\nextensive clinical experience but who taught biochemistry, microbiology, immunology, and parasitology found this to be\na challenge. They said that they had to provide more than theory and facts and include relevant clinical knowledge to\nproperly teach their students. An academic teaching biochemistry who had a background in biology stated as follows:\nStudents have asked me some clinical questions, which shows that they have this demand. This demand actually\nreminds me that I should include clinical knowledge in my teaching. This could make my class more attractive and\nmake them realise that my course is very helpful for their clinical study in the future and can help them solve\nclinical problems. In addition, it can make students realise the importance of my course, at least for now, and\nincrease their learning enthusiasm. I’ve already started learning relevant clinical knowledge. (P6)\nSome of the academics also acknowledged that their lecture slides probably needed to be more interesting. They stated\nthat it may be necessary to use teaching aids in addition to writing on the blackboard, such as video, audio, and animation,\nto make the classes more dynamic and easy to understand for students. An academic teaching pharmacology mentioned\nhow he used online technology to make the learning in his class more effective.\nI like to visit YouTube to find some videos about the mechanisms of drugs to show in the class. Maybe my\nexplanations are not very good, but the videos explain very well, which can help deepen students’ understanding.\nMoreover, I feel that playing videos when students are sleepy can increase their attention. (P22)\n1.2 Alignment of teaching content with licence exams\nOver a quarter of the interviewees noted that a primary reason why many international students lack interest in learning is\nthat the current course content and resources do not correspond closely enough to the requirements of the licence exams.\nAcademics usually teach the knowledge that they think is important, and this may not precisely match a curriculum based\non the licence exam. A combination of academics’ specialist knowledge and a prescribed curriculum is required. This is a\ncritical issue, as students will be more motivated to learn if they perceive that what they are asked to learn will be useful for\npassing examinations and in their working lives. However, some of the academics interviewed argued that it would be\ndifficult to address all of the licence exam needs of students from diverse countries, as the content is likely to vary.\nDeveloping a more contextualised curriculum therefore seems essential, as explained by an academic teaching anatomy:\nOne of the key points of our teaching is to help students pass licence examinations. If a student does not pass the\nexam, he/she will not even qualify for matching hospitals. This is true for both Chinese students and foreign\nstudents. Teaching and licence examinations cannot be separated. If we want to help students pass the licence\nexam, teaching activities should play a guiding role … The passing rate for licence exams is the most important\nstandard measure of a medical university. Practice is the sole criterion for testing truth. (P2)\nTheme 2: Language barriers\nIn addition to teaching pedagogy, language barriers were identified as a significant challenge to successfully teaching\ninternational students. Most of the interviewees indicated that language differences and difficulties negatively influenced\ntheir teaching and students’ learning quality. They also offered various potential solutions. These Chinese academics\nspoke English as a foreign language, while international students spoke English as their first or second language (although\nmany were far from perfect in English). This is not a criticism of either group, but merely reflects the actual situation.\nCommunication was therefore limited because the academics were not necessarily fluent in English, and neither were\nmany of the students. The academics often found it difficult to understand their English accents and expressions, as they\nmay not speak standard American or British English, particularly those from India, Nepal, and Pakistan.\n\n\n\nThe academics admitted that while they generally had a good command of medical English in their own field after years of\nteaching, they still struggled with day-to-day and cross-disciplinary English. This limited vocabulary outside of their field\naffected their teaching and was one of the main reasons why they often resorted to reading verbatim from slides rather than\nexplaining concepts in their own words, and why they avoided engaging with students. Consequently, their students\nbecome bored during class and were sometimes annoyed by the poor communication. One academic who taught surgery\nnoted the difference between Chinese and international students in terms of language in theory lectures.\nIf it is a Chinese class, the academic has many methods of teaching. He has eloquence and rich experiences; he can\nsolicit and quote from others when giving lectures. However, in case of teaching foreign students, due to the\nlanguage barrier, academics cannot express themselves 100% smoothly. They can only communicate with\nstudents in the simplest way, so they cannot always respond appropriately to students. If they cannot attract\nthe students, the students will lack interest in the class. Naturally, they do not devote themselves to learning. (P35)\nAcademics teaching clinical medicine who were also responsible for clerkships and internships at hospitals indicated that\nit was hard to translate some Chinese words concerning a patient’s symptoms into English. Sometimes the translation was\nnot completely accurate, and international students could not comprehend explanations delivered in conversational\nChinese. Therefore, the effectiveness of clerkship and internship learning in clinical medicine was adversely affected\nbecause of language barriers, as one academic teaching internal medicine explained:\nTake the symptoms of angina pectoris for example. When I talk about angina pectoris to Chinese interns, I use the\nXuzhou dialect, which is very easy to understand. However, it is difficult for me to convey the clinical symptoms\nreflected by most patients to foreign students through a more appropriate word, because the pain reflected by the\npatient is not the oppressive feeling mentioned in the textbook. We cannot simply transfer the knowledge from\nbooks to students; we have to combine it with our clinical practice. However, sometimes, it is very difficult to\ntranslate properly. (P25)\nTheme 3: Resource management and learning environment\n3.1 Management of teaching resources\nOver half of the interviewees stated that issues with teaching resources also affected teaching and learning. Four subissues emerged within this theme, as follows.\n3.1.1 Demand of textbooks and teaching materials\nTwelve academics complained that they lacked easy access to necessary textbooks, English question banks, and other\nrelevant teaching materials, such as reference books and videos. Universities did not mandate or provide any particular\ntextbook for academics to use, and the academics had to seek out textbooks or create their own materials by drawing on\nvarious English and Chinese sources. If the textbooks were different from the students’ own, they could find it difficult to\nunderstand the lectures. The students were at times not assigned any textbooks or relevant learning materials. In addition,\nacademics responsible for different modules or units in a course often used different textbooks, which led to inconsistencies. Thus, they felt that experts should develop good textbooks to help students understand and review the knowledge\nlearned in the lectures, and they suggested that universities should purchase specialised English books, medical videos,\nand question banks for staff and students to use. One academic teaching gynaecology commented on her difficulty with\nusing textbooks as follows:\nOur Clinical College once gave us some textbooks, but I think the versions of those books were relatively old.\nI have not seen updated books. In those books, old methods of screening gynaecological cervicitis, which were\nused one or two decades ago, were introduced. (P27)\n3.1.2 Need for collaborative lesson planning\nSeven academics felt that it was important to engage in collective lesson planning to ensure effective communication.\nThis approach is currently lacking, but can be important for a teaching team to share experiences and discuss\nwhich knowledge points are the most important for students to master. These planning sessions can be used to discuss\nthe application of flipped class teaching and how to properly create synergies among different topics taught by different\nacademics. Experienced academics could also advise new members of staff about how to teach international students\nthrough collective lesson planning. Those inexperienced in teaching international students expressed their need to receive\nteaching tips about appropriate pedagogies, and to obtain feedback from more experienced lecturers. However, in most\ndepartments, and particularly in clinical medicine subjects, collective lesson planning and guidance was lacking, as the\nteachers were busy with their own clinical work and research:\n\n\nTheoretically, there should be collective lesson planning, but it cannot be done in practice … Teachers have their\nown interpretations of knowledge and lecturing methods, and collective lesson preparation has little impact on\nthem … Basically, teaching has been weakened and become a mere formality. There is no teaching demonstration\nfor new teachers anymore. The supervision mechanism has gradually disappeared. Now the overall orientation no\nlonger takes teaching as the main evaluation index, but mainly takes SCI publications. (P24)\n3.1.3 Weak teaching supervision system\nSeven academics reported that universities lacked a sound system for supervising teaching quality. This may lead\nacademics to conclude that it makes no difference whether their teaching is of a good standard. Without a system that\nrewards good performance and penalises poor performance, academics have no incentive to teach well. Some of the\ninterviewees stated that strengthening the supervision of teaching through regular observation by experts and spotchecking lecture quality would benefit academics.\nThe School of International Education needs to establish a system or assessment standard and implement rewards\nand punishments in each teaching and research department. If there is no difference between good and bad\nteaching, teachers will not be motivated. (P33)\n3.1.4 Inadequate clinical teaching resources\nFive senior clinical academics mentioned that there was a shortage of well-qualified personnel to teach theory, clerkships,\nand mentoring internships in hospitals. Due to the high turnover of staff, some departments lacked a stable team\nresponsible for teaching international students. They were often taught by recently hired academics or new doctors who\nhad minimal or no prior teaching experience. Their approach was limited to reading their notes or slides out loud, without\nany explanation or discussion. Those with teaching experience felt that universities and hospitals should attach more\nimportance to consistency in teaching international students, and should introduce incentives to encourage young,\nqualified doctors to participate in teaching. A perceived need to train new academics for future teaching work was\nobserved:\nDuring the clerkship, we had two teachers to teach 25 international students. The venue space and teachers were\nnot enough … For foreign interns, an internship was more like clerkship learning, just for a visit, because some\nteachers could not speak English and they were very busy. Foreign interns could not operate, due to doctor–\npatient relationship concerns. They could not receive much guidance … You know, this is a problem. (P24)\nThree academics also mentioned that their course syllabuses were obsolete. A senior academic who taught paediatrics\nsaid it was imperative to discuss with international students which specific diseases were common in their own countries,\nand thus what they needed to learn about. The course syllabus could then be updated accordingly. The students could then\nmore easily relate to the content being taught in class. These academics suggested their departments should evaluate their\nexisting syllabuses and improve them by making them more relevant where necessary.\n3.2 Classroom discipline management\nChinese academics felt frustrated and perplexed by some of the behaviour of international students in class. These\nstudents were often late to class, left in the middle of the class for no reason, or listened to music, used smartphones, and\nchatted with others and made noise during class. The mood of the teaching staff and their attitudes towards international\nstudents was adversely affected by such behaviour, as such discipline problems would rarely occur in classes of Chinese\nstudents. Seven academics stated that although such behaviour might be due to cultural differences, the students should\nfollow Chinese rules. The academics dedicated time to disciplining the students to make them aware of the seriousness of\ntheir studies. However, some emphasised that it was the responsibility of the student administrators to properly manage\nstudent discipline. One academic teaching hygiene explained how she managed class discipline and encouraged students\nto learn.\nIn sharp contrast to Chinese students, foreign students pay too little attention to class discipline... For example, for\nmorning class at 8 a.m., some students do not come until 9 a.m. I wondered why. Later, I went to Pakistan for a\nvisit. When I went to a university, I understood that this was their habit. However, in China, if you want to obtain a\nChinese degree, you have to follow Chinese rules … I count the check-in time and lateness in the class\nparticipation score, and I call the roll five minutes in advance. Sometimes I close the classroom door for fear\nthat they might sneak out. Sometimes I take a roll call in the second quarter of class to prevent anyone from\nsneaking out after signing in … It is like fighting a battle of wits and of courage with them. (P30)\n\n\n3.3 Assessment methodologies\nSix academics stated that the current assessment system was problematic, and they specifically mentioned the exam\nevaluation system and the final elimination system.\n3.3.1 Exam evaluation system\nOnly a summative assessment based on final exam performance was conducted at the two focal universities, with no\nformative assessment. Some of the participants suggested that the university should add formative assessments such as a\nmid-term exams or in-class quizzes, because this could help academics follow students’ progress, identify any\ndifficulties, push students to study harder, and reduce the pressure they felt regarding the final exam.\nA teacher of pathophysiology suggested that any evaluation of international students’ academic performance should take\ninto account their specific characteristics rather than strictly following the approach for Chinese students. This academic\nalso felt that international students were more interested in practical learning in laboratory classes and generally\noutperformed Chinese students in these classes, so the university could consider increasing the weight of the practical\nscore in their final course scores.\nSome also mentioned they wanted to change the assessment methods, but in most cases, this was beyond their control, as\nthe changes had to be approved at the organisational level after going through complicated application procedures. One\nparticipant who taught parasitology noted how their department had improved the assessment method based on students’\nfeedback.\nStudents came to complain that the exam was unfair because some students did not attend class, just memorised\nkey points the week before the exam and got higher marks. After receiving their feedback, we improved this. We\ntook their formative performance as a part of their assessment, and the final exam accounted for only 50%.\nAnswering questions and signing into class are all part of the formative performance. I think we should add\nformative assessment and tell students to listen to the whole class. Our purpose is not to let them fail in the exam,\nbut to guide them to learn something. Students understand this. Finally, almost all students passed my course.\n(P29)\n3.3.2 Final elimination system\nSeven academics pointed out that the universities’ assessment and evaluation systems were less strict for international\nstudents. This was perceived as a loose approach to entering and exiting the course, and did not put pressure on students to\nstudy. These academics suggested the university should implement a final elimination system to delay graduation or fail\nthose who do not have the ability to graduate or attain an MBBS degree.\nThe university must strictly enforce the examination system and cannot deliberately make exceptions in international students’ favour. Schools should dare to weed out unqualified students, so that they will be afraid. Once they\nare afraid, most of them will study hard. (P10)\nTheme 4: Teacher attributes and guidance\n4.1 Addressing teacher attributes\nNearly half of the interviewees acknowledged that the attributes of academics, such as their dedication, passion for\nteaching, positive attitude towards teaching, sense of responsibility, and subject knowledge, play a pivotal role in\nensuring teaching quality and thus enabling students to learn. They suggested that much more time and effort is needed to\nprepare lessons for international students, due to the modifications required. Some regarded the workload of preparing a\nlesson in English to be three to ten times greater than preparing a lesson for Chinese students. For instance, they had to\nwrite a lesson script beforehand to read aloud in class, because it was too challenging to improvise in English during the\nsession. This type of language problem would not occur in Chinese classes.\nThe interviewees admitted that many academics were not motivated to teach international students because their\nuniversities prioritised research over teaching. Academics had no incentives to devote time and effort to areas that\nwould not advance their careers. Therefore, although they were qualified to teach international students, they felt more\nmotivated to conduct research. Apathy regarding teaching also affected its quality. A typical observation was as follows:\n\n\n\nAll materials for lesson preparation must be in English. It is already very difficult to prepare lessons in Chinese.\nI have to prepare lessons in English and practice my manuscript … In this current university environment, which\ngenerally attaches importance to scientific research rather than teaching, everyone is willing to focus on their own\nresearch rather than pay attention to teaching. Research awards are more tempting... When it comes to\nprofessional rank evaluation, papers are more useful than a teaching competition award. (P3)\nDespite the common perceptions of a heavy workload and challenges and dissatisfaction due to the lack of incentives,\nsome academics maintained a personal commitment to their teaching out of a sense of responsibility and selfimprovement.\nTo maintain the enthusiasm of academics for teaching, it is not about monetary stimulation, but a sense of value\nand honour. Academics are not unsentimental people … Many academics are dissatisfied, but they continue to\nteach even if they are not satisfied. This is dedication, awareness, and sentiment … I don’t want to talk about\ninterests, but the school should give me some other aspects that emotionally make me feel honoured to teach\ninternational students. (P4)\nWhen asked about what incentives universities could provide to motivate academics, the interviewees said that university\nmanagement should attach more importance to MBBS education and to the academics who provide it, instead of\nregarding the field as peripheral. They emphasised the need for continuous professional learning to improve pedagogies,\nand assistance with oral English through formal or informal learning opportunities at home or abroad. Several academics\nsuggested that their teaching performance should be regarded by the university as part of its evaluation for promotion,\nrather than solely basing it on the number of publications. This would motivate academics to teach and make them more\nenthusiastic about teaching. A few academics also said they had sought funding so they could implement teaching\ninnovations in the MBBS programme, but had been unsuccessful. Two academics felt that the university should count\ntheir class hours as double or three times more than for Chinese classes, because they spent much more time on lesson\npreparation and class hours were considered in terms of academic promotion.\n4.2 Supervision and guidance by teachers\nWhen asked what academics could do to help students improve their performance, nearly half of the interviewees\nindicated that there was a need for them monitor their students’ progress more closely, and to push underperforming\nstudents harder, to make them aware they were responsible for their own learning. Some students were lazy and lacked\nself-discipline. The academics felt that if they could devote more time to guiding low-achieving students and talking with\nthem after class to encourage them, students might feel that their teachers are concerned about them and become more\nmotivated to study. An academic teaching cell biology highlighted the importance of encouragement and caring about\nstudents:\nStudents are still children. If academics communicate with them more, they will feel pressure and study under\npressure … In the first year of my teaching, I dedicated all my energy to push them. I tried my best to memorise all\nof the students’ names and faces. They felt my care. Several students have kept in contact with me since graduation\n… I was busy with my own life and family, but if I had spent more time at that time, I could have saved a few more\nstudents from failing. (P21)\nSix academics felt that it is important to impart not only knowledge and skills to students but also moral and ethical\neducation, values, and a sense of responsibility. They stated that it is critical to teach students to cherish learning\nopportunities in China, to ensure they have the correct learning attitudes, and to acknowledge their role in medicine. This\nwould stimulate students’ intrinsic motivation and instil in them a sense of respect and responsibility, as stated by an\nacademic teaching anatomy:\nIn anatomy class, the specimens are donated by others. No matter which country in the world, the donors are those\nworthiest of respect. As a medical student, you should respect them and the patients, because they, ordinary\npeople, have given us the opportunity to learn and explore medicine. You should treat patients well in the future.\nWhy did they donate their bodies? Because they hoped you could become a good doctor. (P28)\n4.3 Establishment of teacher–student rapport\nOne third of the interviewees mentioned that getting to know students and their cultures and establishing good teacher–\nstudent rapport are conducive to better communication and teaching. Good communication between academics and\nstudents can help build emotional connections and understanding in the classroom. For example, without knowledge of\nstudents’ lives and cultures, an academic might accidentally be culturally insensitive and cause discomfort. An academic\n\n\nteaching orthopaedics with more than ten years’ experience of teaching international students shared how he successfully\ndeveloped a good relationship with students by incorporating aspects of their culture into his class.\nOnce, as soon as I finished watching the Indian film Dangal, I quoted ten classic sentences in my slides to motivate\nmy students in class. They became very interested when they saw these quotes. “Wow, this is our Indian movie.”\nSometimes, I talk about my feelings about Nepalese festivals in class. They feel excited and think the teacher\nacknowledges their culture... If students like you, they will learn your course well. (P18)\nAn academic teaching anaesthesiology mentioned that those who taught clinical subjects generally knew less about their\nstudents, and thus they failed to establish a good rapport. This made their lectures seem rigid and lacking attention to the\nstudents as individuals. Those who taught clinical medicine suggested that universities should arrange activities to\nincrease communication between staff and students, which is particularly important for younger academics.\nDiscussion\nFrom the perspectives of academics who teach international students, this study revealed the critical factors that can affect\nthe academic success of international students in China. Four main themes emerged: the challenges associated with\npedagogy and content alignment, language barriers, resource management and the learning environment, and educator\nattributes and guidance.\nPedagogy and language barriers were the factors most frequently reported to affect Chinese academics’ teaching\neffectiveness and international medical students’ academic success. This supports the findings of the systematic review\nby Dang et al. (2023), who identified these factors as the most common challenges facing Chinese academics in Englishmedium-instruction (EMI) programmes. In addition, Helm and Guarda (2015) reported that in an Italian university, most\nlecturers of programmes where English is the medium of instruction needed to improve their teaching competences,\nwhich echoes our finding that pedagogy as the factor perceived most relevant to student academic success. The literature\nalso indicates that the pedagogical skills of academics are significantly influenced by language barriers (Ding, 2016;\nHuang, 2019; Zhan, 2017). Yang et al. (2019b) found that EMI teachers in a Chinese university were more didactic\nand less interactive in class than non-EMI teachers, which then hindered teaching and learning. However, improved\nEnglish does not necessarily equate to improved pedagogy (Dafouz, 2018; Dang et al., 2023; Helm & Guarda, 2015).\nThe findings of this study suggest that while it is important for universities to address the oral English abilities of\nacademics in terms of both specific disciplines and in daily life, improving their pedagogy is more important, as this\nis key to the academic success of international medical students. Universities should offer ongoing professional learning\nthrough teaching approaches such as PBL and CBL for pre-service and in-service academics, and they should fully\nconsider the needs, abilities, and interests of international medical students (Liu et al., 2021b; Saleh et al., 2012; Yang and\nLei, 2021). Universities should also strongly encourage and support teaching innovations with sufficient funding.\nThe problem of the detachment of teaching content from the licence exam requirements has been recognised in some\nChinese studies (Han & Guo, 2009; Huang, 2019). Some universities have been able to integrate licence exam content\ninto their teaching. For example, the MBBS curriculum at Zhejiang University has been internationalised with reference\nto UK medical education standards and clinical executive standards such as Tomorrow’s Doctors (Fan et al., 2020), the\nUnited States Medical Licensing Examination, and the Professional and Linguistic Assessment Board. Li and Cui (2010)\nfrom Dalian Medical University suggested introducing licence exam tutoring by inviting foreign academics to teach\nChinese academics using exam questions and content, but this was viewed as separate from regular teaching. This implies\nthat university staff should not only adequately prepare students for assessments but also shape the curriculum to include\nrelevant topics (e.g. topics relevant to the FMGE).\nThis study revealed that limited access to teaching resources can damage the teaching and learning experience.\nThe lack of standard English textbooks and relevant teaching materials for international students has been noted in\nmany Chinese universities (Huang; 2019; Jiang et al., 2014; Yang et al. 2019a; Zeng & Sheng, 2019) and in other\ncountries, such as Ukraine, where EMI programmes are taught to domestic university students (Goodman, 2014). Subject\ncontent is acknowledged to be mainly delivered through textbooks, so their selection and use will directly affect teaching\nand students’ knowledge attainment (Li & Cui, 2010; Li et al. 2013). Compared with the rich integrated curriculum\nteaching resources in developed countries such as in Europe and the US, China lacks English medical teaching materials\nthat have complete independent intellectual property rights and that align with the characteristics of its medical education\nin terms of relevance and adaptability (Wang et al., 2018). The dire shortage of English teaching resources has limited the\nsustainable development of medical education for international students in China (Wang et al., 2016). Although some\nChinese universities have begun to write their own textbooks and have made progress, problems remain. For example,\nChinese teachers’ levels of English ability are uneven, and their writing proficiency lacks unity (Wang et al., 2018).\n\n\n\nIn alignment with the literature (Ding, 2016; Huang 2019; Yang et al., 2019a; Zeng & Sheng, 2019), the findings of this\nstudy show that a lack of collaborative lesson planning for MBBS classes, a shortage of clinical teaching resources, and\nweak supervision mechanisms are common in Chinese universities. In terms of the perceived obsolete syllabuses, Zhou\n(2016) noted that Tianjin Medical University has added relevant teaching content according to changes of the disease\nspectrum in the country of origin. For instance, Schistosoma aegypti and Schistosoma mansoni, which are prevalent in\nAfrica, were added to the Human Parasites course. The example of Tianjin Medical University implies that organisational\nsupport plays a critical role in guaranteeing the quality of MBBS education.\nClassroom disciplinary issues are common with international students and can negatively influence the lecturing style of\nacademics. This problem has also been identified in other studies (Jin et al., 2009; Zeng & Sheng, 2019). Some academics\ntake disciplinary action, while others ignore the behaviour of problem students but feel uncomfortable with the lack of\ncontrol. In a survey of learning experience in a medical university in Southwest China, half of the MBBS students felt that\nthe academics were not strict enough in class (Yang & Lei, 2021), probably because of the language barrier. However,\nthe findings of this study suggest that academics should ensure good disciplinary management to encourage good\nbehaviour amongst students. Action needs to be taken with misbehaving students so they focus on attendance and\nlistening during lectures.\nThe findings regarding the influence of assessment methodologies on international medical students’ academic\nachievement have rarely been discussed in the literature. In a study conducted in Ningxia Medical University, Zhang\net al. (2020) focused on how the implementation of formative assessment in its MBBS microbiology course helped\nimprove class attendance and learning attitude, develop creativity and self-directed learning habits, increase teacher–\nstudent communication, and made evaluation fairer and more objective. This demonstrates the advantages of revising\nassessment styles. Zhao et al. (2020) claimed that the current assessment of students in medical colleges and universities\nin China is generally summative (at the end of the course), and that there is a lack of understanding of and attention to\nformative (ongoing) assessment and evaluation during the course. Summative assessment was viewed by the interviewed\nacademics as a relatively single and weak method that could reduce students’ enthusiasm for learning. Assessment only at\nthe end of a course makes it difficult to monitor progress in students’ professional knowledge development (Zhan, 2017).\nTherefore, universities should appropriately adjust their assessment methodologies to encourage international medical\nstudents to learn and improve the learning processes and environment.\nAs in other studies (Wang, 2013; Zhang & Hu, 2019), we noted that there was often a tendency in Chinese universities to\ngraduate international students by reducing the level of exam difficulty or lowering the pass mark, or by modifying the\nstudents’ marks to help them graduate. These approaches not only severely undermine student quality but also affect the\ninternational reputation and status of Chinese higher education. A system that deliberately holds back any student who is\nnot mastering the knowledge and skills taught in the MBBS programme is required.\nBlömeke et al. (2016) found that attributes of academics such as their experience, education background, motivations,\nprofessional knowledge, and self-efficacy predicted student achievement. Similarly, our findings suggest that academics’\ncharacteristics, such as passion for teaching, professional responsibility, dedication, attitude, and content knowledge, can\nhave an effect on teaching quality. As indicated by the interviewees, academics are often demotivated to teach\ninternational students because of a lack of emotional, material, and career incentives. A lack of incentives seems to be\na prevalent phenomenon, as reported in other universities in different provinces in China, such as Liaoning Medical\nCollege (Ding, 2016), Three Gorges University (Zeng & Sheng, 2019), and Zhengzhou University (Zhang, 2016).\nThe problem has also been identified in other countries such as Sweden (Stenfors-Hayes, 2010). This suggests that\nuniversity policy makers need to be attentive to the opinions and needs of academics when developing an appropriate\nreward system that can promote teaching quality and thus student success.\nThe findings concerning teacher guidance for students and its effect on their success is new to the literature. Chinese\nacademics appeared to subconsciously assume a parental role when disciplining international students, which implies\ntheir commitment to education. The importance of providing moral and ethical education for international students during\nand outside lectures has also been recognised in Chinese studies (Liu et al., 2021a; Wang & Huang, 2019). This may be\nparticularly relevant in a Chinese context because of the influence of Confucian ideology on Chinese education, which\nsuggests that academics should not only impart knowledge and skills but also have an obligation to cultivate the mind\n(Ye, 2001).\nAs previous studies set in China (Yang & Lei, 2021; Zhao, 2021) and Iraq (Saleh et al., 2012) have noted, this study\nrevealed that teacher–student rapport is an overlooked dimension in MBBS teaching. Differences in language, cultural\nbackground, and religious beliefs can lead to a lack of such rapport, and make the dynamics within a classroom dull and\n\n\nrigid (Yang & Lei, 2021). Building a good relationship between academics and their students may help to foster a more\nfavourable classroom environment for learning. This will require academics to learn more about the life and learning\ncharacteristics of international students and to communicate with them more after class to establish a harmonious and\nsupportive teacher–student relationship.\nConclusion and limitations\nThe findings of this study show that teaching-related factors have a major influence on international medical students’\nacademic success. The problems identified, and the findings of other studies, suggest that the same problems are prevalent\nacross Chinese universities that host international medical students. It seems that although China’s MOE attaches great\nimportance to the development of MBBS education quality, many challenges remain to implementing its policies at the\nuniversity level. These issues may be related to the importance and support that each university attaches to the MBBS\nprogramme, and its own ability to teach the programme effectively. The findings highlight that university policy makers\nshould devote more attention to the quality and development of MBBS education. Academics are responsible for\nimplementing internationalisation in education and the developers of educational resources (Ding, 2016). Universities\nhave the responsibility to support and meet the needs of academics in terms of professional learning and in the provision\nof incentives, and they should consider their recommendations for improving teaching quality. Effort should jointly be\nmade at the individual, department, and university levels to ensure high teaching quality, which is the key factor in\npromoting student academic success.\nOur study has several limitations. It was conducted at two sites, so caution should be taken when generalising the findings.\nThey may be relevant to other Chinese universities offering courses to international students, but not in other contexts.\nBoth research sites were located within a single province, and although representative of the general MBBS education in\nother universities in the same province, a broader geographical scope or the inclusion of more diverse medical universities\nfrom other regions or provinces might have led to new or different insights. Additionally, as the data were sourced from\nsubjective opinions expressed by the academics, potential biases may exist. In future studies, this research could be\nextended to investigate the perceptions of academics on other student-related factors that may affect their academic\nsuccess.\n\nReferences\nAryal BP: Docs trained in China, Russia, Philippines fail licensing exam\nmore often.
