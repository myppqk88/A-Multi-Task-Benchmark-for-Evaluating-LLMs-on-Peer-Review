In\naddition to introducing new teaching methods into their own\ncourses and programs, innovative teachers meet regularly\nto share experiences so that the collaborative network itself\nbecomes a learning community. The question this research\nstudy set out to answer is how learning and collaboration in\nsuch a global collaborative network can be facilitated in order\nto contribute to a fundamental change of the way education is\ndone at universities today.\nBased on experiences of the network members, we have learned\nabout successes as well as difficulties in implementing new\napproaches for sustainability education at universities. Despite coming\nfrom widely heterogenous backgrounds, cultures, and institutions these actors choose to engage in collaboration because\nthey believe this strategy will help achieve goals that would be\nunattainable working on their own (Camarinha-Matos, 2005;\nDzhengiz, 2020). Global project networks bring together actors\nfrom academia, business, public, and non-profit sectors to form\ninternational consortia that span disciplines, sectors and nations\nto potentially accelerate research and learning on solutions to\nsustainability challenges (Keeler et al., 2016). In collaborative\nEU-projects, partners from diverse organisations and countries\ncooperate to address societal challenges through research. A\nrecent study (Vinke-de Kruijf et al., 2020) on collaborative\nEU-projects concluded that wider learning outcomes cannot\nsimply be achieved by internal project conditions, instead there\nneeds to be increased focus on the processes of knowledge\nco-production and each projects’ interactions within the\n\ntransition context. However, these interactive processes are not\ndeeply understood, and how to organise and catalyse effective\nlearning across various project domains deserves more focus.\nThe need to foster collaborative approaches to improve governance of social-ecological systems was highlighted by Berkes\n(2017, p. 9) who concluded that “operationalizing adaptive governance requires learning from, and improving on, practices of\ncollaborative learning”.\nLiterature on strategic niche management has emphasized\nthe importance of learning from local experiments with novel\nideas (Hoogma et al., 2017; Kemp et al., 1998). Transitions are often\nrelated to solving complex sustainability challenges involving co-evolution of several different domains including technology, the economy, and education (Loorbach & Rotmans, 2010;\nRotmans et al., 2001). Network learning can be understood\nas “learning by a group of organisations as a group” (Knight\n& Pye, 2005, p. 371). The concept of network learning is best\napplied to learning within an interorganizational setting, whether\nby individuals, groups, or organizations. The study of learning in a regime\nor emerging niche is especially relevant for addressing the\ninteractions between actors. Network learning is different\nfrom the kind of organizational learning that is the result of\ninter-organisational partnerships and alliances (Dzhengiz, 2020),\nwhere focus is on learning that takes place in each organization\nand not in the shared space of network members. For example,\nif learning processes result in changes within an organization\nwithout having any discernible effect on network properties,\nthen one could say there has been organizational learning within\nthe network (Crossan et al., 1995). Expanding this concept,\n‘organisational learning’ is valuable when it affects how\norganisations learn to change their practices due to frequent\ninteractions among its members. But in order to understand\nlearning in sustainability transitions it is important to examine\nthe interactions that occur between organisations through processes of learning, feedback and knowledge generation (Van\nMierlo & Beers, 2020). Moreover, network learning is associated\nwith developing shared meaning among actors from different\nbackgrounds and practices. One issue that\nsurfaced was the negative attitude toward action-oriented learning practices among teachers who, according to several members of the consortium, are not always interested in alternative\nlearning methods or are not willing to go outside their comfort\nzones. One year into the project most of the partners were convinced about the benefits of the new learning models. Some members had a good overview while others asked for a clearer picture of the contents of each work\npackage and wanted to develop a better understanding of the\nlinkages between them. Assuring that all consortium members\nhave insight into various parts of the project as well as the\nwhole is necessary to foster good collaboration to create complementarity, and to avoid overlapping activities. The challenge of coordinating activities between various tasks and work\npackages continued throughout the project and even increased\ntowards the end when different elements had to be integrated\nin the final outcomes. Concrete, straight-forward\nissues could be dealt with online, but the online format\nproved to be less efficient when it came to communication\nabout more complex and less well-defined issues.\nCommunication\nOne year into the project consortium activities, research tasks and\nlocal learning arenas were up and running and there was need\nto share experience and knowledge from different initiatives.\nMuch of the discussions at the second partner meeting in\nBudweis were focused on how to improve internal project communication. In general, partners wanted to improve the horizontal information exchange between people on different project\ntasks and between members engaged in the same task, and they\nidentified the need to organize more virtual meetings between\nphysical conferences. In 2020 all meetings were\nonline, with which all consortium members agreed due to the\nCOVID-19 pandemic dangers and restrictions on travel.\nOrganisation\nThe complexity of the NF project was discussed at the\nkick-off meeting in May 2018, and in the notes from the workshop outcomes several consortium members expressed frustration with not being able to see a clear structure of the whole\nproject, some describing it as “amorphous” (Melin et al., 2021).\nThe consortium consisted of a wide and diverse network of\nactors in a system with interaction and exchange between both\nactors within the system (among consortium members) and in\nthe external educational landscape. Due to the complexity of\nthe project, consortium members found it challenging to understand project structure and plans, including responsibilities,\ncoordination of activities, and the process for moving forward.\nThese worries were connected to the start of the project and at\nthe consortium conference in 2021 these issues did not receive\nany particular attention.\nExternal stakeholders\nThe main areas of discussion at the second consortium meeting\nwere how to meaningfully involve external food system stakeholders in knowledge creation and how the project could have\npositive impacts on the food system. One main concern raised\nwas about engaging different stakeholder groups in the educational case studies, given that actors have different aims and\nexpectations and often people speak different ‘languages’. The\naim must be that everybody involved in transforming education should learn and derive something new from participating.\nTo some consortium members it is still not always clear what\nrole external stakeholders play and how they contribute.\n\nNetwork learning outcomes\nAccording to the concept of network learning developed by\nKnight & Pye (2005), the content of network learning may\ninclude observed key changes to network-level interpretations,\npractices and structures occurring within a given period of time.\nFor the NEXTFOOD consortium the achieved learning outcomes\nwere identified in workshops at the annual conferences and are\nlisted in Table 3.\nChanges to network interpretations\nThe consortium members were offered opportunities for discussing and reflect together in participatory workshops, as\ndescribed in the methods section. As a result of participation in\nthese activities they gradually developed a common understanding of the project’s overall structure and its key concepts.\nThe initial frustration experienced by consortium members in\nnot fully seeing the whole picture decreased by the end of the\nwrap-up session at the kick-off meeting. Consortium members\nsaid they had gained an improved understanding of the educational approach used in the project, both in the case development process as well as in the action research process.\nThe consortium conferences in 2019 and 2020 offered an overview of the network, and contributed to an increased understanding of the different project components and how they\nwere interrelated. Moreover, they said the culture was open-minded\nand that partners are open to try out new things. Members also\nhighlighted a number of barriers that were impeding learning within the network. For example, they thought that internal\ncommunication sometimes was failing, e.g., there was a lack\nof feedback from project partners and consortium members\nhad a narrow focus on their specific task which caused fragmentation. The purpose of the workshop\nin Vienna was to stimulate co-learning among network members on the challenge in shifting from being a teacher to a learning facilitator, on action research in education, and to establish\na productive collaboration among different elements of the\nproject. These success factors had previously been identified at\nthe consortium conference in Budweis, Czech Republic, 2019.\nThe network learning process is about developing interorganizational knowledge, that is, “a common repertoire of experiences\nand know-how from which the participants can draw” (Mariotti,\n2012). This involves more than merely sharing existing\nknowledge, rather it involves participation in fruitful interaction with other members for co-learning and co-creation of\nnew knowledge. In NEXTFOOD, the facilitators of the learning events aimed to combine dissemination of mid-term results\nand follow-up on milestones and deliverables through a process\nof dialogue, reflection, and visioning. This was a new approach\nto some network members, and some expressed frustration\nthat too much time was spent on the process part and too little on formal project content (milestones, reports, deadlines).\nThis is a parallel situation to what was experienced in many\nof the educational cases in the project, where some students\nstruggle with the shift from passive to active learning in a diversity of learning arenas. Like these students, most consortium\nmembers soon came to appreciate consortium workshops for\ntheir stimulating and productive dialogues, leading to a shared\nunderstanding of project aims and stimulating collaboration.\nAs facilitators of the network learning process we learned to\nestablish balance between content and process at consortium\nmeetings, and stressed the importance for members to be well\nprepared and well aware of the intended learning outcomes\nto be able to make a contribution and learn from the interactive sessions. In addition to the annual consortium conferences\na series of workshops was organized on-line and in real life with\nthe purpose of building capacity among the consortium members to work effectively for a transformation at educational\ninstitutions as well as in the consortium.\n\nDiscussion\n\nBy a recent declaration, the European Education Arena 2025,\nEU-leaders aim to build an inclusive and high-quality education as a part of the green deal strategy (European Union,\n2020). Education that is transdisciplinary, learner-centered and\n\n\nchallenge-based is particularly mentioned as an important\nmeans to foster ‘transversal skills’. Although policymakers\nand governments engage in the sustainability education agenda\nthrough top-down policy measures, the responses to educational policies are too often not translated into practical activities on campuses. Current literature tends to focus on niche-scale\nuniversity initiatives for sustainability education that provide\ninsights on various impactful teaching and learning approaches\nfrom a variety of different subjects. But empirical research is\nmissing on the communication and learning associated with\ninter-organisational and international collaborations in higher\neducation. It is important that teaching institutions share their\nachievements within the field of sustainability education with\nthe global community, and research should evaluate mechanisms and potential venues for sharing experience of local scale\namong niche-level actors. The model presented in this paper\ncould facilitate co-learning and collaboration by connecting\ninnovative university initiatives into a supra-community and,\nin the long term support, a transition of the educational system\nwithin agriculture, foods, and forestry.\nIn this paper, we used the concept of network learning to explore\nto what extent and how a facilitated consortium learning process can improve the capacity to change the educational practices\nin agricultural and forestry education, and thereby contribute\nto a transition of the larger educational system. In the context of\nlearning for sustainability transitions, reflexivity is an essential\nprocess of introspective examination of one’s personal beliefs,\nvalues, and judgments which may impact the research process.\nThis is seen as a vital quality that allows each person to challenge dominant regimes and methods by stimulating inquiry,\ndialogue and co-learning (Beers & van Mierlo, 2017). We particularly consider the important potential role of the consortium\nin promoting sharing of ideas, successes, and failures. Among\nwider expected modifications in strategy are transformational\nchanges from entire focus on content to a growing focus on\nprocess, while not abandoning the importance of essential content in courses and workshops. Another important change is\naway from traditional management and control to more inclusive cultures of education practiced and promoted by all participants in the learning landscape. A needed transformation is\naway from the current focus on isolated educational projects and\ntheir independent spheres of activities, towards a system that\nwill give way to some extent to an efficiently functioning collaborative network of projects. This does not suggest that all\nprojects will follow the same menu of educational methods,\nbut rather will pursue the most appropriate strategies to meet\ntheir goals in each particular ‘educational terroir’. An essential\ncomponent includes communication within the network about\nwhat each group is doing and encouraging a sharing of their\nevaluations of lesson learned including successes. This will result\nin a larger ‘learning network’ that will base future planning on\nlocal successes as well as ideas from other network participants,\nand eventually from other networks.\nWe recognize that the development of educational projects is\ninfluenced by specific contextual factors of the region in which\nthey have been carried out. Despite the individual differences\n\nspecific to the location, the projects struggled with similar challenges in gaining acceptance for the new educational\napproach among teachers, students and managers at their home\norganisations. These challenges were identified on multiple occasions in the workshops of the consortium and are mentioned\nin a summary list of challenges above. Most case leaders\nwere positive about the benefits they experienced as part of\nthe collaborative network. They felt that the network provided\nnecessary knowledge and support while they were implementing the new educational approach. They also mentioned\ngaining an improved image from being associated with the\nnetwork was helpful in achieving acceptance for the initiative\namong colleagues and managers.\nThe network learning process helped the consortium overcome\nchallenges and implement a new learning model at the partnering institutions, i.e. structural change happened despite frictions between the educational innovation and the institutional\ncontext. This is in line with Beers & Van Mierlo (2017) who\ndescribed a situation where an increased reflexivity leads to a\nconsiderable influence on the institutional setting, hence paving the way for change. Although change happened in all educational case studies, the road to get there was different for each of\nthe partners. Most importantly they started at different levels of\npre-knowledge and operated in vastly different contexts, which\ninfluenced the direction and the pace of moving forward.\nThe UNESCO report “education for sustainable development\ngoals” (UNESCO, 2017) highlights the importance of developing systems thinking through action learning to be able to\ndeal with socio-ecological complexities, such as the agrifood\nsystem. To develop a holistic view is challenging not only to\nstudents but also to academic staff, as reflected in the consortium’s struggle to take an integrated approach on the project.\nEven though consortium members saw the possibility of creating synergies by closer collaboration, they expressed a lack\nof understanding of the work done by other project teams and\ncould not always see how to go about collaborating in practice. To avoid compartmentalization, they expressed a need\nfor clear direction and for help to link their activities to other\nparts of the project, which became more difficult when the\npandemic hit and physical meetings were restricted. The consortium members viewed NEXTFOOD as a network organization, connecting different disciplines and a wide diversity of\nstakeholders. On one hand this generated dynamic and creative\ninteraction, but also resulted in a somewhat loose project structure, unclear goals and roles, which members sometimes found\nconfusing. To overcome such vagueness, Ayre et al. (2018)\nsuggested that individual roles and processes for working\ntogether should be negotiated in the initial phases of a co-creative\nresearch initiative. Scott et al. (2018) argues that new tools are\nneeded in the management of collaborative project networks\nwith emergent dynamic properties. An implication of our\nexperience is that an effective coordination instrument has to\nbe in place from the beginning to promote communication\nacross partner organizations. Collaboration in networks leaves\nmore responsibility to the participants in following up their\nwork, autonomously taking individual initiatives to collaborate\n\n\nwithin the network, and keeping updated with other parts of\nthe project. According to Oerlemans & Assouline (2004), a successful management of innovative networks means to combine\nleadership with shared responsibility and commitment. Ollus\net al. (2011) conclude that in collaborative project management,\nfocus is on understanding and monitoring tangible assets like\ncommunication, collaboration performance, and trust. Decisions\nare made in a decentralized and democratic manner, which\nimplies that partners share a common understanding of project\nobjectives, requirements and practices, and then have sufficient\nskills and competences to accomplish their project tasks.\n\nIt is often a new challenge for some to be invited into the\nplanning and evaluation cycle of the total learning process,\nand may not be one to which they are willing to dedicate additional time and energy. Individual projects tended to seek\nstakeholders who would be best suited to participate in such\na mutual learning process. Facilitating a learning process is\ncommon to essentially all teaching programs, but to truly engage\nstudents and stakeholders as co-learners we need to add the\nresponsibility to contribute to design of the sequence of topics\nand practical activities in a course and the activities needed to\nenhance learning.\n\nIn NEXTFOOD, partners sometimes had a narrow focus on completing their own responsibilities in the project at the expense\nof looking to the whole project. It is essential to design a\nreward system, through shared publication of journal articles\nor special recognition of each partner project, that will provide the needed incentives for each participant not only within\ntheir own organization but to see the value of engaging with\nand learning from others. Partners expressed that in order to\nimprove collaboration it would be necessary to develop further a\ncommon understanding of purpose, aims and fundamental concepts of the project. It became clear that partners of the consortium interpreted concepts central to the project differently, which\nhampered project development because communication and\ncollaboration were more difficult without shared meaning. For\nexample, ‘cyclical learning’, ‘sustainability’, and ‘skills’ had\ndifferent meanings to different partners. This is not surprising\nwhen the language of the project is English, which is not the\nnative language for most participants, and there are multiple\ndefinitions of terms used in the literature and in educational\nimprovement programs. In relation to this discussion, it is valuable to mention here the difficulties the consortium members\nexperienced with remote collaboration. Developing collaborative\nnetworks is easier when meeting face to face, which allows\ncommunication with people you haven’t met in person before,\nto establish more personal relationships that allow people to\nbe more direct and informal. It requires building mutual trust to\nestablish strong networks of institutions and foster frank and\nhonest exchange among participants.\n\nAlthough it was anticipated that serious participation in the\nnetwork would lead to positive pay-offs for each project, this\nmeans that members would justify the time and energy investment in the network in terms of advancing their own group’s\ngoals. It is important that those leading the NEXTFOOD\nnetwork recognize and respect the fact that each group has\nthe obligation to assess the costs and benefits for them to continue to participate in the network, or whether their goals\nwould be better met by using what they have learned to develop\nautonomous and independent activities. This is in keeping\nwith the principles of uniqueness of terroir and the need for\nusing scarce resources to best meet individual group goals. It is\na strength of the network to realize that some groups may choose\nto ‘do their own thing’ if they do not recognize advantages of\nworking in a network.\n\nIn the field of sustainable management of natural resources, there\nis an increased understanding of the importance of involving different sets of actors with complementary types of knowledge\nthroughout the whole research process, from initial planning to\ndissemination and demonstration (EU SCAR AKIS, 2019). The\nexternal stakeholders were involved to various degrees in the\ndifferent projects. In some cases they were mentoring the student teams in the product development process, while in other\ncases they gave input more from an external perspective.\nEngaging external stakeholders in a broad sense helps expand\ntheir roles from providing a farm to visit, an agricultural or\nforest-related business to analyse, an input supplier to evaluate,\nor other farming, forest, or food system activity where students\ncan conduct interviews to learn about their goals, activities, and\nchallenges. All these are important, yet they put the stakeholders primarily in a ‘teaching mode’ as suppliers of information.\n\nConclusions\n\nThe consortium action learning process connecting several multistakeholder research activities in a supra-community that contributed to network-level learning outcomes. This was observed\nas changes in how network members interpreted the new\neducational approach, the widespread implementation of new\neducational practices at teaching institutions, and the structural\nchanges at the partnering institutions. These results were\nachieved also in cases where the institutional environment was\nfar from supportive. Based on experiences of the consortium\nmembers, we have learned about successes as well as difficulties during implementation of the transformational process.\nManagement of an inter-organisational collaborative network\nmust early in the project clearly communicate the project structure with roles and links between different tasks and work\npackages to help partners in finding their place in the network\nand identifying important key synergies.\nThe responsibility of each individual partner must also be\nclearly communicated. There should be a constant learning process and ample opportunities to discuss and reflect together in\nparticipatory workshops, and these should be offered early in\nthe network activitiers. It is important that meeting facilitators\ntry to balance project content with an effective learning process. Even though this initially may evoke some resistance it will\ncontribute to developing a shared understanding of important\nterms and key concepts. To foster network learning, the achievements of annual consortium conferences must be followed-up\nby collaborative activities in between these meetings.\n\n\n\nTo adapt to new realities such as the pandemic and the goal to\nreduce climate impact, the consortium collaborated in a virtual environment during much of 2020 and 2021, and learned\nthat routine tasks such as project follow-up and presentations in one-way communication mode can preferably be done\nremotely while more complex processes involving more people is\nbetter done in face-to-face meetings.\nLessons learned in NEXTFOOD will reveal methods that are\nmost useful to facilitate the transformation needed by individual teachers, by students, by administrators, and by stakeholders. Progress so far with a wide range of learning activities in\ndifferent educational organizations has been encouraging, and\nwe look forward to seeking continuing grant support from EU\nor elsewhere to support projects that will contribute to a wide\ntransformation in participatory education.\n\nData availability\nUnderlying data\n\nZenodo: Educational transformation and network learning\ndataset – qualitative data from an international collaborative\nEU-project. Another\nexample is a case that educates the future gastronomist, i.e.\n“a man or a woman capable of thinking and framing food critically, as one of the crucial aspects of understanding the world\nand also fundamental for its transformation” (case leader,\nPollenzo 2018). For the gastronomist the food includes: “objects\nof knowledge in its many aspects” and “ a means of interpreting\nreality as a whole” (ibid.). These two examples show how the\nNextFood case leaders aim at training learners in taking a systemic approach to solve the complex challenges in the farming\nand food system. Moving students to the field as a starting point\nfor their learning journey is very different from the more traditional pedagogy at universities and challenges the academic culture where theories and conceptual knowledge is in focus. By\ngaining field experience students get an understanding of the\nfarming system and how practical experience can be connected\nto theoretical knowledge. The case leaders quickly appreciate how action-based learning contributes to a sustainability\ntransition on many levels -- by students aquiring sustainability\nskills, by the interaction of academia with the wider community, by contributing to the development of a generic curricula\n\n\nTable 2. After changing to an action-based learning\nmodel “the students and me realized that it is high time to come\nout of the classrooms and apply those ideas in society and thus\nbecome agents of change” (case leader, Pollenzo 2018). Case\nleaders say that making this transformation is not always easy\nand requires a shift of mindset among teaching peers and students. In a discussion at the Vienna workshop in 2019, the case\nleaders agreed that teachers need training in facilitation as well\nas in the core competences. The consortium members concluded\nthat the mindset of a good facilitator is someone who is well\nprepared in order to be flexible and in control of the schedule;\ninclusive and patient, making sure everyone is heard; ethical,\naccepting other views than your own; and aware of the group\nprocess and culture.\n\nA shift in educational practice to producing knowledge\nthat matters\nThe purpose of action-oriented education is not only to educate\nstudents but also to have an impact on extra-university stakeholders, and thereby have an impact on the local food system.\nAll cases organized a multistakeholder platform consisting of\nacademics, students and key stakeholders (local authorities,\nfarmers, rural entrepreneurs, cooperatives and associations).\nIn this local innovation system participants could act together\nin a way to find solutions and answering to specific demands.\nThe aim was to create “knowledge that matters” (Case leader,\nPollenzo 2018), where the knowledge creation process is\ndriven by the needs of stakeholders. In an action research process where outputs from one course cycle are being used in the\nnext version of the course and for feedback to the stakeholders, the intention was to combine knowledge development and\nthe facilitation of change. In one example, the case leaders\nsupported academic staff to go from teaching in their narrow\ntopics to become a part of a multistakeholder platform. They\nidentified a thematic or geographical area that could form a\ncommon ground for the multi-sectoral and multi-disciplinary\nteaching. They connected their case to a coastal park in\n\nsouthern Italy where agricultural activities and tourism co-existed\nwith high ambition to protect nature, preserve biodiversity and\ncultural-historical heritage. In one of the course cycles, student\nteams worked together with food entrepreneurs to increase the\nvalorization of organic food from the local area. In another\ncase, a course focusing on forest biodiversity and nature considerations, forestry professionals used social media to share\ntheir insights and observations from the field in a community of practice consisting of other professionals and experts.\nThis was an effective way to improve horizontal learning and to\nimplement nature-friendly logging techniques.\nThe NF cases made an effort to mobilize key stakeholders, who\nare not seen as passive actors; instead they are supposed to\ntake a very active role in education. The external stakeholders\nwere involved to various degrees in the different projects. In\nsome cases they were mentoring the student teams in the product development process, while in other cases they gave input\nmore from an external perspective. One case involved a network\nof farmers connected to Slow Food communities in various countries. The farmers opened their farms for field visits\nand invited students to spend three months in their communities\nfor doing action research. By building long-term relationships\nbetween faculty and stakeholders, students gain practical experience and the external partners gain access to faculty expertise\nand the creative capacity of student teams.\nAnother example on the change to education driven by societal needs is a NEXTFOOD partner that runs a food supply chain innovation competition game open to any team of\nmaster-students globally. A key change in the competition that\nwas implemented during the NextFood project is going from\na research question was defined by experts to questions are\nidentified by the participants themselves. By working on real\nproblems defined by students this education can contribute to\npushing the green shift of farming and food systems. The\nwinning teams of the past two years developed a solution\nfor reversing food waste to probiootic food in Nigeria, and\n\n\n\nenhanced local food access in the state of Kentucky. “This competition also pushes the green shift to reality because the problem of climate change becomes a specific problem related\nto food that you can work on at your university and solve in a\ncouple of months” (Case leader, Pollenzo 2018). One apparent change in all cases was an increased focus on soft skills\nin courses, such as collaboration and facilitation that teachers\nsaw as important for students to acquire for being able to\nfully participate in action learning activities. These changes\nwere reinforced by the positive feedback from students who\nembraced learning these new competences and appreciated the\nimprovements in teaching methods.\n\nA shift in educational practices to a focus on learning\nprocesses and methods\nFor the cases, experiential learning became an integral part\nof education and the universities organised many study trips\nwhere students engage in experimental activities in laboratories, in the field and in school gardens. In addition, students\nare inspired to volunteer in the activities organized by farms\nnetworks as an extra-curricula activity. Even if some of the\ncase leaders have been running an action-oriented short\ncourse for several years before the project, they emphasize the\nimportance of improving their theoretical and practical knowledge on experiential learning. “Every year brings me something”, one case leader said to stress the continuous improvements\nbetween each course cycle (Pollenzo 2018). Case leaders stress\nthat other teachers too should be more aware about the importance of learning processes and methods, and point out that\nthey should reflect on course outcomes and strive to become\nwhat is called a reflective practitioner. The professors involved\nin many of the NF cases are coming from different disciplines\nand don’t always share a common view on the importance of\nlearning processes. One case ran four different competitions\nin the four years of the NF project, and after each competition\nthey try to improve education and training methods based\non the feedback from participants. One example from this particular case was the development of interactive online workshops where the students were given the opportunity to train\nsoft skills. Knowledge on experiential learning is also necessary for students, so that they are able to support the learning\nprocess and learn how to become involved in agrifood systems,\nhow to reflect on their experiences, and build their knowledge\non what they have learned. One year into the project, consortium members noted that the global network and the learning\nprocess worked well. At a workshop about the learning culture\nof the project, held in between two annual consortium conferences in Vienna 2019, the 24 participating consortium members\ndescribed this culture as open and supportive of participation\nand co-learning. Members also highlighted a number of barriers\nthat were impeding learning within the network. For example,\nthey thought that internal communication sometimes was failing, e.g., there was a lack of feedback from project partners\nand consortium members had a narrow focus on their specific\ntask which caused fragmentation.\n\nAlthough reflection on experiences is fundamental to learning,\nsuch elements are not often a natural part of education today,\nand action-oriented learning methods are seen as the exception\nto a long-accepted conventional one-way style of teaching.\nThis became obvious in one of the cases in India that runs\na certificate course in agroecology with action-oriented\nlearning. The teaching used to be entirely based on theory transferred by teachers lecturing in a monologues style. There was\na total lack of transdisciplinary course content and for students the only purpose of education was merely achieving a\ndegree and accumulating theoretical knowledge. In line with the\nmotto of the university: Karmani Vyajyathe Prajnja meaning\n“Wisdom manifests itself in action”, the course transformed\ninto a holistic action-based learning model and since then\nthere has been a positive shift among learners in the development of the NF sustainability core competences (observation,\nreflection, dialogue, participation, envisioning, facilitation).\n\nChanges in structures through initiation of new\nprograms and courses\nChanges to network structures relate to how network learning outcomes were conducive for changing existing educational\nprograms and courses, as well as starting new ones, at the partnering institutions. These changes also include how new constellations of academics and external stakeholders were formed.\nNew pilot-courses initiated at some of the partnering institutions and existing courses that were subject to improvement\n([external resource identifier omitted]\nimplied a long-term commitment by institutions, teachers, and\nstudents. The fact that most pilot course leaders were successful and took steps toward institutionalizing their new activities demonstrated how learning outcomes led to structural\nchanges in the network. The educational pilots gained increased\nattention and attraction among students and institutions. For\nexample, in one partner university a short course in agroecology was developed into a full MSc program that started during\nthe third year of the project (Master in Agroecology and Food\nSovereignity at the University of Gastronomic Sciences of\nPollenzo, Italy, [external resource identifier omitted] A new Agroecology program started\nat University of Chile with a first batch of students in 2021.\nTwo courses covering social sciences, business modelling\nand agri-entrepreneurship started at Sekem foundation and\nHeliopolis Univeristy in Egypt and at Calcutta University in\nIndia. A new course focusing on biodiversity and nature considerations for forestry professionals started at Skogforsk, the\nForestry Research Institute of Sweden ([external resource identifier omitted]\nenglish/projects/nextfood/).\nAnother structural change is related to the new role of teachers as learning facilitators that was gradually accepted by the\nteachers at the partnering institutions. One year into the project\nmost of the partners were convinced about the benefits of the\nnew learning models. Our observations and conversations\n\n\n\nwith consortium participants and other educators in our universities is that this transition is well under way, and more people\nevery year are ‘buying into the concept’ of co-learning and accepting the reality of depending on a wide range of information\nresources beyond the personal expertise of teachers. However,\nat the consortium meetings the consortium members pointed\nout several barriers to the wider implementation of the new\neducational approach, which are presented in the following\nsection.\n\nBarriers\nAt the annual consortium conferences the members were asked\nto individually reflect, and then discuss in groups and in plenum\n“what is most challenging to the development and implementation of new action-oriented educational approaches in higher\neducation”. The outcomes of this discussion are reported in\nthis section as barriers on the individual, group and institutional\nlevels.\nIndividual level\nMotivation of students. There was concern that all students may\nnot be ready for this transformation and it is necessary to understand their attitudes toward their own learning. How to support student motivation and engagement in the new learning\nlandscape came up in several workshop notes. Although most\nstudents appreciate action-oriented activities, differences in\ntheir backgrounds and pre-knowledge levels influence how well\nprepared they are for self-direction in the learning process.\nTeachers becoming learning facilitators. A concern raised by consortium members was how faculty members can be supported\nto grow from their roles as conventional teachers to becoming learning facilitators. In the beginning of the case it was\nunclear what the role of a learning facilitator should be. Although\nsome teachers enjoyed taking this role, some did not, and continued to give students the right answers instead of teaching them the tools needed to understand the whole system and\nfrom this derive their own solutions. Case leaders also discussed\nwhether the facilitating role should be designated to specific\nindividuals or whether it is the responsibility of all teachers\nin the course. To account for these individual preferences\namong teachers some cases reported they had two facilitators\nfor each student group.\nEngaging external stakeholders. The main areas of discussion\nat the second consortium meeting were how to meaningfully\ninvolve external food system stakeholders in knowledge creation and how the project could have positive impacts on the\nfood system. One main concern raised was about engaging different stakeholder groups in the educational case studies, given\nthat actors have different aims and expectations and often people speak different ‘languages’. The aim must be that everybody involved in transforming education should learn and derive\nsomething new from participating. To some consortium members it is still not always clear what role external stakeholders\nplay and how they contribute.\n\nGroup level\nResistance among teaching peers. According to several members\nof the consortium, teachers are not always interested in alternative learning methods or are not willing to go outside their\ncomfort zones. As an example, in one of the cases instructors\nare travelling from the outside to give lectures for one or two\nweeks in the course. For them to take the role as a learning facilitator was not appreciated by many teachers since this would\nmean to follow the students in their group projects during eight\nmonths, which is totally different from ‘parachuting in and\nmaking a short guest performance’ in the course.\nInstitutional level\nSupport from upper management. “We have the impression that\nit would be easier at NF partner universities, but it still would\nrequire a lot of work, and we would need a better way of communicating between teachers and heads of departments and the\nadmin staff”. This quote was captured at the on-line meeting\nin 2020 and highlights one difficulty for the educational innovation to become institutionalized, i.e. become a part of the\nmainstream educational regime. One way case leaders tried to\ninstitutionalize their cases was to try to make the certificate course\nrecognized as a credit course, which was not always accepted\nby the teaching institution.\nLack of rewards and credits. The institutional setting sometimes caused challenges to the implementation of the new learning approach. For example, in one case the teachers were only\nready to spend a few hours in the field compared to the ten days\nof field activities that were needed to accomplish the action\nlearning activities. Teachers and students not being rewarded for\nspending this amount of time in the field as well as the shortage of suitable teaching facilities at farmer training centers\noutside the university was particularly a challenge for one of\nthe cases.\nTaking action learning on-line\nThe COVID-19 pandemic that hit in 2020 raised major challenges to teachers using action-oriented learning approaches,\nbut also offered opportunities to develop creative methods for\non-line learning. At the consortium conferences in 2020 and\n2021, case leaders reported positive experiences of having the\ncourses on-line, but at the same time they worried that lack\nof interaction with peers and stakeholders in real life might\ndecrease learners’ experiential abilities. In some partner countries the infrastructure for was not enough developed in the\ncountryside to successfully run courses on-line.\n\nAnalysis and discussion\n\nIn this article we explore the development of a transformative\ninnovation– the NEXTFOOD educational approach-- and its\ncapacity to achieve outcomes that might contribute to a transition to sustainability action learning approaches in food and\nagricultural education. It is transformative because it is rooted\nin local teaching initiatives that are connected to a supracommunity for sharing ideas and experiences across different contexts\n\n\n\n(Loorbach et al., 2020). Transformative innovations can potentially contribute to transitions on a broader scale than regular\ninnovation projects since they build on the collective efforts\nand experiences of many local initiatives (ibid.). The action\nresearch model pursued in this project favored a translocal\ndevelopment of the educational approach by supporting learning\nin local cases and at the same time stimulating a diffusion\nof experiences between teaching institutions in different geographical and cultural contexts. From a transition management\nperspective, a concern is how this development will continue\nafter the project has ended. The individual cases will most likely\ncontinue on their chosen path on their own, but without having a connection to the learning network the transition will lose\nmomentum and miss the opportunity for a broader impact on\nthe wider educational sector. In this section we provide recommendations on how a transformative innovation can be firmly\nestablished in the regime of food and agricultural education.\nThe development of the NEXTFOOD educational approach represents the start of a productive transitioning process that will\nbe ongoing. It began as a conceptual model for action learning\neducation that was tested and further developed in several action\nresearch projects with promising contributions to the transition\nof the larger educational system. The outcomes of the cases\nresulted in changes in mindset among teachers and students,\ndiffusion of educational practices and new structures at the\nteaching institutions. People working in local cases encountered\nseveral barriers an individual level (new roles for students,\nteachers and stakeholders), on a group level (resistance among\nteaching peers), and at the institutional level (no managerial\nsupport and inflexible administration). This is what one would\nexpect from any transition experiment, that in similarity with\nNF, shows potential to contribute to a transition but with a\nhigh risk of failure due to many hindering forces (Rotmans &\nLoorbach, 2009).\nThe promising contribution to educational transition was conveyed by the three mechanisms of deepening, broadening and\nscaling-up (Van den Bosch, 2010). When it comes to deepening, the facilitated network learning process resulted in shared\nmeanings and methods and improved the capacity to change the\neducational practices in courses and programs at the teaching\ninstitutions. In the cases, there was an intentional shift from the\none-way transfer of knowledge through lectures to participatory\naction learning in the field. However, it was obvious that it\ntakes a shift in mindset among the members of the network to\ngo from a ‘content focus’ to a ‘process focus’, where attention\nis directed towards co-learning processes instead of only looking at their individual project outcomes and formal outputs\nsuch as reports. Implications for facilitators of translocal networks are to design a process with the right balance between\ncontent and process and promote regular reflective sessions for\nteachers, students and other actors involved in education where\nimprovements in teaching methods are discussed. Activities\nfor self-reflection are not common at teaching institutions today,\nand universities with ambitions to take an active role in societal\nchange processes need to take steps towards becoming learning\n\norganisations (Albrecht et al., 2007). Higher education organisations are bound to tradition, and change therefore comes slowly\nand in small steps. There exists a fundamental paradox at the\nheart of higher education organizations: ‘they are institutions\ndesigned to teach, but not to teach themselves. Change, therefore,\ncomes slowly and incrementally’ (Stephens & Graham, 2010,\npp 617).\nWhen it comes to broadening, the educational experiment was\nrepeated in several different locations and broadened in terms\nof types of learners, geographical and cultural contexts. It was\nalso linked to different functions, i.e. pursuing education within\ndifferent topics (agriculture, forestry and aquaculture) and for\nlearners at different levels (high school, university and lifelong\nlearning).\nSome partners disseminated the NF educational model beyond\nthe consortium by teaching faculty and staff at other institutes by means of the methods developed, which indicates that\nbroadening was taking place. Some attempts were made to connect the cases directly to regional vocational schools and to\ninternational networks for sustainable education in agriculture but no firm connections were established during the project\nperiod. Broadening was encouraged by the formation of a\nsupra-community connecting several multistakeholder educational cases and local learning processes. Being a part of\nsuch a community gave the case teams the necessary support to\nadopt the educational approach at their home institutions. The\nnetwork learning process was important to reach shared goals\nand to align the various case activities. The cases that entered\nthe project had from the beginning different levels of maturity and the broadening process implied a transfer of knowledge from the more experienced cases to the ones that recently\nadopted the approach.\nThe network learning model presented in this paper facilitated co-learning and collaboration by connecting innovative\nuniversity initiatives into a community of practice and, in the\nlong term, could support a transition of the educational system\nwithin agriculture, foods, and forestry. It is a useful instrument\nthat can support the development of transformative innovations by allowing transition facilitators to study how learning outcomes from niche-experiments complement each other\nand contribute to enhancing a global sustainability trajectory.\nThis local-global dynamics is based on a flow of knowledge\nand experience across individual transitions experiments, and\none emergent property may be general lessons drawn from the\ncollective experience (Geels & Deuten, 2006). Literature on\nstrategic niche management has emphasized the importance of\nlearning from local experiments with novel ideas (Hoogma\net al., 2017; Kemp et al., 1998). Socio-ecological research that\ngenerates biological and social knowledge on the adoption of\nsustainable production methods in agriculture is a promising\ncontribution to the transition literature (Gaba & Bretagnolle,\n2020; Teschner et al., 2017). By facilitating reflexive learning\nand promoting productive interactions between researchers\nand stakeholders, it contributes to identify strategies for a\n\n\n\nsustainable transition. By linking several such experiments in a\ntranslocal learning process, e.g. by connecting to or developing\nan international network for annual conferences, publications and\non-line communities, research outcomes could be combined and\nprogress could be reached faster.\nWhen it comes to scaling-up, the educational approach was\nmore or less embedded in the institutional environment at the\npartnering institutions but still has to prove its merits in being\naccepted as a real alternative or complement to mainstream\nconventional education. Four years that the transition experiments lasted was not enough time to establish a firm foundation\nwithin mainstream education, which is not surprising since\nregime shifts are long-term changes. We identified a vast number\nof barriers that need to be addressed on the road to a transition\nto action-oriented sustainability education. Identified barriers\nwere related to factors at teaching institutions, at group-level,\nand mindset of teachers, students and stakeholders. To support up-scaling, we can learn from transition management that\nstrategic, tactical, operational and reflexive activities should be\ncombined as these interact and reinforce each other (Loorbach\n& Rotmans, 2010). Previous studies on change at universities\nsuggest that there usually is more focus on operational and tactical levels, i.e. planning and running experiments, while less attention is given to the strategic and reflexive activities (Thompson\n& Green, 2005).\nIt is necessary that sustainable education becomes a core mission of higher education institutions and that academic leaders\nreconsider the educational strategy, embracing a more integrated\nand holistic approach and co-curricular activities on campus and in the community (Cortese & Hattan, 2010). Whereas\nbottom-up leadership and change initiatives driven by students\nand faculty are crucial to achieve the necessary change, top\nmanagement leadership support is important to gain broad support and accomplish larger, more revolutionary educational\ntransformation (Lee & Schaltegger, 2014). In this study, one\ncase received support from upper management to establish a\ncenter for agroecological research and education at the institute,\nwhich is one example of how managerial support can make\nthe niche more stable. However, there was no evidence that the\nNF educational approach had been adopted at other programs at the partnering organisations or at neighboring teaching institutions. However, research outcomes and best practices\ndisseminated by the end of the project might inspire others to drive change in education in the spirit of the NF project.\nTwo main outcomes of the project were a roadmap and practical manuals for anyone who would like to initiate change in\neducation. This may be useful for fresh initiatives that have to\novercome the vast number of barriers identified in this\nproject.\nWe recognize that achieving an educational transition will\nrequire long-term commitment and involvement by participants\nin the project, as well as by stakeholders in the field. Hopefully\nthe empirical knowledge on barriers and mechanisms of transition processes presented in this study may support the planning\n\nand implementation of future initiatives in the area of\nsustainability education.\n\nConclusions\n\nThe NEXTFOOD case study showed how facilitation of an innovative educational approach in a network of local actors can be\na key instrument to contribute to a transition in sustainability\neducation. Results of a multi-dimensional learning process\nwere observed as changes in how network members interpreted\nthe educational approach, the implementation of new educational practices, and the structural changes taking place at the\npartnering institutions. These results were achieved also in cases\nwhere people in the institutional environment were far from\nsupportive and willing to change. Changes in education were\nconveyed by the mechanisms deepening, broadening and\nscaling-up, and complemented rich descriptions of local cases\nwith an analysis of the transition process across teaching institutions. This makes the results of our empirical research more\ngeneralizable and therefore outcomes could contribute significantly to successful implementation of future initiatives in sustainability education. Knowledge and experience flowed between\nthe local projects and the educational approach was successfully repeated in several locations within different educational\ncontexts, which could contribute to a regime change.\nTo what extent this education contributed to a food system transformation is difficult to assess, but the cases had a direct impact\nby the co-creation of knowledge in relation to real sustainability problems and by sharing nature-friendly practices in\neach community of practice. Indirectly the cases have an impact\nthrough the competences learners developed necessary to\ntake responsible action for sustainability in their professional\nlives. To overcome the many barriers related to individuals,\nteams and institutions, bottom-up initiatives driven by teachers and students need support from educational management.\nFollow-up action research should investigate how the involvement of educational management in long-term goal formulation\nand envisioning may enhance scaling-up promising initiatives\nand contribute to a regime change.\n\nData availability\nUnderlying data\n\nZenodo: Educational transformation and network learning\ndataset – qualitative data from an international collaborative\nEU-project.
