      "text": "Abstract\nApproach biases to foods may explain why food consumption often\ndiverges from deliberate dietary intentions. Yet, the assessment of\nbehavioural biases with the approach-avoidance tasks (AAT) is often\nunreliable and validity is partially unclear. The present study continues\na series of studies that develop a task based on naturalistic approach\nand avoidance movements on a touchscreen (hand-AAT). In the handAAT, participants are instructed to respond based on the food/nonfood distinction, thereby ensuring attention to the stimuli. Yet, this\nimplies the use of instruction switches (i.e., ‘approach food – avoid\nobjects’ to ‘avoid food – approach objects’), which introduce order\neffects. The present study increased the number of instruction\nswitches to potentially minimize order effects, and re-examined\nreliability. We additionally included the implicit association task (IAT)\nand several self-reported eating behaviours to investigate the task’s\nvalidity. Results replicated the presence of reliable approach biases to\nfoods irrespective of instruction order. Evidence for validity, however,\nwas mixed: biases correlated positively with external eating, increase\nin food craving and aggregated image valence ratings but not with\ndesire to eat ratings of the individual images considered within\nparticipants or the IAT. We conclude that the hand-AAT can reliably\nassess approach biases to foods that are relevant to self-reported\neating patterns.\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nWhat and how much someone eats depends on explicit processes such as deliberate dietary intentions as well as on implicit\nresponses to environmental food cues. While this cue reactivity expresses itself through cue-induced consumption and\nsubjective food cravings, it is also evident in behavioural biases\nthat motivate responses towards the respective food cue (Boswell\n& Kober, 2015; Brockmeyer et al., 2015). These behavioural\nbiases may play a pivotal role in the cascade of events that initiates and maintains dietary failure: approach bias towards food\nhas been related to higher food intake when self-regulatory\ncapacity is low, and it has been related to more uncontrolled eating in impulsive individuals (Booth et al., 2018; Kakoschke\net al., 2015; Kakoschke et al., 2017b). This does not seem to\nextend to individuals with clinically diagnosed binge eating however, who do not show an increased approach bias to food compared to healthy controls (Paslakis et al., 2017). At the other\nend of the spectrum, a decrease or absence of approach bias\ntowards food may help to explain the persistently reduced food\nintake in individuals with anorexia nervosa (Neimeijer et al., 2019;\nPaslakis et al., 2016; for a review see Paslakis et al., 2020). Perhaps most importantly, a recent review has concluded that modification of approach bias can help reduce food consumption,\nthereby supporting a causal role for approach biases within\nnormal and disordered food consumption (Kakoschke et al.,\n2017a). Hence, research has begun to focus on the reliable\nassessment of behavioural biases so they can be measured and\ntargeted for treatment in uncontrolled eaters.\nBehavioural bias to food cues can be quantified using the\napproach-avoidance task (AAT). In the AAT, participants are\nrequired to approach and avoid stimuli from at least one category.\nAn approach bias is inferred if a stimulus category, such as\nfood, is approached faster than avoided, and this advantage for\n\napproach is larger than that of another stimulus category, such\nas office articles (e.g., Lender et al., 2018). It is necessary to\ncompare two stimulus categories to control for stimulus-independent factors, such as differences in muscle strength or posture,\nwhich influence speed of approach and avoidance responses. Next\nto stimulus categories, the used representation of approach and\navoidance (e.g. zooming stimuli, moving a manikin towards\nor away from stimuli, moving stimuli in 3D environment), the\ninput devices (e.g. keyboards, computer mice, touchscreens,\nmotion sensors) and the instruction types (Krieglmeyer & Deutsch,\n2010) affect psychometric properties of AAT implementations.\nTwo different task instructions have been used. In the (more\ntypical) irrelevant-feature AAT (e.g. Kakoschke et al., 2017a;\nBrockmeyer et al., 2015), participants must approach or avoid\nstimuli based on a feature of the stimulus that is unrelated\nto the bias being measured (e.g. approach stimuli with green\nframe and avoid those with a blue frame); in the relevantfeature AAT (e.g. Brignell et al., 2009; Lender et al., 2018; Meule\net al., 2019), the participants must approach or avoid based on\nthe stimulus category, thereby directing attention to the feature\nfor which there might be an approach bias (e.g. approach food\nstimuli and avoid objects and vice versa). Feature-irrelevant\nAATs are appealing as they do not require an instruction\nswitch halfway through the experiment, and are less susceptible to demand characteristics, thus allowing for stronger claims\nregarding automatic stimulus-response associations (Chen &\nBargh, 1999; Rinck & Becker, 2007). Yet, a meta-analysis including various stimulus categories (e.g. faces and affective words,\nPhaf et al., 2014) as well as direct task comparisons in the food\ndomain suggest that only relevant-feature AATs have yielded\nsignificant and reliable approach biases, whereas biases in\nirrelevant-feature AATs were not significant or unreliable (Lender\net al., 2018; Meule et al., 2019; Phaf et al., 2014). Next to instruction, it matters whether it is the object or the participant that moves\ncloser or further away. In set-ups using a joystick, approach bias\neffects are elicited primarily by the stimuli zooming in or out\n(Krieglmeyer & Deutsch, 2010; Rinck & Becker, 2007) and accordingly, approach bias could be attained when stimuli were moved\nwith simple key presses (Becker et al., 2015; Peeters et al., 2012).\nHowever, it was shown that manipulating a stimulus’ position at\na distance, as simulated with the zoom-feature, elicits smaller\napproach biases than moving oneself towards or away from a\nstimulus in a three-dimensional digital environment (Rougier\net al., 2018). Thus, unclear perspective and irrelevant-feature\ninstructions may explain why only some studies find approach\nbiases to foods or correlations with food craving and eating styles such as restrained and external eating (Brignell et al.,\n2009; Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Lender et al., 2018; Maas et al.,\n2017a; Meule et al., 2019; Veenstra & de Jong, 2010) while\nothers do not (Matheson, 2018; Meule et al., 2020).\nTo improve the assessment of approach bias, we developed a\nnew variant of the AAT. In this ‘hand-AAT’, participants slide\ntheir hand towards or away from a picture on a touchscreen,\nwith movement direction depending on the stimulus category\n(relevant-feature: food vs. object). By moving one’s own, physical hand, there is no metaphoric ambiguity about whether the\nparticipant or the stimulus is moving, and participants perform\n\n\nactual ecologically valid approach and avoidance movements\nrather than producing abstract representations of approach and\navoidance using artificial input devices such as joysticks or key\npresses. While being unrelated to\neach other, both biases were stronger in individuals with\nhigher external eating. Larger AAT biases were further found\nin participants reporting increases in food craving over the\ncourse of the experiment. We thus demonstrated a relationship\nbetween forms of cue-reactivity as expressed through implicit\napproach responses, cue-induced craving, and eating style.\nYet, individually more desired food items were not approached\nfaster, even though larger AAT biases were related to\nhigher mean food liking, and neither the AAT biases nor the\nIAT biases correlated with state craving, trait craving, restrained\neating, or general desire to eat different foods.\nThe lack of an association between AAT and IAT scores is\nnot an uncommon finding in the eating literature (Maas et al.,\n2017a; Woud et al., 2016) and in implicit bias research more\nbroadly (Pieters et al., 2014), with some researchers even finding\na negative correlation between the two (Larsen et al., 2014;\nWarschburger et al., 2018). These findings suggest that the\nAAT measures the association between stimuli and directional\nmovements, whereas the approach-avoidance IAT measures\nthe association between stimuli and words. The associations\nwith approach-avoidance words do not necessarily overlap with\nactual behavioural tendencies and can directly oppose each other,\n\nfor example in dieters who have an approach bias towards food\nbut associate it with avoidance-related self-talk (Wiers et al.,\n2017), hence supporting a more ‘verbal’ nature of the IAT, at\nleast relative to the AAT. Despite being unrelated to each other\nin the current study, both tasks were associated with external\neating, the tendency to eat in response to external cues rather\nthan internal ones such as hunger. This suggests that some participants may display external eating due to strong cue-elicited\napproach responses, while others may display external eating\ndue to a more cognitive association between food and\nconsumption, for example due to food-related beliefs and cultural\nnorms.\nWe could not replicate the finding that interpersonal differences in the desire to eat individual food items predict approach\nbias for those individual food items (Kahveci et al., 2020;\nKahveci et al., 2021). This may be because this study featured a one-week delay between the desire to eat ratings and\napproach bias measurement, whereas the aforementioned studies collected ratings directly after measurement of approach bias.\nThe relationship between approach bias and food preferences\nmay thus be momentary, as the desire for specific foods changes\nwithin days (Reichenberger et al., 2018) and the relationship between implicit associations and consumption behaviour\nwas only found under high craving and hunger (Richard\net al., 2019). While preferences for specific food items change\nover time, the general tendency to either like or dislike most\nfood types may be more stable (e.g. picky eating; Kauer et al.,\n2015). In line with this, we showed that higher mean food liking relates to stronger approach bias despite the one-week delay.\nOn the most general level, AAT correlates come both from\nstate-domain (e.g. desire for specific foods at that moment) and\ntrait-domain measures (e.g. external eating, general food liking).       "text": "Abstract\nApproach biases to foods may explain why food consumption often\ndiverges from deliberate dietary intentions. Yet, the assessment of\nbehavioural biases with the approach-avoidance tasks (AAT) is often\nunreliable and validity is partially unclear. The present study continues\na series of studies that develop a task based on naturalistic approach\nand avoidance movements on a touchscreen (hand-AAT). In the handAAT, participants are instructed to respond based on the food/nonfood distinction, thereby ensuring attention to the stimuli. Yet, this\nimplies the use of instruction switches (i.e., ‘approach food – avoid\nobjects’ to ‘avoid food – approach objects’), which introduce order\neffects. The present study increased the number of instruction\nswitches to potentially minimize order effects, and re-examined\nreliability. We additionally included the implicit association task (IAT)\nand several self-reported eating behaviours to investigate the task’s\nvalidity. Results replicated the presence of reliable approach biases to\nfoods irrespective of instruction order. Evidence for validity, however,\nwas mixed: biases correlated positively with external eating, increase\nin food craving and aggregated image valence ratings but not with\ndesire to eat ratings of the individual images considered within\nparticipants or the IAT. We conclude that the hand-AAT can reliably\nassess approach biases to foods that are relevant to self-reported\neating patterns.\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nWhat and how much someone eats depends on explicit processes such as deliberate dietary intentions as well as on implicit\nresponses to environmental food cues. While this cue reactivity expresses itself through cue-induced consumption and\nsubjective food cravings, it is also evident in behavioural biases\nthat motivate responses towards the respective food cue (Boswell\n& Kober, 2015; Brockmeyer et al., 2015). These behavioural\nbiases may play a pivotal role in the cascade of events that initiates and maintains dietary failure: approach bias towards food\nhas been related to higher food intake when self-regulatory\ncapacity is low, and it has been related to more uncontrolled eating in impulsive individuals (Booth et al., 2018; Kakoschke\net al., 2015; Kakoschke et al., 2017b). This does not seem to\nextend to individuals with clinically diagnosed binge eating however, who do not show an increased approach bias to food compared to healthy controls (Paslakis et al., 2017). At the other\nend of the spectrum, a decrease or absence of approach bias\ntowards food may help to explain the persistently reduced food\nintake in individuals with anorexia nervosa (Neimeijer et al., 2019;\nPaslakis et al., 2016; for a review see Paslakis et al., 2020). Perhaps most importantly, a recent review has concluded that modification of approach bias can help reduce food consumption,\nthereby supporting a causal role for approach biases within\nnormal and disordered food consumption (Kakoschke et al.,\n2017a). Hence, research has begun to focus on the reliable\nassessment of behavioural biases so they can be measured and\ntargeted for treatment in uncontrolled eaters.\nBehavioural bias to food cues can be quantified using the\napproach-avoidance task (AAT). In the AAT, participants are\nrequired to approach and avoid stimuli from at least one category. An approach bias is inferred if a stimulus category, such\nas food, is approached faster than avoided, and this advantage for\napproach is larger than that of another stimulus category, such\nas office articles (e.g., Lender et al., 2018). It is necessary to\ncompare two stimulus categories to control for stimulus-independent factors, such as differences in muscle strength or posture,\nwhich influence speed of approach and avoidance responses. Next\nto stimulus categories, the used representation of approach and\navoidance (e.g. zooming stimuli, moving a manikin towards\nor away from stimuli, moving stimuli in 3D environment), the\ninput devices (e.g. keyboards, computer mice, touchscreens,\nmotion sensors) and the instruction types (Krieglmeyer & Deutsch,\n2010) affect psychometric properties of AAT implementations.\nTwo different task instructions have been used. In the (more\ntypical) irrelevant-feature AAT (e.g. Brockmeyer et al., 2015;\nKakoschke et al., 2017a), participants must approach or avoid\nstimuli based on a feature of the stimulus that is unrelated\n\nto the bias being measured (e.g. approach stimuli with green\nframe and avoid those with a blue frame); in the relevantfeature AAT (e.g. Brignell et al., 2009; Lender et al., 2018; Meule\net al., 2019), the participants must approach or avoid based on\nthe stimulus category, thereby directing attention to the feature\nfor which there might be an approach bias (e.g. approach food\nstimuli and avoid objects and vice versa). Feature-irrelevant\nAATs are appealing as they do not require an instruction\nswitch halfway through the experiment, and are less susceptible to demand characteristics, thus allowing for stronger claims\nregarding automatic stimulus-response associations (Chen &\nBargh, 1999; Rinck & Becker, 2007). Yet, a meta-analysis including various stimulus categories (e.g. faces and affective words,\nPhaf et al., 2014) as well as direct task comparisons in the food\ndomain suggest that only relevant-feature AATs have yielded\nsignificant and reliable approach biases, whereas biases in\nirrelevant-feature AATs were not significant or unreliable (Lender\net al., 2018; Meule et al., 2019; Phaf et al., 2014). Next to instruction, it matters whether it is the object or the participant that moves\ncloser or further away. In set-ups using a joystick, approach bias\neffects are elicited primarily by the stimuli zooming in or out\n(Krieglmeyer & Deutsch, 2010; Rinck & Becker, 2007) and accordingly, approach bias could be attained when stimuli were moved\nwith simple key presses (Becker et al., 2015; Peeters et al., 2012).\nHowever, it was shown that manipulating a stimulus’ position at\na distance, as simulated with the zoom-feature, elicits smaller\napproach biases than moving oneself towards or away from a\nstimulus in a three-dimensional digital environment (Rougier\net al., 2018). Thus, unclear perspective and irrelevant-feature\ninstructions may explain why only some studies find approach\nbiases to foods or correlations with food craving and eating styles such as restrained and external eating (Brignell et al.,\n2009; Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Lender et al., 2018; Maas et al.,\n2017a; Meule et al., 2019; Veenstra & de Jong, 2010) while\nothers do not (Matheson, 2018; Meule et al., 2020).\nTo improve the assessment of approach bias, we developed a\nnew variant of the AAT. In this ‘hand-AAT’, participants slide\ntheir hand towards or away from a picture on a touchscreen,\nwith movement direction depending on the stimulus category\n(relevant-feature: food vs. object). By moving one’s own, physical hand, there is no metaphoric ambiguity about whether the\nparticipant or the stimulus is moving, and participants perform\nactual ecologically valid approach and avoidance movements\nrather than producing abstract representations of approach and\navoidance using artificial input devices such as joysticks or key\npresses. This task set-up previously yielded reliable approach\nbiases to foods that correlated with explicit desire ratings in a\nhealthy student population (Kahveci et al., 2021), but unfortunately, the order of instruction blocks confounded inter-individual\ndifferences in approach bias: Both in the hand-AAT, as\nwell as in a feature-relevant joystick AAT (Kahveci et al.,\n2021; Wittekind et al., 2021), participants who avoid food and\napproach objects in the first block (inconsistent instructions)\nshowed larger approach biases to food and stronger correlations with food craving than participants who approach food\nand avoid objects in the first block (consistent instructions).\nAdditionally, two previous studies showed that the subjective\n\n\ndesire to eat an individual food item is only correlated with reaction times on approach trials of that individual food item and\nnot with reaction times on respective avoidance trials, making the value of avoid trials in the appetitive domain doubtful\n(Kahveci et al., 2020; Kahveci et al, 2021). Given this, we\nspeculate that block-order may confound bias size as participants gradually master the task and thus only respond in automatic fashion to approach-food trials when they occur in the\nsecond rather than the first block. Such block order effects were\npreviously documented in the domain of implicit associations,\nand remedied through the introduction of multiple alternating blocks (Messner & Vosgerau, 2010). In the current study, we\nthus chose to decrease the temporal primacy of one condition\n(instruction type, i.e., approach food, avoid objects) over\nanother by presenting both conditions thrice and an alternating\nfashion rather than once.\nAs preregistered ([external resource identifier omitted] we expected that the\nhand-AAT would reliably detect a general approach bias to\nall foods in the task, based on the assumption that also low\ndesired foods possess inherently rewarding properties thanks to\ntheir significance for human survival. As an indication of validity, we aimed to replicate the finding that more desired food\nstimuli are approached faster than less desired food stimuli (Kahveci et al., 2021) and included the single category\nimplicit association task (IAT) with approach and avoidance\nwords to relate the AAT to another implicit measure that was\nvalidated for the assessment of motivational tendencies in the\nfood context (Kraus & Piqueras-Fiszman, 2016). Additionally,\nhigher approach bias was hypothesized to relate to more\nself-reported food craving, as a marker of cue reactivity\n(Brockmeyer et al., 2015). To further explore the relationship\nbetween bias scores and subjectively perceived cue-reactivity,\nwe correlated the bias with changes in craving after exposure to foods cues in the hand-AAT, external eating, as well\nas overall liking and desire to eat ratings across food stimuli\n(Brignell et al., 2009; Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Lender et al.,\n2018). To scope validity of this paradigm further, we assessed\nthe relationships between AAT and IAT bias on the one hand and\nrestrained eating and body mass index (BMI) on the other hand,\nas previous studies have argued that dietary lapses in individuals\nwith obesity and in individuals restraining their food intake\nmay be explained by strong implicit approach responses to\nfood cues (Kemps & Tiggemann, 2015; Veenstra & de Jong,\n2010).\n\nMethods\nParticipants\n\nWe recruited 59 students (24 male) of the University of\nSalzburg with announcements during lectures and with flyers on\nsocial media platforms. In a typical AAT trial, participants placed their hand on\na symbol centrally on the screen, and after a random delay\nbetween 300ms and 700ms, a stimulus was displayed on the\ndistal side of the touchscreen. While being unrelated to\neach other, both biases were stronger in individuals with\nhigher external eating. Larger AAT biases were further found\nin participants reporting increases in food craving over the\ncourse of the experiment. We thus demonstrated a relationship\nbetween forms of cue-reactivity as expressed through implicit\napproach responses, cue-induced craving, and eating style.\nYet, individually more desired food items were not approached\nfaster, even though larger AAT biases were related to\nhigher mean food liking, and neither the AAT biases nor the\nIAT biases correlated with state craving, trait craving, restrained\neating, or general desire to eat different foods.\n\n\n\nFigure 4. Scatterplots of significant correlations between AAT bias and a) increase of the FCQ-S from pre to post-AAT, b) body mass\nindex, c) DEBQ-external eating and d) mean food valence.\n\nThe lack of an association between AAT and IAT scores is\nnot an uncommon finding in the eating literature (Maas et al.,\n2017a; Woud et al., 2016) and in implicit bias research more\nbroadly (Pieters et al., 2014), with some researchers even finding\na negative correlation between the two (Larsen et al., 2014;\nWarschburger et al., 2018). These findings suggest that the\nAAT measures the association between stimuli and directional\nmovements, whereas the approach-avoidance IAT measures\nthe association between stimuli and words. The associations\nwith approach-avoidance words do not necessarily overlap with\nactual behavioural tendencies and can directly oppose each other,\nfor example in dieters who have an approach bias towards food\nbut associate it with avoidance-related self-talk (Wiers et al.,\n2017), hence supporting a more ‘verbal’ nature of the IAT, at\nleast relative to the AAT. Despite being unrelated to each other\nin the current study, both tasks were associated with external\neating, the tendency to eat in response to external cues rather\nthan internal ones such as hunger. This suggests that some participants may display external eating due to strong cue-elicited\napproach responses, while others may display external eating\ndue to a more cognitive association between food and\nconsumption, for example due to food-related beliefs and\ncultural norms.\nWe could not replicate the finding that interpersonal differences in the desire to eat individual food items predict approach\nbias for those individual food items (Kahveci et al., 2020;\nKahveci et al., 2021). This may be because this study featured a one-week delay between the desire to eat ratings and\napproach bias measurement, whereas the aforementioned studies collected ratings directly after measurement of approach\n\nbias. The relationship between approach bias and food preferences may thus be momentary, as the desire for specific foods\nchanges within days (Reichenberger et al., 2018) and the relationship between implicit associations and consumption behaviour was only found under high craving and hunger (Richard\net al., 2019). While preferences for specific food items change\nover time, the general tendency to either like or dislike most\nfood types may be more stable (e.g. picky eating; Kauer et al.,\n2015). In line with this, we showed that higher mean food liking relates to stronger approach bias despite the one-week delay.\nOn the most general level, AAT correlates come both from\nstate-domain (e.g. desire for specific foods at that moment) and\ntrait-domain measures (e.g. external eating, general food liking). Future research should thus invest into decomposing validity effect into proximal states and their role in mediating more\ndistal traits.\nOne important methodological step is that the present task\nsuccessfully remedied the confounding effect of block order\non approach bias scores, which was found in the previous\nfeature-relevant AATs (Kahveci et al., 2021; Wittekind et al.,\n2021). Such block order effects introduce differences in participants’ bias scores which are unrelated to the participant’s\ninherent approach bias, and thus reduce the correlations\nbetween the measured bias and external measures. As instruction switches are inevitable in feature-relevant AATs, the\nremoval of block-order effects is pivotal for the further development of this ‘strain’ of AAT methods. In the current study,\nbiases were found regardless of whether the inconsistent or\nconsistent block started the block sequence. Yet, also the bias\nduring the first two or four blocks was unaffected by instruction\n\n\norder and thus, increasing the number of blocks to six was\nnot the only reason block order effect disappeared. Possibly, the current task was easier to master than the previous one,\nwhich reduced the influence of learning effects that would\notherwise slow reaction times during whichever block is presented first. We assume that the current task was easier to\nmaster because stimuli were displayed only in one position\non the screen, whereas the previous study changed the location of the stimuli halfway through each block. Therefore, we\nrecommend an extended practise phase to decrease the artificial differences in behavioural approach bias when it is not\nfeasible to avoid counterbalancing block order.\nReliability of the hand-AAT may seem promising when considering the ‘reliability crisis’ in the broader field of cognitive bias measurement (LeBel & Paunonen, 2011; McNally,\n2019) and was in line with that of another feature-relevant\nAAT in the food domain (Kahveci et al., 2020). However, two\nother feature-relevant AATs using either a joystick or symbolic manikin to approach and avoid stimuli unrelated to food\nattained higher reliability (Krieglmeyer & Deutsch, 2010),\njust like our previous version of the hand-AAT (Kahveci et al.,\n2021). Reliability of this previous AAT may have been numerical higher because the study featured stimuli with a more\nnarrow range of stimulus valence, and because the strong\nblock-order effects likely increased the variability of approach\nbias scores. The reliability and power of the current paradigm\nmay be improved with more trials and with stimulus\nsets standardized on graspability (Baker et al., 2020).\nThe current study is part of a line of research geared towards\nimproving the assessment of food-related approach biases\nthrough the use of manual responses on a touchscreen.\nBlock-order effects that were found in previous studies were\nremediated, and thus a strong confound of bias size was\neliminated in the current task set-up. Accordingly, the bias\nscores attained concurrent validity: larger approach bias related\nto stronger cue-induced craving, external eating and food liking.
