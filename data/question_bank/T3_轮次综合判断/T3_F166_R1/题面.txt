Undisruptive interaction between\nthe sides concerned is not possible. This renders the material\nproperty of impermeability of boundaries of great importance\nin their definitions. It would make a logical extension to consider material properties of boundaries that mitigate impermeability: that readily permit forming and enacting relationships\nacross the boundary without any party actually crossing it.\nThis could be related to various senses, such as vision and\nhearing, which invest windows, hedges, fences, or construction\nmaterial with socio-spatial significance. That notion of closing off from\nthe inside towards the outside immediately derives from the\nrudiments of architectonic space as shelter or a simple cell\n(cf. Appleton, 1975; Hillier & Hanson, 1984; Van der Laan,\n1983) and the occupation of a constructed space by a specific\nsocio-spatial system (cf. Koch, 2005). A material articulation of extracting a discrete space from its surroundings also\n\nexerts a mundane spatial power relation, because the same\nspace cannot be occupied twice (Hägerstrand, 1975; Pred, 1977;\nPred, 1981: neither by construction nor people). Thus, impermeable seclusions dominate the space they occupy and may\ncause spatial morphological associations of dependence with\nsurrounding boundaries. There is an intrinsic interest in entranceways, because they create orientations in how dominant\nspaces are connected to their surroundings by facilitating solicited or voluntary interaction. Doors and gates as entranceways\nhave material properties that afford closeability, effectively\nrendering a stretch of boundary reversibly (im)permeable\n(i.e. the temporary manipulations of opening, closing, and locking a door, cf. Latour’s (1992) wall holes and their reversible\nstate resulting from door hinges).8\nThe boundary line types will distinguish a dominant and a\nsolid dominant, in which the latter refers to a single cell configuration (Hillier & Hanson, 1984) or the single room house.\nWhen considering architectural information in typical town\nplans buildings or open spaces are merely outlined; disregarding internal organisation or design. The prefix ‘solid’ alerts\nus that there is no further differentiation inside, which on the\nflipside permits the mereological possibility of a spatial hierarchy where a closable boundary can enclose a subset of\nboundaries.\nFrom the premise of an inhabited built environment follows\nthat the spaces that emerge from boundaries must be occupiable to a degree of permanence by a specific socio-spatial system pertaining an activity or set of interactions (see Vis, 2009;\ncf. Koch, 2005). This precludes the occurrence of spaces that\ncannot be occupied, even though such spaces do exist within\nintensively developed built environments (e.g. electrical substations, water towers, steep slopes or challenging rocky outcrops,\nbodies of water). Socio-spatially speaking, such boundaries of unoccupiability generate ‘negative spaces’ in the social\ncomplex, which means they are void of occupation, but not\nnecessarily of significance. Therefore, the inclusion of negative boundary line types will ensure that the spatial morphology of built environments can be defined contiguously and\ncomprehensively, thus upholding their ontological claim.\nUltimately, it is essential that each boundary line type definition will be empirically identifiable, be that on town plans or\nwalking around town. Yet, as a consequence of the above\nrequirements and stipulations, a single boundary line type\nidentification is never capable of fully defining and thus redescribing the spatial morphology in terms of rudimentary social\nfunctioning. Koch, 2005). This precludes the occurrence of spaces that\ncannot be occupied, even though such spaces do exist within\nintensively developed built environments (e.g. electrical substations, water towers, steep slopes or challenging rocky outcrops,\nbodies of water). Socio-spatially speaking, such boundaries of unoccupiability generate ‘negative spaces’ in the social\ncomplex, which means they are void of occupation, but not\nnecessarily of significance. Therefore, the inclusion of negative boundary line types will ensure that the spatial morphology of built environments can be defined contiguously and\ncomprehensively, thus upholding their ontological claim.\nUltimately, it is essential that each boundary line type definition will be empirically identifiable, be that on town plans or\nwalking around town. Yet, as a consequence of the above\nrequirements and stipulations, a single boundary line type\nidentification is never capable of fully defining and thus redescribing the spatial morphology in terms of rudimentary social\nfunctioning. While topographical data represent an abstraction of material boundaries based solely in observation of their\nphysical presence, applying BLTs to these boundaries will\nde- and reconstruct them according to empirically recognisable conceptualisations that are no less abstract. Information\nselection and abstraction is exactly what renders mappings\nuseful (see MacEachren, 2004; Monmonier, 1996; Wood,\n1992) and similarly the conceptual ontology that emerges\nfrom the identifications of BLTs is not designed to express\nthe entire concrete truth (cf. Sayer, 1981). We recognise\nthat boundaries should always be considered from two\nsides and that there may be mereological relationships and\ntopological specifications that result from their material and\nmorphological properties. Therefore, it becomes an expectation that the significance of spatial morphology to the\ninhabitation of built environments is only revealed through\ncombinations of BLTs operating along the same boundary.\nThe fact that boundaries do not become defined by a single act\nof defining also stresses their emergent logic and complexity.\nThe BLT definitions are presented accompanied by photographs\nof street scenes in aid of improving understanding of the\nformal analytical language used. These photographs allow me to\ndemonstrate their general applicability unencumbered by the\nparticularities associated with using topographical data which\nrequire knowledge of their original purpose and construction\n(see Vis, 2014b).\n\nBoundary Line Type (BLT) definitions\nThis section defines and discusses the thirteen BLTs (numbered\nand named) that I currently recognise. The initial order is\ndetermined by departing from the simplest occurrence of\nseclusion generating a discrete space: the solid dominant\n(cf. Hillier & Hanson’s (1984) single cell configuration). As\nsuch the numbering is generally representative of the iterative\nabstraction process (cf. Pratt, 1995; Sayer, 1981; Sayer, 2000;\n\nYeung, 1997) through which they have been identified and do\nnot express any further meaning. The BLTs have also been\nnamed in active voice to express their role in the inhabitation\nof the built environment. In line with iterative abstraction, their\nontological conceptualisation is deemed practically adequate\nby having been tested through several instances of application\n(Vis, 2014b; Vis, 2018a), but remains forever open to revision and refutation depending on empirical findings. Note\nthat no BLT need to occur in any given built environment\nand that only through their relations do they describe\nboundaries that compose the spatial morphology of inhabited\nbuilt environments. Since the language used should avoid being\nsocio-culturally prescriptive, the comprehensive definitions\n(first presented in Vis, 2018a: 141–158) may come across\nformulaic, complex, and descriptive. To aid understanding and\nto promote their use, in this paper I reproduce their abridged\ndefinitions in Table 1 (adapted from Vis, 2018a: supplement), followed by a discussion based on a set of annotated\nphotographic illustrations.\nThe ontological formulations of the BLTs, based on how\nboundaries operate, ensure they are useful in comparative\napplication along the lines of Kropf’s (2009, paraphrased)\ninsistence on a consistent, coherent and comprehensive set\nof definitions, which are general enough to be used comparatively in all thinkable contexts, yet specific enough\nto be identified as analytical units in the empirical reality\nof datasets. When applied, the BLTs will still resemble the\nspatial entities that socio-culturally specific or commonplace\ncategories in geographical data (e.g. house, street, garden, etc.\nas indicated in the final column of Table 1) recognise, but these\nentities will have been redescribed. As such it is capable of unburdening common mappings and (institutional) geographical data from\npredetermined, socio-culturally specific, use-space conflations.\n9\n\nUnocupiability indicates that the surface cannot be inhabited or structurally utilised by a residing socio-spatial system. Steep slopes\nmight be made tractable by stairs or funiculars. When physical evidence\nsuggests a surface is rendered occupiable, other BLTs could apply.\n\nFigure 12. Boundary Line Type 13 in context.\n\nTo that end it proposes a formulaic language or codification\nof how spatial morphology matters to social life at a rudimentary level. What results are emergent formal socio-spatially\nthick redescriptions of the spatial morphology that organises\nbuilt environments. Even though the BLT definitions are fixed,\nalbeit suspended in their practical adequacy (sensu critical\nrealism), for any given case the combinations and distributions in which they occur cannot be predicted. This means\nthat while we can approach any given place (inhabited built\nenvironment) with BLTs, we may not necessarily find the\nsame combinations of BLTs in each place and we certainly\nwould expect them to differ in their frequency and distribution.\nThis in turn permits particular kind of boundaries to play more\n\ndominant roles from one place to the next (see case demonstrations in Vis, 2018a). In Yaneva’s (2012: 4) words, BLTs\nhelp ‘to describe [and make explicit] the local treatments of\nthe universal’.\nAmin (2008: 12) posits that the ‘placement of time through\nmaterialization [...], repetition and rhythmic regularity [...],\nand juxtaposition [...] is its taming.’ As theoretical constructs, BLTs express a useful material reification of the both\nessential and commonplace constitutive role of boundaries in\nsocial life from which built environments develop. Precisely\nthrough their experiential and social constitutive theoretical\nfoundations – the principles of differentiation and subdivision\n\n\nand the operation of seclusion – BLTs make a natural\nconceptual partner for long-term comparative studies on how\nbuilt form develops. The materialisation of BLTs represents a\nstate of establishment immediately followed by negotiation,\nmodification, and potentially transformation as afforded by their\nproperties (see Vis, 2013; Vis, 2018a). We appreciate ‘that\nboundaries may be both contested and consensual, depending on the perspectives and interests of the parties involved,\n[which] leads to a more dialectical understanding of their\nnature and transformations’ (Rodman & Cooper, 1996: 94).\nIn other words, these boundary concepts provide the capacity\nfor systematic social investigations of built environments as\nthey develop, considering both the deep past of settlements and\nexamples of vanished cultures. BLTs will help archaeologists to\n‘engage conceptually with work in other disciplines to make\neffective ancient-modern comparisons, and […] to analyze\n(or reanalyze) our data so that we can address the topics and\nconcepts of interest’ (Smith & Peregrine, 2012: 15; see Vis,\n2018a). Through a developmental lens, spatial morphology\nitself becomes a necessary constitutive part of social phenomena. Allowing the significance of the material presence\nof spatial morphology as a causal power in structuring social\nlife and events avoids that social insights become largely\ndetached from the space they originate in (cf. Zimmermann,\n2012). Vice versa, conceptual ontologies of inhabited built\nenvironments arising from BLT applications will help to\nmitigate what Batty (2009: 194) describes as ‘considerable\nconfusion about the way that the physical structure relates to\nhuman behaviour.’\nIn ensuring that BLTs’ theoretical foundations acknowledge\nthat the materialisation of boundaries represent a time-space\nspecific state, we understand that the repetition, rhythmic regularity, and juxtaposition of the occurrence of boundaries in\nthe built environment do not ‘tame’ time. ‘Boundaries are\nnot merely artifacts of the differentiation of places. They are\nintrinsically related to the actions they contain’ (Rotenberg,\n1996: 56). In fact, their causal powers will co-determine what\nwill happen next. So, we might recognise BLTs as an ‘underlabourer’ for spatially conscious social research, akin Pratt’s\n(1995) description of critical realism as an ‘underlabourer’\nfor the research process.\nAs shown through the photographic illustrations, BLTs can be\nused simply as a transferable vehicle to think with and organise studies about events, activities, and land-use occurring in\nspace or spatial development processes. Photographs offer\nan accessible approximation of lived experience. It is worth\nbearing in mind that ‘a photograph provides an instance of\na phenomenon, not the essence of a phenomenon’ (Hutson,\n2012: 290). When annotating the BLTs we augment perceived\nreality by making the transferable essence of a material\n(social-spatial-physical) phenomenon explicit. The photographic\nillustrations demonstrate that through an individual’s perspective, (view and interaction), one’s experience and understanding\nof the nature of boundaries can remain limited. Inhabitants\nwould get to know boundaries by moving through space and\nbuilding up experience with negotiating and engaging with\n\nboundaries over time, and can be expected to interpret partial\nperception of boundaries according to their previous experience\nof similar situations.\nIn contrast, when BLTs are applied to maps, this affords us a\ntotalising ‘god’s view’ (Aitken & Craine, 2006; Monmonier,\n1996; Morton et al. 2014; Vis, 2018b; Wood, 1992) through\nwhich BLTs act as a heuristic device to elucidate the significance of the material presence of spatial morphology to\nrudimentary social functioning. It is worth emphasising that\ntheir empirical identifiability following from their formal\ndefinitions permits the BLTs to be used in quantitative geographical analyses (Vis, 2018a). However, since their definitions\ndo rely on human and expert judgment their identification\nremains interpretive, as is, to some extent, judging which\ndetails from geographical input data to include or exclude.\nThis reflects Galison (1998: 341), who emphasises: ‘On the\nside of the observer, there is a peculiarly human ability to\nseize patterns, and therefore to classify even when our algorithmic forms of reasoning fail. Subjectivity becomes an\nimportant feature of classification because the objects do not\nhold universal essential properties and because it is within\nour species’ nature to be able to classify univalently.’ While\nthe BLTs themselves are argued to express essential and\nuniversal properties occurring within spatial morphology, their\napplication currently relies on the judgments of Galison’s\n‘empirically artistic’ human mind.\nThe proposed BLTs lay a foundation for future research and\nwelcome triangulation or enrichment with other data, methods,\nand interpretations. The critical realist underpinning of the\ndefinitions should be regarded as an invitation to adapt and\napply according to individual research objectives and data\nneeds. Through applications and adaptations, the ontological\nredescriptions of built environments using BLTs should contribute a comparative base to themes that are socially, culturally,\ncognitively, emotively, or architecturally specific.
