      "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nCollaborative Workspaces are rapidly growing and evolving across the\nworld. Traditionally understood as an urban phenomenon, most\nresearch understands them as either ‘entrepreneurial-led’, as profitdriven and commercial spaces such as business incubators and\naccelerators, or ‘community-led’ as being bottom-up, not-for-profit\nventures aimed at catering for the needs of their community. Methods applied\nwere: semi-structured interviews (N=28), participant observation and\nfocus groups (2).\n\n\nO\n\nResults\nCommunity-led rural collaborative workspaces hold transformative\npotential from i) their ability to assist rural actors with their capacities\nand realizing their desires and ii) changing individual subjectivities\ntowards collective. Coworking spaces,\ncreative hubs, hackerspaces, fab labs etc host nowadays more\nthan 5 million coworkers worldwide (ergonomictrends.com).\nAlthough primarily an urban phenomenon, CWS have also\nstarted to spread in medium and small cities in the last few\nyears, away from urban agglomerations (Avdikos & Merkel,\n2020). Recent research suggests that 66% of CWS are located\nin urban regions, while the rest (34%) are in intermediate\nor rural regions (Marmo & Avdikos, 2024). CWS usually\nprovide workspaces to freelancers, paid employees, digital\nnomads and remote workers in general, but also, they facilitate professional encounters and networking possibilities\nbetween different people and they can also act as mediums for\ncultural exchanges. Especially, CWS in small cities and\ntowns can play the role of a socio-cultural center where locals\ncan engage through new participatory modes in different\nkinds of projects that benefit their localities and that can\neventually end up in social innovation (hereafter SI) processes and projects. The paper aims in unpacking the role of\nCWS as initiators and facilitators of SI processes in rural\nareas, looking at two community led CWS in Western Greece\nand Upper Austria.\nAccording to Moulaert et al. (2017) SI is i) a way of understanding a wide range of activities and practices oriented to\naddressing and providing solutions to specific social\nproblems or meeting human needs. Moreover, SI ii) involves\nchanging relations through the adoption of new social practices, institutional arrangements and/or forms of participation. This means that SI does not separate means from\nends, but treats needs and problems as inherent in social relations. However, the effects of SI extend beyond the immediate\nmeeting of needs. SI seems to improve long term opportunities for individuals and communities, through producing\nmore efficient, effective and sustainable means for society\nto deal with its challenges, and that iii) can have a deeper\ntransformative impact over communities and society.\nThrough the analysis of qualitative data, mainly through interviews and participant observation, we delve into the multiple\nsets of relations found in these two rural CWS and attempt\nto unpack the ways these arrangements cover local needs\nand at the same time they foster new social relations. Moreover, we engage with the literature in unpacking the ways\nCWS can also cause a transformative effect on social relations towards a post growth/post-capitalist society, challenging the ways local institutions work. To do so, a novel\ntheoretical framework, consisting of Diverse and Community\nEconomies and Assemblage Thinking will be elaborated upon.\n\nSocial innovation, assemblages and diverse\neconomies: a literature review\n\nThe use of SI over the last 30 years has increased almost\nexponentially (Van der Have & Rubalcaba, 2016). Within\nacademia the term’s meaning varies across fields such as\n\ninnovation studies, management studies, sustainable and\nterritorial development (Howaldt et al., 2014). This wide\narray of understandings is said to both be of help (Moulaert &\nMacCallum, 2019) and hinderance (Bock, 2012) to understanding the ‘fuzzy’ concept that is SI (ibid.). While not providing an exhaustive literature review of SI, this section\nwill serve to unravel SI, while positioning the paper within\nthe literature and allowing for a reconceptualization of\nthe concept through Diverse and Community Economies\n(Gibson-Graham, 2008).\nSI seems a ‘contested’ concept (Ayob et al., 2016; Marques\net al., 2018). On the one hand ‘social’ innovation can be\nunderstood as social entrepreneurship whereby social entrepreneurs come up with innovative ideas to address society’s\nmost pressing challenges, while the other approach is more\nunderstood through collective action and the social and solidarity economy. We want to highlight the capitalocentric nature\n(Gibson-Graham, 2008) of the entrepreneurial understandings\nof SI, and aim to demonstrate how SI processes work beyond\nan organization, or individual entrepreneur. While not reducing the role they can have in these processes, we argue that\nSI is also not reducible to one actor, but it operates as a\nSI assemblage, as a whole of different actors that have a\ncollective desire in addressing social problems.\nWithin the capitalocentric strand, SI is understood as a technocratic, entrepreneurial/market driven complement to the\nneoliberal state (e.g. Mulgan, 2006; Murray et al., 2010). Much\nof the focus is on individual entrepreneurship and mainstream\ninnovation approaches to ‘solving’ society’s most pressing\nproblems. Here, SI is understood as ‘an idea of a need that\nisn’t being met, coupled with an idea of how it could be met’\n(Mulgan, 2006: 149) or more specifically “new ideas\n(products, services and models) that simultaneously meet\nsocial needs and create new social relationships or collaborations. In other words, they are innovations that are both good\nfor society and enhance society’s capacity to act.” (Murray\net al., 2010: 3). Such understandings promote a capitalocentric worldview, whereby civil society is viewed as an\nuntapped source of innovative capacity that can be harnessed\ntowards creating new markets and economic value. Such\nunderstandings have also pierced policy documents, with SI\nseen allowing governments to ‘do more with less’ (BEPA,\n2014), increasing growth and competition while addressing\nsociety’s most pressing problems (Fougère et al., 2017).\nSeveral scholars critique this capitalocentric discourse, generally as it proliferates both neoliberal governance and subject production (Bragaglia, 2021; Fougère et al., 2017;\nJessop et al., 2013; Swyngedouw, 2005). In these interpretations, SI is understood as quick-fix solutions to social problems\nthrough the creation of active economical and entrepreneurial subjects for a capitalist economy (Arampatzi, 2022;\nFougère et al., 2017; Jessop et al., 2013), while shifting social\nand environmental costs from the state to citizens (Fougère\net al., 2017; Swyngedouw, 2005).\n\n\n\nMoving to the more critical strand of the literature, it has\nbeen developed over the last 30 years by Frank Moulaert\nand colleagues (e.g. Moulaert et al., 2010; Moulaert et al.,\n2013), who developed SI as a concept vis-à-vis neoliberal\npractices and policies, originally understood as an alternative\nmodel of local development (Moulaert et al., 2005). With\nits roots in new social movements (Godin, 2012; Martinelli,\n2010), SI is understood not as an organizational form, but as\nethical spatial projects, by changing social relations towards\nmeeting human needs and not as a gap in the market to\nbe addressed. Moulaert & Mehmood (2020: 17), portray\nhow many SI’s are rooted in socio-ecological movements, as\nconsequences of increasing privatization, climate crisis\nand increasing social inequalities. Examples include the\nnew-municipalist movements (e.g. in Madrid and Barcelona),\nthe creation of new urban and rural commons, agroecological initiatives, local development experiments aiming to\nmove past the market-based economy etc.\nThus, SI is broadly understood as innovation in social\nrelations by meeting human needs (Moulaert et al., 2013). SI is\nunderstood as both a practice (collective satisfaction of\nhuman needs) and a process (changes in social relations, collective empowerment) (Moulaert & Mehmood, 2020). Part\nof our contribution is through understanding the underlying\nprocesses that lead to collective action, SI can be understood\nas a means of opening new economic subjectivities and\npost-capitalist possibilities (Gibson-Graham, 2008; Wright,\n2010) which we would like to conceptualize as the transformative potential of SI, building through Assemblage thinking,\nas the analytical tool.\n\nTransformative SI\nDespite the split in SI literature, there is a nascent literature regarding SI and societal transformation, that transcends\nthe binary (Moulaert & MacCallum, 2019). An important\ncontribution to its theory has been that of the EU funded\nTRANSIT project to develop a theory on transformative\nsocial innovation (e.g. Avelino et al., 2019; Haxeltine et al.,\n2016; Pel et al., 2020). Here, transformative SI is understood\nas a “process, through which social innovations challenge,\nalter and/or replace established (and/or dominant) institutions\nin the social-material context” (Haxeltine et al., 2016: 19).\nThe theoretical framework of the project comes from sustainability transitions thinking and conceptualizes transformative\nSI through the Multi-Level Perspective framework (MLP)\n(Geels, 2004), whereby SI’s need to adapt to dominant rule\nsets in society and adapt to institutional logics (such as state\nregulations or collaborating with institutions) to become institutionalized and have a transformative impact in society (Pel\net al., 2020). Despite the value however, there are several\nconceptual limitations on this approach, that are generally\nexistent in most transitions’ literature. Firstly, there is a lack\nof critique regarding capitalism (Chatterton, 2016; Törnberg,\n2021) and secondly the MLP privileges structure and hierarchy, reproducing the very power relations responsible for\nthe need for social change to take place (Gillard et al., 2016).\n\nIn contrast, coming from an anti-essentialist and relational\nontology inspired by Deleuze and Guattari and Gibson-Graham,\nwe aim to overcome such limitations. It is\nthis process of subject formation for the community economy and opening up of economic diversity in the economy\nwe would like to stress as the desired transformation for\npost-capitalism, without stressing an end product, but process, struggle and deliberation (Gibson-Graham & Roelvink,\n2009).\nFurthermore, in Real Utopias Olin Wright (2010) develops\na robust theoretical framework for post-capitalist transformation through envisaging real utopias - ‘proposals for\npragmatically improving our institutions. Instead of indulging in utopian dreams we must accommodate to practical\nrealities.’ (p. 4). Wright identifies three main ‘pathways’ for\nsocial transformation: radical ‘ruptural’ transformation through\nwide-scale revolt against state and market, while bringing in\nradical alternatives; ‘symbiotic’ transformation is through long\nterm co-operation with existing structures and institutions\n(e.g. trade unions); while ‘interstitial’ transformations take a\nprefigurative approach, by creating future alternatives in the\nhere and now. Regarding SI and post-capitalist transformations,\ninterstitial transformations aid us in their conceptualization,\n\n\n\nby seeking to create new forms of social empowerment\nwithin the niches and margins of capitalism, while building\nsocial power through civil society, they mirror many features\nof the grassroots conceptions of SI.\nTo give these re-conceptualizations of SI further analytical\npower, we draw on Assemblage thinking (Deleuze & Guattari,\n1980). The main contribution of assemblage is analysing\nsocio-spatial phenomena and understanding them relationally\nas dynamic wholes, in constant flux, or becoming. Rather\nthan their essential qualities, it gives priority to relations and\nqualities, enabling a mode of analysis where we can compare bodies through their given affective relations, (Buchanan,\n1997). The term ‘assemblage’ is a translation from the\nFrench - agencement - originally applied by Deleuze and\nGuattari to signify the capacity to act. Agencement holds\nmore meaning than the English ‘assemblage’, including to\narrange, to dispose to fit up, to combine, to order (Law, 2004:\n41). Therefore, while assemblages are made up of material,\nexpressive and symbolic components, we must look at them\nbeyond a collection of things but their interaction, arrangement, ordering and (dis)assembly. Moreover, we must look\nbeyond the components themselves, which are just the “props\nneeded to actualize a particular arrangement of desire.”\n(Buchanan, 1997: 65), thus assemblages are desire driven.\nFollowing other approaches drawing from Deleuze & Guattari\nin trying to understand SI, we want to highlight the rhizomatic nature of social relations (Pel et al., 2020; Scott-Cato\n& Hillier, 2010). Rather than essentializing SI processes, we\nview changing social relations as a non-linear, rhizomatic process that occurs through the culmination of everyday actions,\nwithout a necessary beginning or end. Rhizomatic change is\na micropolitics of becoming, that seeks to open new possibilities and multiplicities, towards post-capitalist futures. We\nwant to “focus on process rather than on product, on struggle\nand deliberation rather than on an image of a predefined\ncollective identity or geographical locality.” (Gibson-Graham\net al., 2020: 5).\nMoreover, throughout the analysis we will draw heavily\nfrom the concept of affect, understood as the capacity to\naffect and be affected. When bodies enter affective relations with one another, it refers to those ‘encounters’ that may\naugment, or diminish their respective capacities (Slaby &\nMühlhoff, 2019). This is central to our assemblage analysis,\nas we will demonstrate how various (human and non-human,\nmaterial and expressive) component parts affect the assemblage and its capacities. This autonomy of parts can also be\nreferred to as the exteriority of relations (DeLanda, 2006).\nAffective labour (Hardt, 1999) understood as the production\nand manipulation of affects, will also be essential to our\nunderstanding of the CWS role in organizing social relations,\nand subject formation, as individual capacities are made,\nunmade and re-made (Slaby & Mühlhoff, 2019).\n\nCWS\n\nCWS refers to spaces, which are primarily work purposed environments, but they can also be places where people come\ntogether for both work and other personal activities in a\nshared space such as coworking spaces, makerspaces, fablabs, business incubators and accelerators, social and cultural\ncentres etc. (Schmidt & Brinks, 2017). CWS have been\ndescribed as sites of ambivalence, demonstrating tensions\nbetween both corporate and counter-corporate, capitalistic and\ncommoning, collaborative and individualistic ways of being\n(Bandinelli & Arvidsson, 2013; De Peuter et al., 2017;\nVidaillet & Bousalham, 2020). This contrast displays the\nplurality of CWS, the term may relate to work collectives,\nco-operatives for freelancers and other forms in response to\nprecarious markets, which operate with values of the social\nand solidarity economy; or alternatively CWS may act as\nbusiness incubators or accelerators for start-ups, along with\ncorporate chains of coworking (Arvidsson, 2020; Brooke\net al., 2014; De Peuter et al., 2017). Contributions generally\ndifferentiate between entrepreneurial-led vs community-led\nCWS (Avdikos & Iliopoulou, 2019; Avdikos & Merkel, 2020),\nwith the latter also referred to as resilient CWS (Gandini &\nCossu, 2021). While both types overlap in their activities and\nfunctions (Avdikos & Pettas, 2021), community led CWS\ngenerally are not-for-profit, and have an authentic concern\nfor their community (Avdikos & Iliopoulou, 2019).\nMoreover, there is a growing number of contributions referring to the political economy of CWS, and their potential for\nfostering alternative economic practices (e.g. Smith (2020);\nVidaillet & Bousalham, 2020. While not always offering a\ncomplete alternative to the market, it appears that a number\nof community led CWS may oscillate between the market\nand the commons (Avdikos & Pettas, 2021). These CWS thus can be used as\n\n\n\na tool to foster economic diversity (Gibson-Graham, 2008;\nSmith, 2020) by engaging in simultaneous capitalist, alternative and non-capitalist practices, and they can act as ‘syntopias’\nfor their communities (Vidaillet & Bousalham, 2020).\n\nMethods\n\nThe research applied a qualitative case study approach (Yin,\n2018). A key informant\n(the manager of the CWS) was used to gain access to the\nCWS, local people and various activities and events that took\nplace during the stay. This is emancipatory and transformative as it\ndestabilizes what Deleuze and Guattari describe as the\nmachinic unconscious, unlocking a revolution of micropolitical\nbecoming in those that encounter the CWS’s. Rather than\nbeing subjected to machinic enslavement and inertia, the\nCWS’s offer liberation from the circuits of the machines at\nplay in both localities, be it through the ‘left-behindness’ in\nGreece or lack of purpose in Austria. The use of affective\nlabour has transformative potential, as it creates collective\nsubjectivities, social networks, community, and biopower\nfrom below (Hardt, 1999).\n\nDiscussion: the transformative potential of CWS\n\nThe third dimension of SI, according to Moulaert &\nMacCallum (2019) is ‘socio-political transformative potential’. We would like to understand the type of transformation\nthe CWS’s enact is enabling ‘intentional economies’ in the\nwords of Gibson-Graham (2008) or ‘real utopias’ as Wright\nemphasised (2010). Both represent open ended post-capitalist\nrealities, by facilitating pluriversality (Escobar, 2021) or economic diversity (Gibson-Graham, 2008), working towards creating many different worlds, rather than the perceived dominant\ncapitalist world, destabilizing its perceived hegemony.\nRather than providing an exact post-capitalist strategy, the\nCWS provide a “multitude of possibilities of what could come\n\nafter, as well as building daily competences to leverage social\nchange.” (Chatterton, 2016: 405)”. Moving beyond certainty,\nwe aim to provide one potential pathway of many possibilities towards creating such a future. To do so, we would like\nto infuse Wright’s thinking with that of DCE, understanding\nsocial transformation as post-capitalism, and Wright’s three\ntypes of transformation, as three stages of transformation.\nAs highlighted in the literature review section of the paper,\nWright provides three pathways towards social transformation – ruptural, interstitial and symbiotic. Interstitial transformation may be the social transformation most closely linked\nto grassroots SI, referring to the ‘various kinds of processes\nthat occur in the spaces and cracks within some dominant\nsocial structure of power’ (p. 229). Wright gives examples of\ninterstitial transformations such as various forms of co-ops,\nworkers councils, community based social economy organizations etc., which are also common examples of SI. Such\ninitiatives offer viable alternatives to state and capitalist\nways of organizing, however, are often critiqued as operating\nin isolation, whereby they do not offer a real viable alternative, but merely as heterotopias that operate within ‘niche’\nor ‘protective spaces’ (Smith & Raven, 2012). This is what\nwe conceptualize as our first level of transformation, which\nwe can clearly see as the two CWS’s work towards satisfying\ncommunity needs and build community economies, through\ndesiring production and building affective relations.\nBoth spaces act as heterotopias, or syntopias (Vidaillet &\nBousalham, 2020), that allow users to experience other ways\nof being in the world. While both hubs offer this, CUA is\nmore explicit about its interstitial function of the space – “we\nhave, kind of research Lab to try out things outside of the\nnormal economic, a free space where it’s not based on the\nnormal ongoing system status and that was one of the basic\nideas with CUA to create the spaces, to create ideas. Maybe\nfor the future. Maybe it is quite small, but maybe change\nsomething if we need.” (CUA co-founder, interview).\nRegarding symbiotic transformations, Wright differentiates\nthem from interstitial mainly due to their orientation towards\nthe state, which they use as a mediating body to reduce capitalist power, with the main example given as class compromise, where workers and capitalist firms negotiate through\nthe state over workers’ rights, pay etc. However, we would\nlike to offer a different conceptualization of symbiotic transformation, as a second stage of interstitial transformation\ndiffusion, as they influence other spatiotemporal arrangements. This may initially be in antagonism, and lead to\ntheir practices being copied/replicated by state or municipal\norganizations, or it may mean these institutions recognising and supporting interstitial transformations as important\nsocial economy actors. In both cases we also observe this level\nof transformation, with long term institutional collaboration\nof CUA and the municipality and more recently in WGH as\nthey have begun to collaborate with the new municipality\nthrough organizing a public consultation regarding the town’s\ndevelopment. Moreover, both CWS also offer their resources\n\n\n\nand capacities to local actors such as schools, local businesses and other market entities, NGOs, and other local\nassociations, blending DCE economies with the market economy. While interstitial transformation through the DCE\nframework is usually performed inside the CWS, symbiotic\ntransformation happens outside the bounded environment\nof CWS and offers to other entities and the state to experience the alternative socio-economic arrangements, through the\nDCE framework that CWS’s assemblages perform.\nWithin SI literature, SI is often deemed to be transformative\nwhen it pierces this institutional level (Haxeltine et al., 2016;\nMoulaert & MacCallum, 2019), when SI bottom-up initiatives are supported by institutions, it is referred to as\nbottom-linked SI that “develops when citizens’ collective initiatives result in agreements with local institutions that enable\nand sustain such initiatives through sound, regulated and\nlasting practices” (Garcia et al., 2015: 93). This symbiotic\napproach has many merits and is key for SI’s transformative\npotential to move beyond their space in the margins. However, SI being recognized by institutions does not equate to\nthe neoliberal rhetoric by governments over the last twenty\nyears (for example see Fougère et al., 2017 for a full discussion), something that ‘limits social innovation to marginal\nchange’ (Bartels, 2017). Initiatives need to be given resources\nand power rather than just responsibility (Bragaglia, 2021).\nMoreover, through symbiosis it allows us to move beyond\nthe binary of initiatives either rejecting institutions or\nbeing co-opted by them, allowing for real change to be enacted\nand further institutionalized (Bartels, 2017).\nFigure 1 demonstrates how as DCE practices are performed and\npracticed, moving from the edifices and margins as interstitial\n\ntransformations, to piercing the institutional dimension, and\nbeyond we envisage CE practices spreading in a form of contagion\nuntil they reach a saturation point of post-capitalist becoming, signifying not necessarily a straight clean break from capitalism to\npost-capitalism, but a revolution of becoming, as different\naspects of life become recoded and DCE are heavily territorialized and enacted in society. Both cases do not reflect this\nlevel of transformation (yet, at least), but an example of such\na ‘real utopia’ would be the widely documented case of\nMondragon in the Basque country, with the co-operative\nmodel completely institutionalized in the region. While as an\nexample it is highly contextual and non-replicable, it gives\nan example of possibility (Gibson-Graham, 2008). We cannot state this is the case for either case studies, both remain\nstill in their symbiotic stage. The passage to a ruptural transformation requires CWS to be supported by various local and\nextra-local actors to be acknowledged as the only alternative and post-capitalist solution, where DCE can become the\ndominant mode of production and gain a hegemonic place\nin society.\n\nConclusion\n\nThe paper discussed the role of CWS as initiators and facilitators of SI processes in two community led CWS in two\nrural areas in Austria and Greece. The research methodology\nemployed a qualitative case study approach, utilizing participant observation and semi-structured interviews to explore\nthe multiple sets of relations found in these rural CWS, aiming to understand how these arrangements cover local needs\nand foster new social relations. The findings reveal that\nCWS play a significant role in addressing social needs and\nfacilitating grassroots SI practices, creating affective relations\nthat contribute to the transformation of individual to collective subjectivities. CWS seem to provide a site where the\nunmet needs of the local people are negotiated, redefined\nand are covered in a co-creative and collective manner. This\nis happening in a DCE framework, where affective labour\nmostly performs.\nCWS have a significant impact on individual subjectivities\nby transforming them into collective subjectivities through\naffective relations and desiring production, through the\nimplementation of collaborative projects. The CWS play a crucial role in changing social relations and increasing affects\nand capacities towards community interests, which is an essential part of becoming post-capitalist subjects. They provide\nopportunities for actors to affect and be affected, increasing their agency and assisting them with their own desires\nby providing resources such as space, knowledge, materials,\nnetworks, and equipment. This process enables desiring\nproduction and creates non-alienated and significant relationships with the world and with others, which is essential\nfor subject formation and the development of collective\nsubjectivities.\n\nFigure 1. How Diverse and Community Economy practices\ndiffuse throughout the three levels of transformation.\n\nFurthermore, the affective relations formed within CWS’s\nhave a transformative potential, as they create new collaborations and relationships in places where the narrative that\n\n\nnothing is happening. These affective relations also extend to\nthe larger community in a symbiotic manner, creating positive\naffects for the town and fostering collective action and affective labor to create a window into what could be for the\ncommunity. Additionally, the CWS’s influence other larger\nassemblages through the concept of relations of exteriority,\nallowing them to ‘infiltrate’ other more strictly coded and\nhighly territorialized assemblages, such as schools and\nmunicipalities, while maintaining their autonomy.\nFuture research could explore similar community led CWS\nassemblages in other contexts, including outside of Europe, to\nsee what we can learn and how their transformative potential can be institutionalized. Furthermore, both cases were\nmainly led by an individual, who had a significant impact\non the overall assemblage, regarding the resources they\nput into it and their decision making. Their power seems to\nhave a (dis)empowering (Avelino et al., 2019) impact on\nthe overall assemblage, as despite their energy and intensity\nincreasing its affects, it could sometimes be overwhelming\nfor other participants, causing one staff member in the Greek\ncase to leave. Thus, future research could focus on more\nrhizomatic forms of SI initiatives, with less control of one\nindividual, and how it differs to these SI assemblages.\n\nEthics and consent\nConsent\n\nInterview Participants were given an information sheet\nabout the study and were required to sign a consent form\nprior to being interviewed. In these interpretations, SI\nis understood as quick-fix solutions to social problems through\nthe creation of active economical and entrepreneurial subjects for a capitalist economy (Arampatzi, 2022; Fougère et al.,\n2017; Jessop et al., 2013), while shifting social and environmental costs from the state to citizens (Fougère et al., 2017;\nSwyngedouw, 2005).\nMoving to the more critical strand of the literature, developed\nover the last 30 years primarily by Frank Moulaert and colleagues (e.g. Moulaert et al., 2010; Moulaert et al., 2013), who\n\ndeveloped SI as a concept vis-à-vis neoliberal practices and\npolicies, originally understood as an alternative model of local\ndevelopment (Moulaert et al., 2005). With its roots in new social\nmovements (Godin, 2012; Martinelli, 2010), SI is understood\nnot as an organizational form, but as ethical spatial projects,\nby changing social relations towards meeting human needs\nand not as a gap in the market to be addressed. Moulaert &\nMehmood (2020: 17), portray how many SI’s are rooted in\nsocio-ecological movements, as consequences of increasing\nprivatization, climate crisis and increasing social inequalities.\nExamples include the new-municipalist movements (e.g. in\nMadrid and Barcelona), the creation of new urban and rural commons, agroecological initiatives, local development experiments\naiming to move past the market-based economy etc.\nThus, SI is broadly understood as innovation in social relations by meeting human needs (Moulaert et al., 2013). SI is\nunderstood as both a practice (collective satisfaction of human\nneeds) and a process (changes in social relations, collective\nempowerment) (Moulaert & Mehmood, 2020). Part of our contribution is through understanding the underlying processes that\nlead to collective action, SI can be understood as a means of\nopening new economic subjectivities and post-capitalist possibilities (Gibson-Graham, 2008; Wright, 2010) which we\nwould like to conceptualize as the transformative potential of\nSI, building through Assemblage thinking, as the analytical and\nmethodological tool.\n\n2.2. Collaborative Workspaces\nCWS refers to spaces, which are primarily work purposed environments, but they can also be places where people come\ntogether for both work and other personal activities in a shared\nspace such as coworking spaces, makerspaces, fablabs, business incubators and accelerators, social and cultural centres etc.\n(Schmidt & Brinks, 2017). CWS have been described as sites of\nambivalence, demonstrating tensions between both corporate\nand counter-corporate, capitalistic and commoning, collaborative and individualistic ways of being (Bandinelli & Arvidsson,\n2013; De Peuter et al., 2017; Vidaillet & Bousalham,\n2020). For example, the term may relate to work collectives,\nco-operatives for freelancers and other forms in response to\nprecarious markets, which operate with values of the social\nand solidarity economy; or alternatively CWS may act as business incubators or accelerators for start-ups, along with corporate chains of coworking (Arvidsson, 2020; Brooke et al., 2014;\nDe Peuter et al., 2017). Contributions generally differentiate\nbetween entrepreneurial-led vs community-led CWS (Avdikos\n& Iliopoulou, 2019; Avdikos & Merkel, 2020), with the latter also referred to as resilient CWS (Gandini & Cossu,\n2021). While both types overlap in their activities and functions (Avdikos & Pettas, 2021), community led CWS generally are not-for-profit and have an authentic concern for their\ncommunity (Avdikos & Iliopoulou, 2019).\nSuch tensions between collectivity and entrepreneurship also\nreflect how CWS engage with SI. For example, more entrepreneurial and individualistic understandings are demonstrated by\nthe franchise Impact Hub, that acts as an incubator for\n\n\n‘changemakers’ such as social entrepreneurs and socially orientated organizations. On the other hand, there is a growing number\nof contributions referring to the political economy of CWS, and\ntheir potential for fostering alternative economic practices.\nFor example, Sandoval (2016) demonstrates how co-operative\nCWS that ease precarity for freelancers, may simultaneously\nreplace competitive and individualized work cultures with more\ncollaborative ones. Other CWS, such as makerspaces engage\ncan facilitate grassroots innovation practices when used by local\ncitizens to address local social needs. For example, providing marginalized groups such as refugees in Greece or favela\ndwellers in Brazil with collective space for repair and community activities (Dias & Smith, 2018; Liodaki, 2024). Moreover,\nsocio-cultural centres have potential to engage with SI processes\nby offering their space to be used by the local community (e.g.\nneighbourhood associations) for events and other services\n(Brown, 2017; Garcia et al. 2015; Stockdale & Marmo, 2023).\nFor example, Garcia et al. describe two initiatives in popular\nneighbourhoods in Barcelona that are partly publicly funded\nand engage in a variety of cultural activities while also providing social services to the neighbourhoods such as a library and\nwelfare services. Finally, more radical examples of SI include\nautonomous social centres and occupations that aim to offer a\ncomplete alternative to the state or market, while still offering various services and cultural activities for people to take\npart in (Membretti, 2007 cited in Marques et al., 2018). While\nnot always offering a complete alternative to the market, it\nappears that a number of community-led CWS may oscillate\nbetween the market and the commons (Avdikos & Pettas, 2021).\nThese CWS thus can be used as a tool to foster economic diversity (Gibson-Graham, 2008; Smith, 2020) by engaging in simultaneous capitalist, diverse and non-capitalist practices, acting\nas ‘syntopias’ (Vidaillet & Bousalham, 2020).\nMore recently, the CWS phenomena has diffused to rural and\nperipheral areas, yet questions remain about how their dynamics may both compare and contrast to their urban counterparts. Recent research with data collected from 23 European\ncountries (12,006 CWS in total) suggests that 66% of CWS\nare located in urban regions, while the rest (34%) are in intermediate or rural regions (Marmo & Avdikos, 2024)1. Multiple\nsources (e.g. Görmar, 2021; Tomaz et al., 2022) have highlighted\nthat CWS in rural areas are more diverse in the activities they\noffer than urban CWS, ranging from community services to\nsocial and cultural initiatives. Through offering a range of services, such CWS can act as a ‘social hub’, or ‘social infrastructure\nof care’ for communities, by both creating and maintaining the social fabric of rural areas (Avdikos & Merkel, 2020;\nMerkel, 2023; Marmo & Avdikos, 2024). Conceptual framework: Transformation of social\nrelations\n\nThere is a growing body of research linking SI and societal transformation (Moulaert & MacCallum, 2019). This growth can\nlargely be attributed to the ‘polycrisis’ outlined in the introduction, in which various reputable international bodies have called\nfor the need for society to change, such as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Working Group II and the\nUN calling for timely and significant society-wide transformational change (Blythe et al., 2018) What is more, rather than\npolitical will driving the need for transformation, it is out of\nstructural necessity (Rosa, 2019).\nRegarding transformative SI, an important contribution to\nits theory has been that of the EC funded TRANSIT project\nto develop a theory on transformative social innovation (e.g.\nAvelino et al., 2019; Haxeltine et al., 2016; Pel et al., 2020).\nHere, transformative SI is understood as a “process, through\nwhich social innovations challenge, alter and/or replace established (and/or dominant) institutions in the social-material\ncontext” (Haxeltine et al., 2016: 19). The theoretical framework\nof the project comes from sustainability transitions thinking\nand conceptualizes transformative SI through the Multi-Level\nPerspective framework (MLP) (Geels, 2004), whereby SI’s\nneed to adapt to dominant rule sets in society and adapt to\ninstitutional logics (such as state regulations or collaborating with institutions) to become institutionalized and have a\ntransformative impact in society (Pel et al., 2020). Despite the\nvalue however, there are several conceptual limitations on this\napproach, that are generally existent in most transitions’ literature. Firstly, there is a lack of critique regarding capitalism\n(Chatterton, 2016; Törnberg, 2021) and secondly the MLP\nprivileges structure and hierarchy, reproducing the very power\nrelations responsible for the need for social change to take\nplace (Gillard et al., 2016). In contrast, coming from an antiessentialist and relational ontology inspired by Deleuze and\nGuattari’s Assemblage thinking and post-capitalist concepts, we\naim to overcome such limitations.\n\n3.1. Post-capitalist utopian thinking\nRather than adapting to the dominant structures in society, we\nfollow Diverse and Community Economies (DCE) thinking\nwith the idea that “imagining and enacting radically different,\nsustainable, non-anthropocentric, postcapitalist futures is what\nwe have here at hand.” (Gibson-Graham & Dombroski, 2020:\n2). Moreover, Wright (2010) refers to alternatives that exist\n\n\n\nin the here and now, within and despite capitalist processes\nof enclosure, as interstitial transformations. Both Wright and\nGibson-Graham offer examples of what Wright (2010: 4)\nterms ‘real utopias’ – “proposals for pragmatically improving our institutions. Instead of indulging in utopian dreams we\nmust accommodate to practical realities”. DCE offers such\npractical realities through a discursive shift of the economy as\na diverse space of ethical and political action. Rather than a\ndominant capitalist economy, Gibson-Graham (2006) ask us to\nread for difference over dominance, and understand the\ndiversity of economic activity at play, such as “non-market\ngifts, volunteer work, commons, cooperative forms of production, criminal economies and much more” (Smith, 2020: 596).\nWhereby, the ‘capitalist’ economy of wage labour, market\nexchange of commodities and capitalist enterprise becomes “just\none particular set of economic relations situated in a vast sea of\neconomic activity” (Gibson-Graham, 2006: 70).\n\nand building competencies to leverage future social change\n(Chatterton, 2016; Miller, 2019). To help conceptualize some\nsteps in the process of transformation, we will draw from Wright,\nwho identifies three main ‘pathways’ for social transformation: radical ‘ruptural’ transformation through wide-scale revolt\nagainst state and market, while bringing in radical alternatives;\n‘symbiotic’ transformation is through long term co-operation\nwith existing structures and institutions (e.g. trade unions);\nwhile ‘interstitial’ transformations take a prefigurative approach,\nby creating future alternatives in the here and now. In the discussion section, we will further elaborate on this schema for\ntransformation, moving from interstitial to symbiotic to ruptural, while stressing it is more of a roadmap rather than an\nessentialized vision of how a post-capitalist transformation will\nunfold. Thus, transformation is not a straight arrow or a specific\nend product, but a constant open process of struggle and\ndeliberation (Gibson-Graham & Roelvink, 2009).\n\nThis non-essentialized understanding of economy allows us\nto see possibility even in perceived dominant institutions in\nsociety, by understanding the multiple transactions, labour\narrangements, enterprise, property relations and modes of\nfinance at play when performing economy (Gibson-Graham &\nDombroski, 2020). While diverse economies is a theoretical\ntool to understand the alternative production practices, the community economy refers to how these five areas of economy may\nbe used to construct ethical economies (Cameron, 2022). More\nspecifically:\n\n3.2. Assemblage thinking\nTo give these re-conceptualizations of SI further analytical power, we draw on Assemblage thinking (DeLanda, 2006;\nDeleuze & Guattari, 1980) as both an analytical framework, and\nontology for transformation. The main contribution of assemblage is analysing socio-spatial phenomena and understanding\nthem relationally as dynamic wholes, in constant flux, or becoming. Rather than their essential qualities, it gives priority to\nrelations and qualities, enabling a mode of analysis where we\ncan compare bodies through their given affective relations\n(Buchanan, 1997).\n\n\u0007“Community economy names the ongoing process of\nnegotiating our interdependence. It is the explicit, democratic co-creation of the diverse ways in which we collectively make our livings, receive our livings from others,\nand provide for others in turn.”\n\u0007(Gibson-Graham et al., 2020).\nGibson-Graham & Roelvink (2009) use the example of\nArgentina post-2008 financial crisis, to demonstrate how SI\nprocesses can encourage the cultivation of community economies,\nwith interstitial transformations emerging from the cracks of a\ncrisis stemming from the capitalist economy – “the unemployed\nstarted to build community economies by engaging in barter\nand using alternative currencies, providing neighbourhoodbased social services and schooling, and taking over factories\nand running them cooperatively” (p. 32). What is key to these\ncommunity economies was the processes that preceded them\n– “To transform themselves into community economic subjects, they created a cooperative radio station; they went to the\nWorld Social Forum in Porto Alegre to see themselves\nreflected in others who were also engaged in projects of\nself-determination.” (p. 32).\nTherefore, rather than thinking about transformation in the sense\nof radical transformation through overthrowing a monolithic system, and replacing it with a better system, or a meta-narrative\nof how the future will unfold, DCE and other post-capitalist\nthinking deals with the here-and-now, and embraces the multiplicity of non-capitalist and more-than-capitalist practices\nthat already exist, the possibilities that could unfold as a result,\n\nThe term ‘assemblage’ is a translation from the French - agencement - originally applied by Deleuze and Guattari to signify the capacity to act. Agencement holds more meaning\nthan the English ‘assemblage’, including to arrange, to dispose\nto fit up, to combine, to order (Law, 2004: 41). Therefore, while\nassemblages are made up of material, expressive and symbolic\ncomponents, we must look at them beyond a collection of things\nbut their interaction, arrangement, ordering and (dis)assembly.\nMoreover, we must look beyond the components themselves,\nwhich are just the “props needed to actualize a particular arrangement of desire.” (Buchanan, 1997: 65), thus assemblages are\ndesire driven. Moreover, central to assemblage thinking is the\nconcept of affect, understood as the capacity to affect and be\naffected. When bodies enter affective relations with one another,\nit refers to those ‘encounters’ that may augment or diminish their respective capacities (Slaby & Mühlhoff, 2019).\nThis is central to our assemblage analysis, as we will demonstrate how various (human and non-human, material and\nexpressive) component parts affect the assemblage and its\ncapacities. This autonomy of parts can also be referred to as the\nexteriority of relations (DeLanda, 2006). Affective labour\n(Hardt, 1999) understood as the production and manipulation\nof affects, will also be essential to our understanding of the\nCWS role in organizing social relations, and subject\nformation\nBeyond its analytical merit however, it is important to note\nat the core of Deleuze & Guattari’s thinking was their use of\nassemblages as “tools to be used to take apart and reconstruct\n\n\nthe present order of things (Russell et al., 2011: 582). Thus,\nassemblage thinking along with being an analytical tool, it also\ncompliments the conceptual framework of DCE and utopian\nthinking. For example, DCE practices can be traced through\nrelations within CWS assemblages to reveal their transformative character, such as a 3D printer being acquired from capitalist\ncircuits but used as a common pool resource, i.e. introduced\nto commons circuits (Avdikos & Pettas, 2021). Moreover,\nAvdikos & Pettas have conceptualized CWS as CWS assemblages, dissolving the internal and external physical boundaries\nof CWS, and understanding them as being composed of material and immaterial components. CWS assemblages also have\na degree of ‘porosity’, that is the degree to which they are\nopen or closed to an external community and engage in\nexchange of ideas and knowledge (ibid.). Following other\napproaches drawing from Deleuze & Guattari in trying to understand SI, we want to highlight the rhizomatic nature of social\nrelations (Pel et al., 2020; Scott-Cato & Hillier, 2010). Thus,\nAT can complement DCE to reconceptualize SI processes\nthrough CWS, whereby changing social relations occurs as\na non-linear, rhizomatic process that occurs through the culmination of everyday actions, without a necessary beginning or\nend. Through assemblage thinking, transformation is rhizomatic\nchange through a micropolitics of becoming, seeking to open\nnew possibilities and multiplicities, towards post-capitalist\nfutures.\n\n4. Changing social relations\nIn this section, we examine ‘changing social relations’, the\nsecond dimension of SI, considering how individual subjectivities are both re-configured and transformed into collective\nsubjectivities through the CWS. Social relations and the subsequent increasing of affects and capacities towards community interests forms an essential part in the part of becoming\npost-capitalist subjects (Gabriel & Sarmiento, 2020). Moreover,\nthese actions form an affective relation, as they also provide the CWS’ with affects and help with its everyday\nreproduction.\n5.1.1. Desiring production and assisting with capacities.\nBoth CWS’s act as nodes that give actors opportunities to affect\nand be affected, by providing them with resources, generally\nwithout charge, such as space, knowledge, materials, networks,\nequipment etc. that increases their capacities, and enables\nthem to bring desires into reality, or in assemblage terms, desiring production. Part of their association comes from the ability\nof the CWS to provide meaning making, or axes of resonance\n(Rosa, 2019) through non-alienated and significant relationships with the world and with others. Rosa (2019: 171)\nidentifies resonance as a basic human capacity and need,\nand that all human desire, is a desire for resonance. It is this\naspect that gives CUA’s motto – ‘CUA does not do anything,\nCUA makes possible’. This highlights how the CWS innovate\nnot in an economic or technical sense but as innovation in\nsocial relations. They create and aid in the creation of new\ncollaborations in places against the narrative that nothing is\nhappening. We can regard this as SI practice, or outputs of SI\nprocesses (Moulaert & Mehmood, 2020). It also highlights\nhow the three dimensions of SI (addressing social needs, changing social relations and collective empowerment) are closely\nintertwined (Moulaert & MacCallum, 2019). By addressing\n\nthe needs of the local community (inertia in this case), they\nincrease actors’ agency changing social relations and empower\nthem with collective sense of identity.\nOne interesting example of this comes from an art teacher/\nartist, who first moved to Western Greece during COVID, as a\nsubstitute art teacher. He initially approached the CWS to meet\nlocal people, and once familiar with the CWS’ activities, collaborated with them for an engraving summer school, which\nevolved into a weekly workshop, and as an element in their\nenvironmental educational programme. The teacher noted the\npersonal importance of these activities, mainly improving his\nskills and doing something meaningful. The CWS also gains\nnew capacities through hosting new artistic activities, and from\nthe atmosphere the teacher would bring, calling in frequently\nfor small chats and gossip. The development of this affective\nrelation also ran through the local community. From participating in the annual festival of the hub, it gave visibility to the\nart teacher, and he was approached by a local NGO that\ndoes artistic workshops with young kids and children with\ndisabilities.\nThere were several other examples of the CWS collaborating with local people to help realize their desires, such as giving sound equipment to owners of a local tavern on their\nre-opening night, helping an elderly woman publish a children’s\nbook and giving a young girl books, links and historical information about the town, in order for her to do a presentation\nto 150 people about a historical event, where the hub marketed\nthe event, provided her with lights and sound equipment to\nmake it happen. Regarding staff, it used to employ two artists;\none of them left and the other could no longer be paid because\nof the end of a state funding programme. However, this affective relation was incredibly important for the CWS, for\nthe town to retain two young artists, and for the artists themselves, who could have employment in their hometown,\ndevelop their careers as artists, and do meaningful work (e.g.\nwith children, the environment). As WG11 stated, “ [artist 1] and [artist 2] are kids that probably the town would have\nnothing to do if the hub did not exist”. The CWS and the town\ngained two artists, who pass on their knowledge to young kids.\nThe mutual benefit was highlighted when one artist independently took part in a prominent Greek animation festival,\nand the organizers came to the CWS do an animation workshop\nwith young kids. Moreover, despite them leaving, the CWS\nis still interested in their professional and personal development, and they still collaborate on projects when they have the\ntime and funding.\nWe see a similar picture in CUA, at the core of what they do\nis allowing people to follow their ideas and interests, helping people to find “ what they really, really want” to do. This\nhappens through CUA’s model of free open space where people can encounter new activities and interesting people. Sometimes this happens to the extent that people can follow ideas\ninto a funded project, generally from municipal, regional, or\nnational funds. For example, the CWS has a climate action\ngroup that started meeting informally to create a discussion\n\n\nabout renewable energy in the town, they have since carried out\nseveral municipal funded renewable energy projects in several\npublic buildings. Another project between CUA, the school\nand the European Capital of Culture funds entailed schoolkids working with CUA in groups to realize their ideas (such as\na film night with friends, hosted in CUA). On occasions an\ninformal group forms into a more coherent body, for example a food co-operative was incubated there, also a company\nthat makes 3D printers. On the individual level, CUA can\nunlock desires that otherwise would not have been possible living in a small town. One of their employees began his relationship with CUA as a teenager, taking part in a soldering\nworkshop, He since has a degree in computer science and\nworks on technology projects with the CUA co-operative. He\ncredits CUA for him following this career path.\n\nimpact report states how these practices of repair are intended\nto contribute to self-efficacy and self-empowerment, going\nbeyond the relationship of consumption with objects. Another\ninteresting group was the ‘death café’ where people would meet\nup and talk about death, changing a typically individual and\npersonal subject into a collective one –\n\nHere we can see the CWS’s acting as a relational tool, creating new relationships between locals. From the individual to the affective community.\nSI theory tells us how SI processes can lead to collective empowerment by changing social relations from individual to community needs (Gibson-Graham & Roelvink, 2009). From\ninitially assembling individuals through their porous membrane,\nthese individuals become subjects of the CWS assemblage,\nand enter new relations, forming part of the CWS’ affective\ncommunity (Zink, 2019). For example, WGH, as noted\nbefore, organizes an annual festival, with a focus on local culture, heritage, and traditions they create an affective arrangement that forms a collective identity. This mainly\nconsists of the core of labour that composes their everyday\nactivities but also other (mainly younger) volunteers who return\nfor the summer. Here we see the mixing of the various individuals and groups that help reproduce the CWS, whose roles\ncan vary from simple administrative tasks to hosting workshops about local culture and traditions, or art exhibitions. The\nfestival acts as a temporary arrangement of desire enacted,\nbringing together collective action and affective labour to create a window into what could be for the town, if everybody\npooled together, highlighting what is possible in a diverse\ncommunity economy.\nIn CUA, they also change social relations towards collective subjectivities through affective labour. The monthly repair\ncafé in CUA epitomizes this. The members of the group offer\ntheir labour, knowledge, and materials to the community for\nfree, while they collaborate with other repair cafes nearby to\nshare materials and ensure they don’t clash timetables. Their\n\n\u0007something I loved was the death café, where people\ntalked about death for hours.. Just about the topic death.\nWhat do you think about death? To summarize the findings, the CWS assemblages assemble material, immaterial and semiotic components and address needs of individuals by providing them with\nmeaningful activities and offering resources that allows them\nto fulfil their desires and increase their capacities. Consequently, individuals are assembled as part of the affective community of the CWS assemblage that helps with its reproduction,\nthrough specific temporal enactments such as a festival, repair\ncafé, workshops and educational programmes. These temporal\nassemblages create socio-material networks composed of ethical relations among actors based on collaboration, reciprocity,\ncare, and ecology through a variety of transactions (market, gifting, reciprocal exchange, donations), property (private, publicly owned) and labour (waged, self-employed, volunteer). In\nboth cases, there exists a few key individuals who act as leaders within both assemblages and whose affects have greater\nimpact. These affects and the value generated by the CWS\nassemblages accrue to the local area as well as the CWS.\n\n6. Discussion: The transformative potential of\nCollaborative Workspaces\n\nSo far, the paper has discussed the role of CWS as initiators\nand facilitators of SI processes in two community led CWS\nin two rural areas in Austria and Greece. The findings reveal\nthat CWS play a significant role in addressing social needs\nand facilitating grassroots SI practices and processes, creating affective relations that contribute to the transformation\nof individual to collective subjectivities, thus answering the\nfirst research question “How do rural collaborative workspaces\nengage in social innovation processes?”. The second research\nquestion “To what extent can these SI processes be considered\nto have transformative potential for society?” and the third\nmore theoretical research question “How can diverse and community economies and assemblage thinking inform transformative social innovation theory?” will be answered in this\nsection. As elaborated upon in the literature review, both\nDCE and AT offer tools for thinking about transformations\ntowards post-capitalism and post-growth realities, through\ntheir anti-essentialist, post-structuralist ontology. DCE offers a\ndiscursive shift of our understanding of the economy to morethan-capitalist and non-capitalist enterprise, labour, transactions,\nproperty and finance relations, while AT offers an analytical\nframework coupled as an ontological and methodological\ntool for rethinking spatial forms.\nWhile very different socio-economic contexts, the processes\nappear quite similar. In both CWS assemblages the multiplicity of relations is clear from the municipally funded space of\nCUA, volunteer/affective labour apparent throughout both\nCWS assemblage’s activities, the self-employed labour\nthrough the external collaborators of WGH, and their financing from EU and state projects, along with financial and material\ndonations. The main contrast is the ease of CUA in accessing\nresources from the municipality or local government compared\n\n\n\nwith WGH having to rely a lot more on their own actions\nto raise money through market actions, funding programs\nor community donations, which adds an element of precarity to their assemblage and the risk of mission drift. This highlights that within a DCE framework, there still exist elements of\ncapitalist logic and innovation. Part of WGH’s vision is to create\neconomic growth and employment, to stimulate both supply and demand and attract wealthy tourists to spend money\nin their town. They have waged employees, and engage in competitive funding tendering, market exchange (tourism, sale of\nproducts). These elements appear more apparent in WGH\nthan in CUA, because of the dire economic circumstances\nof their town, the need for them to pay for their property and\nearn a living through the CWS, whereas CUA has a supportive context through the ease of access to funding through\nboth local and regional government. The lack of a supportive\nenvironment means the CWS must trade on the market and\nspend time applying for funding to earn some income to keep it\nafloat economically despite its successful model of solidarity\ndonations from locals. This constant chasing of economic\nincome can leave them with less time for community activities, struggle to permanently employ staff and can lead to internal tensions, for example one of the artists left because of the\npressure he felt from the founder. Moreover, although both\nCWS offer the opportunity for desiring production, it is\nnot entirely free, and appears somewhat overcoded by both\nfounders, albeit to less of a degree in Austria. In WGH, almost\neverything refers back to the local town and culture, while in\nCUA there is a specific technology focus, which is the main\ninterest of the founder. While both founders have a significant influence on the assemblage, CUA has much more rhizomatic character, as a co-operative but also in the association.\nTheir network of associations spread across several countries\nshare this character, autonomously governed and sharing the\nsame principles. WGH, while porous with the community,\nthe organization has a very hierarchical character, which most\nlikely occurs because of the difficult socio-economic context\nand the reliance on the founder to drive the assemblage forward constantly. However, it does have a rhizome-like interaction with its external collaborators, which could allow for a\nsimilar spread of SI in the region, as seen in CUA.\nThe fact that WGH engages more with capitalism does not\nrid it of transformative potential. Firstly, we need to acknowledge the very different socio-economic realities that exist in\nNorthern and Southern Europe, and what this means when\nthinking about societal transformations. Post-growth thinking\nacknowledges these inequalities, that sometimes an increase in\nmaterial wellbeing and prosperity is required in the Global South\nand disadvantaged populations in the Global North (Schmid,\n2022). Moreover, by drawing from assemblage and diverse and\ncommunity economies thinking, it allows us to move beyond\nessentialized forms, whereby “capitalism becomes just one\nparticular set of economic relations situated in a vast sea of\neconomic activity” (Gibson-Graham, 2008: 68). This is clear\nin both cases, from the vast array of labour, transactions, property and finance apparent in both CWS assemblages. Moreover,\nassemblage thinking does not have us to question whether\nthese assemblages are transformative or not, but what is their\n\npotential for social transformation. When thinking with assemblages, we do not ask what a body is, but what can a body do?\n(Buchanan, 1997). Therefore, going beyond the material conditions of both cases, it is clear that both CWS assemblages address\nsocial needs and engage in the two levels of changing social\nrelations: desiring production (praxis) and changing individual\nto collective subjectivities, these processes have emancipatory potential. Through offering resonant experiences and\nmeaningful connection and allowing people to engage in\nfree-flowing desire production, it destabilizes what Deleuze and\nGuattari describe as the machinic unconscious, unlocking a\nrevolution of micropolitical becoming in those that encounter\nthe CWS’s. Rather than being subjected to machinic enslavement, inertia and alienation, the CWS’s offer liberation from\nthe circuits of the machines at play in both localities, be it\nthrough the ‘left-behindness’ in Greece or lack of purpose in\nAustria (Harvey, 2020; Rosa, 2019). Moreover, through creating\naffective communities, this affective labour has transformative potential, as it creates collective subjectivities, social networks, community, and biopower from below (Hardt, 1999).\nThese reconfigurations enable the creation of what GibsonGraham (2006) call ‘intentional economies’ or ‘real utopias’\nas Wright emphasised (2010). Both represent open ended\npost-capitalist realities, by facilitating pluriversality (Escobar,\n2021) or economic diversity (Gibson-Graham, 2006), working towards creating many different worlds, rather than the\nperceived dominant capitalist world, destabilizing its perceived\nhegemony.\nRather than providing an exact post-capitalist strategy, the\nCWS’ provide a “multitude of possibilities of what could\ncome after, as well as building daily competences to leverage\nsocial change.” (Chatterton, 2016: 405). However, thinking\nabout post-capitalism through DCE, ‘ after’ does not imply a\nclean break from the ‘capitalist system’ and instilling a better\nsystem, but by recognizing the capitalist alternatives in the here\nand now (Miller, 2019; Schmid, 2022). To help conceptualize how this occurs, we return to Wright’s (2010) three pathways towards social transformation – ruptural, interstitial and\nsymbiotic. Wright gives examples of interstitial transformations such as various forms of co-ops, workers councils, community based social economy organizations etc., which are also\ncommon examples of SI. Such initiatives offer viable alternatives to state and capitalist ways of organizing, however, are\noften critiqued as operating in isolation, whereby they do\nnot offer a real viable alternative, but merely as heterotopias\nthat operate within ‘niche’ or ‘protective spaces’ (Smith &\nRaven, 2012). This is what we conceptualize as our first level\nof transformation, which we can clearly see as the two CWS’s\nwork towards satisfying community needs and building community economies, through desiring production and building\naffective relations.\nWright differentiates from interstitial to symbiotic transformations from their orientation towards the state, which they use\nas a mediating body to reduce capitalist power, with the main\nexample given as class compromise, where workers and capitalist firms negotiate through the state over workers’ rights, pay etc.\nWe would like to offer a different conceptualization of symbiotic\n\n\ntransformation, as a second stage of interstitial transformation\ndiffusion, as they influence other spatiotemporal arrangements. This may initially be in antagonism, and lead to their\npractices being copied/replicated by state or municipal organizations, or it may mean these institutions recognizing and\nsupporting interstitial transformations as important social\neconomy actors. In both cases we also observe this level of\ntransformation, with long term institutional collaboration of\nCUA and the municipality and more recently in WGH. Moreover, both CWS also offer their resources and capacities to\nlocal actors such as schools, local businesses and other market entities, NGOs, and other local associations, blending\nDCE economies with the market economy. While interstitial\ntransformation appears to be performed inside the CWS, symbiotic transformation happens outside the CWS, influencing\nother entities and the state to experience the alternative socioeconomic arrangements, through the DCE framework that CWS’s\nassemblages perform. Within SI literature, symbiotic transformation is often used to describe the transformative aspect\nof SI as bottom-linked SI (Haxeltine et al., 2016; Moulaert &\nMacCallum, 2019) that “develops when citizens’ collective initiatives result in agreements with local institutions that enable and sustain such initiatives through sound, regulated and\nlasting practices” (Garcia et al., 2015: 93). This symbiotic\napproach is key for SI’s transformative potential to move beyond\ntheir space in the margins. However, SI being recognized by\ninstitutions does not equate to the neoliberal rhetoric by governments over the last twenty years (for example see Fougère\net al., 2017 for a full discussion), something that “limits social\n\ninnovation to marginal change” (Bartels, 2017: 3802). Initiatives need to be given resources and power rather than just\nresponsibility (Bragaglia, 2021).\nAs shown in Figure 1, it is important to not think of the stages\nas linear steps and stages, and to note there is a degree of\nnon-linearity as DCE practices are performed and practiced,\nmoving from the edifices and margins as interstitial transformations, to piercing the institutional dimension, back to interstitial and moving beyond the symbiotic (Chatterton, 2016).\nRegarding the ruptural stage, we envisage DCE practices\nspreading in a form of contagion until they reach a saturation\npoint of post-capitalist becoming, signifying not necessarily a\nstraight clean break from capitalism to post-capitalism, but a\nrevolution of becoming, as different aspects of life become\nrecoded and DCE are heavily territorialized and enacted in\nsociety. Both cases do not reflect this level of transformation\n(yet, at least), but an example of such a ‘real utopia’ would\nbe the widely documented case of Mondragon in the Basque\ncountry, with the co-operative model completely institutionalized in the region. While as an example it is highly contextual and non-replicable, it gives an example of possibility\n(Gibson-Graham, 2008). We cannot state this is the case for\neither case study, both remain still in their symbiotic stage. The\npassage to a ruptural transformation requires CWS to be supported by various local and extra-local actors to be acknowledged as the only alternative and post-capitalist solution,\nwhere DCE can become the dominant mode of production and\ngain a hegemonic place in society.\n\nFigure 1. How Diverse and Community Economay practices diffuse throughout the three levels of transformation to create a\ntransformative change, to(wards) post-capitalism.\n\n\n\n7. Conclusion\n\nThe paper discussed the role of CWS as initiators and facilitators of SI processes in two community led CWS in two\nrural areas in Austria and Greece. The research methodology\nemployed a qualitative case study approach, utilizing participant\nobservation and semi-structured interviews to explore the multiple sets of relations found in these rural CWS, aiming to\nunderstand how these arrangements cover local needs and foster new social relations. The findings reveal that CWS play a\nsignificant role in addressing social needs and facilitating\ngrassroots SI practices. CWS seem to provide a site where the\nunmet needs of the local people are negotiated, redefined and\nare covered in a co-creative and collective manner. This is happening in a DCE framework, where affective labour mostly\nperforms. They provide opportunities for actors to affect and be\naffected, increasing their agency and assisting them with their\nown desires by providing resources such as space, knowledge,\nmaterials, networks, and equipment.\nOn the individual level, these actions have emancipatory\npotential through freeing desiring production and creating non-alienated and significant relationships with the world\nand with others. These affective relations also extend to the\nlarger community in a symbiotic manner, creating positive\naffects for the town and fostering collective action and affective\nlabor to create a window into what could be for the community\nand help to develop collective subjectivities. From this interstitial level, these processes also enable symbiotic transformations as they ‘infiltrate’ other more strictly coded and highly\nterritorialized assemblages, such as schools and municipalities,\nwhile maintaining their autonomy. These processes give potential for a multiplicity of alternative development pathways, from\ninterstitial, symbiotic and potentially ruptural transformative\npotential.\nThe main aim of this paper was to provide a theoretical framework of assemblage thinking and diverse and community economies to firstly understand how CWS engage in SI processes,\nand secondly to unpack how these processes may have potential for societal transformation. These concepts, through their\nanti-essentialist approach allowed us to move beyond the\ninherit binaries existing in both CWS and SI literature. Firstly,\nthey allowed us to see beyond the individual entrepreneur at\nthe heart of the assemblage and understand the processes of\nindividual and collective empowerment that occur. This is\nimportant for both SI and CWS theory, especially as much of\nthe literature focuses upon binary discourses of either of neoliberal individual ‘changemaker’ entrepreneurs facilitating\nand accelerating social change, or community-based collective\nendeavors (Bandinelli & Arvidsson, 2013; Gandini & Cossu,\n2021; Montgomery, 2016). An anti-essentialist approach allows\nus to see beyond these divides and understand CWS as syntopias rather than absolute heterotopias, whereby both capitalist\nand non-capitalist subjectivities can co-exist while not opposing the other, and potential for transformation may already\nbe latent within (Vidaillet & Bousalham, 2020). Future\nresearch could further explore these tensions and contradictions\ninherit to SI, but also the wide range of SI’s that exist, from the\n\nconformist and reformist capitalist ones (Liodaki, 2024) to radical anti-capitalist SI’s (Apostolopoulou et al., 2022) and how\nthey may interact with Wright’s three stages of transformation.\nFurthermore, the theoretical framework questions the assumption\nthat SI emerges from a lack of services and unmet social\nneeds (Moulaert & MacCallum, 2019). When viewed through\nAT, it appears that SI emerges from a desire to change social\nrelations, often towards more ethical and ecologically sustainable arrangements, and the CWS managed to tap into that desire,\nand addressing social needs in the process.\nFinally, there is an urban bias within SI research, despite the\ngrowing body of rural SI literature. Some of this work recognizes the plurality of economic relations, such as Olmedo\n& O’Shaughnessy (2023) through a Polyanian framework.\nConsequently, there is a need for comparison between rural and\nurban areas and determine if there is indeed a difference in the\ncharacter of SI we see, and if so, how can the understandings of\none benefit the other. Within the context of social-ecological\ncrises, it alters the position of urban agglomerations vis-àvis the rural. Notions of progress and development have been\nlinked with urban agglomerations, seen as drivers of national\neconomies through innovation, with the rural seen as traditional,\nbackward, lacking – thus implying a need to transformation\ntowards being more urban (Shucksmith et al., 2009). However,\nin light of the polycrisis, we cannot solve modern problems, with\nthe very categories that created them – growth, progress, competition, urban economic dynamism (Escobar, 2021). Instead,\nwe must focus on those livelihoods that do not fully assimilate to the supposedly dominant capitalist economy, which is\noften clearer in rural areas where social relations may be more\nembedded with nature and communal life, if not part of the\nsame habitat (Miller, 2019). Rural-urban comparisons can open\nnew questions thinking about SI, such as what territorializes\nthe assemblages of urban SI? Does more intense financialization of space, as observed in urban centres, make urban SIs lean\nmore towards the market? Or what are their practices of subverting this? Moreover, what could rural SI learn from urban\nexamples, for example the new municipalist experiments in\ncities such as Barcelona and Madrid (Thompson, 2021).\nSuch questions emerge when comparing similarities while\nbeing aware of differences, as was the case with the comparison with Greece and Austria. Moreover, assemblage thinking\ncan help with this, as it did with this paper, with its purpose\nnot to develop a homogenous field manual for post-capitalist\ntransformation, but to deconstruct the socially innovative assemblages and their processes of (de/re) composition, and understand how these processes and their outcomes in two very\ndifferent contexts share some similarities, why that is the\ncase, and what conclusions can we draw regarding their\ntransformative potential.\n\nEthics and consent\nConsent\n\nInterview Participants were given an information sheet about the\nstudy and were required to sign a consent form prior to being\ninterviewed.
