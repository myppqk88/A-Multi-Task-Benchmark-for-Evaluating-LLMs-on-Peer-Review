Abstract
Background
The European space sector is on the rise, introducing a rapidly
growing market with a lot of expanding opportunities for innovative
space products, applications and services, even in non-space sectors.
In this article, we investigate the skill levels of innovation
intermediaries that provide support to innovators (i.e., start-ups,
scale-ups and SMEs) in the Central-Eastern (CE) and South-Eastern (SE)
European space sector. Our objective is to identify: (i) the available
competences and (ii) the missing skills (potential areas for
improvement) of innovation intermediaries in the EU space sector.
Methods
Our methodological approach consists of (i) desk research
complemented by semi-structured interviews to introduce a baseline
framework of innovation intermediaries’ desired knowledge and skill
levels, as well as (ii) an online survey, addressing the case of
innovation intermediaries and innovators, respectively. We first
solidify the baseline framework with the expert feedback from the
semi-structured interviews and then proceed to the analysis of the
online survey responses, to capture and discuss the perceived needs
of both innovation intermediaries and innovators.

By analysing of 15 semi-structured interviews and 104 survey
responses, several interesting insights for the future of European
space innovation have been identified and are highlighted in the
present manuscript.
Conclusion
We conclude that innovation intermediaries in the space sector have
the adequate capacity to effectively train and support innovators in
improving, primarily, their organisational skills and soft skills. Trainers
are also competent at assessing the investment readiness levels of
innovators, their pitching skills, and they can provide tailored business
support services to innovators. In parallel, space innovators need to
receive better training to improve their business modelling skills,
better support the management of Intellectual Property, and
understand key technologies and market characteristics of the space
market.
Plain language summary
The European space sector is on the rise, introducing a rapidly
growing market with a lot of expanding opportunities for innovative
space products, applications and services, even in non-space sectors.
In order to exploit these opportunities, entrepreneurs need
substantial business support, to build the knowledge and skills
required to benefit from this erupting New Space era effectively. This
is even more intense in Central-Eastern (CE) and South-Eastern (SE)
Europe, where only 30% of Europe’s space support innovation
intermediaries are established. In this article, we investigate the
knowledge and skill levels of innovation intermediaries that support
innovators (i.e., start-ups, scale-ups and SMEs) of the SE and CE
European space sector. Our objective is to identify: (i) the available
competences and (ii) missing knowledge and skills of innovation
intermediaries in the European space sector, investigating their skill
levels from two main points of view: (a) the perception they have on
their own knowledge and skill levels; and (b) the perception of
innovators who have received business support services, with regard
to the skill levels of innovation intermediaries. A desk research
complemented by 15 semi-structured interviews introduced a baseline
framework of innovation intermediaries’ desired knowledge and skill
levels, which was further investigated via two online surveys, for
innovation intermediaries and innovators respectively. The analysis of
104 responses collected revealed several interesting insights; we have
identified potential knowledge and skill areas where innovation
intermediaries in the EU space sector could improve, extending from
Intellectual Property management to space market research and
business modelling. The results of this analysis provide useful insights
for EU policymaking in the field of space innovation support, the
development of tailored business support programmes for
supporting space entrepreneurship in Europe, as well as for better
informing internal business training programmes of large companies
and SMEs in the space sector.

1 Introduction

Since the advent of the New Space era, the global space
economy has been witnessing staggering growth and innovation. Driven by major technological advances, a vibrant entrepreneurial spirit, an increasing appetite for investment as well
as renewed policy ambitions, the global space economy has
grown to an astounding turnover of $447 billion in 2020, continuing a five-year growth, and is showing no signs of stopping1.
Meanwhile, space technologies, data and services are finding increasing applications across many industries (e.g., from
transport and energy over to agriculture and environment),
serving as an enabling element for their digital transformation and providing the building blocks for innovative business
models2. With that in mind, it may come as no surprise that
the global space context is shifting, and it is shifting fast, as
more and more new players from the private sector enter the
game and innovate, introducing remarkable cost reductions in
accessing and using space to create new solutions and capture
value from a broadening customer base.i
In this context, a new kind of space race is on, with the private sector competing to capture the unprecedented business
opportunities of the New Space era. Despite its world-class

behind its global competitors (e.g., US or China) as many success stories (such as SpaceX and Blue Origin) emerge outside of
Europe with the support of suitable innovative industrial
policies and financial support frames (e.g. first client approach,
technology agnostic anchor tenancy) as well as the increased
involvement of private investors. Given the vital importance of space for key EU policies and political priorities
(from boosting competitiveness to battling climate change and
fostering sustainable use of resources)4, lagging behind is not
an option. We need to quickly adapt and create an environment
conducive to space entrepreneurship, incentivising and supporting EU businesses to start-up and scale in order to seize
the opportunities ushered in by the shifting global space context. All while championing the EU’s industrial leadership,
boosting economic growth, creating jobs and delivering social
and environmental benefits to EU citizens.
Against the backdrop of developing European policies and support schemes for space innovation, several programmes and
initiatives have emerged at the EU level, intending to tackle
well-known barriers that are preventing start-ups from taking up and/or growing their activities in the EU’s space sector (lack of awareness on available opportunities, difficulties in
accessing capital, limited business skills, risk-aversity, etc.)2.
This includes programmes for supporting space innovation
through funding instruments such as the EU’s Horizon 2020 and
the most recent Horizon Europe, the European Investment Bank
(EIB) European Fund for Strategic Investments and the European Space Agency (ESA) Technology Research Programme
as well as initiatives geared more to start-ups (incubators,
accelerators, hackathons, etc.), fostering the use of available EU
space systems, data and services in commercial applications
(Copernicus Start-Up Programme, E-GNSS Accelerator, Galileo
Masters, etc.).
Space hubs have a vital role to play in helping start-ups to
identify and make the most out of suitable opportunities available at the EU level, while at the same time catalysing collaborations and investments that can support their crucial early
stages and scale up. Indeed, through local support initiatives,
space hubs can raise awareness on the business opportunities
offered by space technologies, data and services as well as help
space start-ups and scale-ups access the necessary knowledge
(technical and/or business skills, technologies, licenses, intellectual property, etc.), networks (business partners, first customers, team members, etc.) and financial solutions (grants, credit,
venture capital, etc.) to successfully seize them. At the same
time, they are well-positioned to mobilise and bring together
stakeholders from space and non-space sectors (e.g., entrepreneurs, researchers, industrial actors, users, investors), with a view
to exploring new applications, business ideas and (co-)financing schemes for leveraging space technologies, data and services to deliver demand-driven solutions for non-space sectors
(e.g., agriculture, health, ICT), creating new business opportunities and stimulating entrepreneurs to get new ventures off the
ground5.

European policy-makers have already acknowledged the vital
role of space hubs in the Space Strategy for Europe, recognising them as a powerful platform for shaping favourable business
ecosystems along with enabling framework conditions for
space entrepreneurship2. In fact, there are already several
space hubs that are deploying local initiatives and catalysing space entrepreneurship in the EU. However, these space
hubs are not equally distributed across Europe. The majority of them are concentrated in countries that have been historically more involved in the aerospace field (e.g., France, Germany
and Italy).
CE and SE European countries with emerging or existing
space capacity, have many vibrant technology ecosystems
and industry verticals as well as user communities with great
potential for contributing to the increased commercialisation
of space (including spinning-in and -out opportunities). For
instance, more than 10 unicorns have sprung out of CE European countries (estimated combined value of €30 billion), with a
promising roster of start-ups currently on the rise (e.g. UIPath in
robotics, Kiwi.com in transport, Prezi in software, Flo in health)
and around €700 million invested in CE European start-ups in
2018 alone (e.g. Taxify, Citybee)6. The startup ecosystem in
SE Europe is alive and kicking too, with over 5,000 SE European start-ups estimated to have raised more than $50 million by
2016, and a surge of new start-ups, support actors and schemes
in 20187.
Moreover, the CE and SE Europe are abundant with talent offering a highly skilled and educated tech workforce with great
potential for space innovation. Over the last twenty years, the
regions of interest have shown positive signs in terms of labour
productivity growth, leading to economic growthii, and therefore converging to the EU average8. The new era and the transformation of many CE and SE European economies require that
innovation will be a key component in their new growth models to foster prosperity even further. More importantly, given
that the core of the space sector is comprised mostly of the
development of cutting-edge technologies and applications, it
becomes apparent that the advancement of the space ecosystem in the CE and SE Europe will be positively impacted by an
enabling environment for the overall innovation landscape in
the region, be it support to local innovation intermediaries or
the establishment of new initiatives to support space innovators.
At the same time, it is also essential to ensure that these innovation intermediaries have all the necessary knowledge, skills,
resources and tools in place to effectively organise their local
initiatives to support their portfolio of innovators and enable
them to engage and drive value from space innovation, bringing
the EU at the forefront of the global new space race. Towards this
direction, this manuscript, which covers work performed under
the EU-funded H2020 InnORBIT project (GA No 101004212)iii,

the following:
•	RQ1: Which are the most essential skills and knowledge innovation intermediaries should possess to

•	RQ2: What is the perceived maturity of these skills

Towards addressing these questions, the present study offers

current skill levels and knowledge gaps of members of innovation intermediaries. We offer new insights for the market-driven
design and development of new initiatives to build the business skills and space knowledge capacity of trainers, coaches
and innovation advisors in the CE and SE Europe and beyond.
Secondly, and in line with the previous pillar, we also offer

the CE and SE Europe and the analysis of their inherent needs

supporting space entrepreneurship in the CE and SE Europe,

2 Related work

The role of innovation intermediaries in bridging the knowledge gaps is widely discussed internationally, either via bringing together different actors of the innovation ecosystem (e.g.,
innovators, investors, funding providers, advisors, policymakers,
etc.) or via supporting the diffusion of technology and novel
ideas across different industries and technological domains. An
intermediary facilitates the knowledge exchange and knowledge transfer to a customer through various channels such as
(i) direct knowledge transfer; (ii) exchange of experience; (iii)
networking; and (iv) consulting on companies’ innovation needs
and available solutions9. After all, technology transfer is a timebased process involving multiple actors10, while innovation
intermediaries and consultants need different capabilities to be
able to effectively and successfully support a customer.
A key component of the existing literature that is currently missing, to the authors’ knowledge, is a dedicated analysis of the
knowledge and skill levels that innovation intermediaries believe
they possess when they provide business support services.
In parallel, it is also interesting to investigate the intermediaries’ skills needed by the corresponding counterparts who act as
receivers of these skills and services (innovators). Intermediaries’ knowledge and skill levels in certain business areas exert a
key influence on the quality of the services they provide towards
innovators.
Motivated by this, Gassman et al. (2011)11 have analysed
107 European manufacturing companies and six collaborative
cross-industry innovation projects to reveal the core skills and
expertise an effective intermediary should possess in supporting cross-industry innovation. Based on their results, effective
intermediaries should have the adequate capacity to identify
the companies’ core competencies, technological capabilities,
and problems in a more abstract yet standardised manner (compared to internal company experts). On top of that, the study
of Gassman et al. (2011)11 revealed that technical know-how
and networking skills are also desired elements in the service
portfolio of an intermediary, based on the needs of innovators.
Using regression analysis, Ibrahim et al. (2017)12 highlight
the importance of employees’ soft skill acquisition. They
conclude that innovators and managers need to induce new training methods and curricula in order to enable an effective framework that reinforces soft skills in the company. Given that such
training programmes are provided, most of the time, by specialised business trainers and coaches, the aspect of soft skills should
be considered as a desired element of an effective innovation
intermediary.
Piispanen et al. (2017)13 indicate that entrepreneurs should
possess core business knowledge and skills to enhance their
business development. In a similar vein, Anwar (2018)14 identifies the Business Model Innovation (BMI) as a core driver for
an innovator’s better performance, influencing positively its
comparative advantage, especially in growing industries like

the space industry. Many studies also pinpoint that entrepreneurship is significantly boosted when innovators adopt new
digital technologies, upgrade their digital capabilities, and follow a concrete digital strategy, helping them to become innovation leaders (Khin and Ho, 201915, Ismail et al., 201716, Sousa
and Rocha, 201917). These findings suggest that digital and
organisational skills should constitute a core area of the effective
innovation intermediary.
Within the space domain, clusters, the most common form of
innovation intermediary, are an integral aspect that evolves
rapidly along the space economy, especially considering
that its development is mainly based on dispersed interventions in supporting innovation, rather than a unified national
programme18. In a recent thorough international comparison on
space cluster development19, the authors have found that “skills
are ‘sticky’ and drive the space economy in the long term”.
Nevertheless, in most if not all cases examined, the space clusters are highly dependent on external entities, such as universities, and even in huge organisations, such as NASA, there
is a risk of skills shortage due to an ageing workforce. With all
of that in mind, it is difficult to imagine how space clusters can
effectively support innovation without being up-skilled or reskilled, not only to remain competitive within an erupting market, but also to act as drivers and enablers for the innovator
ecosystem. Moreover, according to the Space Skills Alliance20,
SMEs in the downstream sector, are in need of a more diverse
skill set in their workforce, which in most cases cannot be found,
mainly due to the lack of resources to invest in up-skilling,
but also due to the limited capacity of the available trainers.
There are sparse findings in the literature regarding skill and
knowledge capacity assessment in the space industry. In an
extensive systematic literature review in 201421, have analysed in
depth the international technology transfer trends in the space
industry. Although they have identified multiple dimensions,
they have not investigated the capacity of the involved actors
to effectively support their ecosystems, including technology
transfer support.
Focusing more on the actual job market, the Space Skills Alliance has also created a competencies taxonomy22, including extended knowledge and skills aspects. According to their
findings, which were based on 812 early career UK space job
adverts, a taxonomy of five categories has been created to
describe the competencies required from the space industry in
the UK. Namely: Traits, Knowledge, Technical skills, Transferable skills, and Qualifications. This taxonomy covers a wide
range of necessary knowledge and skills areas, however, its
focus is on the overall workforce of the UK space industry, and
not on the role of innovation intermediaries as support pillars.
A recent skills gap assessment in Australia23, revealed quite interesting findings, starting with the fact that there is a clear shift
from technical to soft skills and space sector enabling skills
(specialised business, finance, regulatory, legal skills), which
are even considered critical for the current market. This,
along with the shortages identified in almost all of the skills

categories in this study, highlights the necessity for up-skilling and
knowledge transfer. Going in more depth in the analysis of
innovation intermediaries, their activities, strategies, roles and
impacts, Vizmar24 thoroughly analysed the Scottish New Space

capacity-building support, while redefining (potentially) the
role of innovation intermediaries for driving innovation in their
local ecosystem.
The existing literature, although not specifically and in detail,
addresses the issue of intermediaries’ skills and knowledge needs in the space industry, offering a variety of desired
attributes and skill sets that an effective business trainer/coach
should possess. Despite the several factors that could be considered in a framework model for the assessment of the skill
perceptions of such trainers, we can mostly identify four key
aspects. Namely, these aspects refer to the business skills of
intermediaries, their capacity to support innovators at embracing digital transformation and designing a digital strategy,
as well as their ability to improve the soft skill of innovators and their networking with relevant innovation actors. This
analysis of our framework model for intermediaries’ skills
and knowledge is further upended by also studying the key
insights stemming from other relevant EU-funded R&D
projects that the authors have been engaged with (see Table 1),
focusing on innovation and business support. It is important to
state at this point that the introduced framework is not aiming
to propose a new taxonomy or to get into a detailed analysis of
the knowledge and skills assessed, but mainly to offer a better
understanding of the current capacity of innovation intermediaries and pathways towards improvements for effectively
supporting their ecosystem.

3 Methods

The approach followed for delivering the envisioned analysis consists of four steps, as can be seen in Figure 1. Starting
with a desk research based on reports and knowledge gained
from previous EU projects, as well as recent literature, so as to
develop an initial baseline framework of the various skills that
competent innovation intermediaries should possess in order
to provide business support services to innovators (Step 1).
Subsequently, we deployed a series of semi-structured interviews with experts in the local SE and CE European ecosystems,
gathering feedback to fine-tune and validate the baseline framework we developed (Step 2). This exercise enabled us to set a
space-related market-driven framework of desired skills and
competencies. At the same time, the main elements of this
framework were transformed into an online survey to investigate in more detail the views and perceptions from both stakeholder sides, i.e., innovation intermediaries and space innovators
(Step 3). The final step of our approach delved into a more
detailed analysis concerning the skill-sets of innovation intermediaries needed for leading their innovators to a successful journey into space innovation, as well as the perceived
business needs of innovators in the space industry, via
analysing our survey results (Step 4). The following sections,
provide a more elaborate description of the methodological
steps followed.

develop a baseline framework for our analysis and identify the
current state-of-play in the skill levels and needs of innovation
intermediaries in providing business support services to innovators. The desk research relied on specific available reports

3.2 Step 2: Semi-structured interviews
The second step in our approach further calibrates the initial
baseline framework and distinguishes between the critical and
non-critical skills and knowledge areas of innovation intermediaries. The goal here is to identify any skill areas and
dimensions not previously envisaged in our initial framework
(Step 1). Therefore, we validate our initial findings, based on
the views of experts belonging to our two stakeholder groups
(innovation intermediaries and innovators). This exercise
reveals which specific skills and skill areas should be included
in the questionnaire of the online survey, fuelling the activities of Step 3. Since detailed information is not always available online, we pursued close consultations with various public
and private stakeholders from the targeted regions via a series
of semi-structured interviews.
To start the process, an interview guideline that comprised
of a summary of the InnORBIT project, an overview of the
objectives of the interview and a baseline framework to
guide the discussion, was prepared. The baseline framework
ensures that the conversations stay focused on the topic and

By engaging InnORBIT partners´ networks we identified
highly experienced innovation intermediaries and innovators, which we sought to interview about their perspectives on
innovation intermediaries´ required skills for delivering business support services, focusing on the space industry dimension
and its applications. Two interview questionnaires were developed, facilitating consultations with the groups of innovators

During interview sessions, the baseline framework was
presented to experts, with a view to defining the types of skills
innovation intermediaries need to possess in order to provide
effective business support to entrepreneurs. Depending on the
stakeholder group, experts were asked to validate the baseline
framework from the point of view of either (i) innovation intermediaries; as providers of support services, or (ii) innovators;
as recipients of such services.
The validation process has been carried out via quantitative
and qualitative means. More specifically, interviewees were
asked to rate the significance of skills categories and specific
skills included in our baseline framework based on a scale
from 1 to 5, with 1 assigned to “not important” skills and 5
to “absolutely important” skills. A skill is considered validated, as long as its average rating and its mode (i.e., the value
that appears most frequently in a data set) exceed the value
of 3 (average level). This threshold derives naturally from the
design of the Likert scale response, under which any value
above 3 is perceived positively. At the same time, respondents
were requested to identify those skills or skills categories missing from our baseline framework as well as to highlight any
changes needed in the definition and classification of skills
included.

In total, we performed 15 interviews (9 with innovation intermediaries and 6 with innovators across the EU) via digital means;
interviews’ insights were collected, synthesized, and analysed
further, ultimately leading to the update of the initial baseline framework. The fine-tuned version of our baseline framework built the premises for developing and deploying an online
survey under Step 3.

3.3 Step 3: Online survey
In the third step, we deploy an online survey to reveal the skill
levels of space-related innovation intermediaries based on
(i) their own perceptions and (ii) the perspectives of innovators who received these business support skills and services.
While this exercise has mostly a geographical focus on the ecosystems of CE and SE Europe, we are also keen to capture
the broader perspective of stakeholders from more developed
ecosystems (EU level), who either provide business support
services to innovators in the region of our focus or have business relationships with innovators in the region. Thus, the
broader perspective of our study offers richer insights into the
business support landscape and the desired skills of innovation
intermediaries that are currently valued by innovators and by
market forces in space-related domains.
We divide each sub-section of the current manuscript based
on each skill level category that is investigated in the survey,
namely (i) Knowledge in the Space Industry, (ii) Business Assessment Knowledge, (iii) Business Support Skills, (iv) Organisational and Digital Skills, and (v) Soft Skills. To study in-depth
each core skill area, we used a set of well-tailored Likert-scale
survey questions, addressing specific business sub-element of
each core skill area. Specifically, we employed a 5-level scale,
with 1 indicating low levels of skills and 5 indicating high
levels of skills.
The selected skill areas and questions of the survey are based
on the fine-tuned framework developed under Step 2. The survey was implemented online via a survey tool (i.e., EUSurveyv)
to ensure a broad and effective data collection process. In particular, two separate and anonymous survey questionnaires
were developed. The first addresses the perceived skills and
needs of innovation intermediaries, namely the perceptions of
advisors, trainers, coaches, and mentors belonging to clusters, incubators, accelerators and SME support organisations in
space-related and other sectors. The second addresses the views
of innovators; we asked them to indicate their experiences and
knowledge received from their participation in training with
innovation intermediaries, and more specifically, we investigated the skill levels of innovation intermediaries from an innovator’s point of view. In essence, we analysed the same factor
(namely, innovation intermediaries’ skills and needs) from two
different angles: the service provider side (innovation intermediaries) and the service recipient side (innovators). This
approach enabled us to reveal any differences in the perceptions of the two groups under investigation, examine how these

perceptions vary, and extract informed implications for the core
objectives of this study; (i) to identify the available missing
skills of innovation intermediaries, and (ii) to reveal the needs
of innovators’ ecosystems.
To enhance the outcomes of our research and to reach out to
a sufficient number of suitable intermediaries around Europe,
relevant experts from the InnORBIT network (within the project
and external) were invited to complete the survey – these
experts include relevant trainers of innovation intermediaries
around Europe, such as consultancies, accelerators, incubators,
and innovation agencies. On top of that, we disseminated the
survey to innovators who are involved in space-related technologies, exploiting mostly the contacts of consortium’s clusters and start-up networks. The online survey aimed for at least
100 participants for extracting meaningful insightsvi.

3.4 Step 4: analysis
The last part of our approach delves into insights stemming
from the online survey. Our objective was to identify deficit patterns amongst innovation intermediaries (current vs. necessary
skills/knowledge) that need to be addressed via capacity building services, as well as to produce intelligence for
fine-tuning the business support services to be provided by
space innovation related local support initiatives.
Survey results were gathered, consolidated, and analysed to
extract insights on the skills and knowledge areas of innovation
intermediaries that require empowerement to effectively support
their local ecosystem to leverage space innovation and beyond.

terms of successfully driving value from space innovation.

skill areas that innovation should possess to effectively train
and provide business support services to innovators active (or
willing to get active) in the space and space-related sectors.
