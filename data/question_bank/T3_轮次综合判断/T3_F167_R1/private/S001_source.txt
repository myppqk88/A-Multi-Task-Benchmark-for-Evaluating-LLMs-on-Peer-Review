Abstract
Background: The European space sector is on the rise, introducing a
rapidly growing market with a lot of expanding opportunities for
innovative space products, applications and services, even in nonspace sectors. In this article, we investigate the skill levels of
innovation intermediaries that provide support to innovators (i.e.,
start-ups, scale-ups and SMEs) in the Central-Eastern (CE) and SouthEastern (SE) European space sector. Our objective is to identify: (i) the
available competences and (ii) the missing skills (potential areas for
improvement) of innovation intermediaries in the EU space sector.
Methods: Our methodological approach consists of (i) desk research
complemented by 15 semi-structured interviews which introduced a
baseline framework of innovation intermediaries’ desired knowledge
and skill levels, as well as (ii) an online survey, addressing the case of
innovation intermediaries and the innovators, respectively. We have
synthesized the key inferences that stem from the online survey,
building on the outcomes of a quantile analysis, the correlations
among various skill areas, and a regression analysis that we have
employed.
Results: By using statistical methods and regression analysis based
on a sample of 104 survey responses, several interesting insights for
the future of the European space innovation have been identified and
are highlighted in the present manuscript.
Conclusion: We conclude that innovation intermediaries in the space
sector have the adequate capacity to effectively train and support
innovators at improving, primarily, their organisational skills and soft
skills. Trainers are also competent at assessing the investment
readiness levels of innovators, their pitching skills, and they can
provide tailored business support services to innovators. In parallel, a
key finding of this study is that space innovators need to receive
better training to improve their business modelling skills, better

Plain language summary

The European space sector is on the rise, introducing a rapidly growing market with a lot of expanding opportunities for
innovative space products, applications and services, even in
non-space sectors. In order to exploit these opportunities,
entrepreneurs need substantial business support, in order to
build the knowledge and skills required to effectively benefit
from this erupting New Space era. This is even more intense in
Central-Eastern (CE) and South-Eastern (SE) Europe, where
only 30% of Europe’s space support innovation intermediaries
are established. In this article, we investigate the skill levels
of innovation intermediaries that provide support to innovators (i.e., start-ups, scale-ups and SMEs) of the SE and CE
European space sector. Our objective is to identify: (i) the available competences and (ii) missing skills (potential areas for
improvement) of innovation intermediaries in the EU space
sector, investigating their skill levels from two main points
of view: (a) the perception they have on their own skill levels in the provision of business support services; and (b) the
current perception of innovators who have received business
support services, in the past, with regard to the skill levels of
innovation intermediaries. We do so by doing desk research and
deploying an online survey. The survey results were analysed
using statistical methods and regression analysis, based on a
sample of 104 survey responses. Our research findings reveal
several interesting insights; we have identified potential skill
areas where innovation intermediaries in the EU space sector could improve, while, on the other hand, we have also spotted some strong skill areas of business trainers that could be
leveraged. The results of this analysis could be provide useful insights for the EU policy making in the field of innovation support, the development of tailored incubation and
acceleration programmes for supporting space entrepreneurship
in the EU sector, as well as for better informing internal business training programmes of large companies and SMEs in
the space sector.

1 Introduction

Since the advent of the New Space era the global space economy has been witnessing staggering growth and innovation.
Driven by major technological advances, a vibrant entrepreneurial spirit, an increasing appetite for investment as well as
renewed policy ambitions, the global space economy has grown
to an astounding turnover of $447 billion in 2020, continuing a
five-year growth, and is showing no signs of stopping1. Meanwhile, space technologies, data and services are finding increasing applications across many industries (e.g., from transport
and energy over to agriculture and environment), serving as an
enabling element for their digital transformation and providing the building blocks for innovative business models2. With
that in mind, it may come as no surprise that the global space
context is shifting, and it is shifting fast, as more and more
new players from the private sector enter the game and innovate, introducing remarkable cost reductions in accessing and
using space to create new solutions and capture value from a
broadening customer base.i

i

Notably, Morgan Stanley estimates that the new revenues induced by the space
industry in the global market may exceed the $1 trillion by the next 20 years
(https://www.morganstanley.com/ideas/investing-in-space).

In this context, a new kind of space race is on, with the
private sector competing to capture the unprecedented business opportunities of the New Space era. Despite its world-class
space sector and cutting edge systems in place (such as
Copernicus, Galileo and EGNOS)3, the EU appears to be falling
behind its global competitors (e.g., US or China) as many success stories (such as SpaceX and Blue Origin) emerge outside
of Europe with the support of suitable innovative industrial policies and financial support frames (e.g. first client approach,
technology agnostic anchor tenancy) as well as the increased
involvement of private investors. Given the vital importance
of space for key EU policies and political priorities (from
boosting competitiveness over to battling climate change and
fostering sustainable use of resources)4, lagging behind is not
an option. We need to quickly adapt and create an environment conducive to space entrepreneurship, incentivising and
supporting EU businesses to start-up and scale in order to
seize the opportunities ushered in by the shifting global space
context. All while championing the EU’s industrial leadership, boosting economic growth, creating jobs and delivering
social and environmental benefits to EU citizens.
Against the backdrop of developing European policies and support schemes for space innovation, several programmes and
initiatives have emerged at EU level, with a view to tackling
well-known barriers that are preventing start-ups from taking
up and/or growing their activities in the EU’s space sector (lack
of awareness on available opportunities, difficulties in accessing capital, limited business skills, risk-aversity, etc.)2. This
includes programmes for supporting space innovation through
funding instruments such as the EU’s Horizon 2020 and the most
recent Hoziron Europe, the European Investment Bank (EIB)
European Fund for Strategic Investments and the European
Space Agency (ESA) Technology Research Programme as well
as initiatives geared more to start-ups (incubators, accelerators,
hackathons, etc.), fostering the use of available EU space systems, data and services in commercial applications (Copernicus
Start-Up Programme, E-GNSS Accelerator, Galileo Masters, etc.).
Space hubs have a vital role to play in helping start-ups to identify and make the most out of suitable opportunities available
at EU level, while at the same time catalysing collaborations
and investments that can support their crucial early stages and
scale up. Indeed, through local support initiatives, space hubs
can raise awareness on the business opportunities offered by
space technologies, data and services as well as help space
start-ups and scale-ups access the necessary knowledge (technical and/or business skills, technologies, licenses, intellectual
property, etc.), networks (business partners, first customers, team
members, etc.) and financial solutions (grants, credit, venture
capital, etc.) in order to successfully seize them. At the same time,
they are well-positioned to mobilise and bring together stakeholders from space and non-space sectors (e.g., entrepreneurs,
researchers, industrial actors, users, investors), with a view to
exploring new applications, business ideas and (co-)financing
schemes for leveraging space technologies, data and services
to deliver demand-driven solutions for non-space sectors (e.g.,
agriculture, health, ICT), creating new business opportunities and stimulating entrepreneurs to get new ventures off the
ground5.

European policy makers have already acknowledged the vital
role of space hubs in the Space Strategy for Europe, recognising them as a powerful platform for shaping favourable business ecosystems along with enabling framework conditions for
space entrepreneurship2. In fact, there are already several space
hubs that are deploying local initiatives and catalysing space
entrepreneurship in the EU. However, these space hubs are
not equally distributed across Europe. The majority of them
are concentrated in countries that have been historically more
involved in the aerospace field (e.g. France, Germany and Italy).
Countries in the CE and SE Europe, be they with emerging or
existing space capacity, have many vibrant technology ecosystems and industry verticals as well as user communities with great
potential for contributing to the increased commercialisation
of space (including spinning-in and -out opportunities). For
instance, more than 10 unicorns have sprung out of CEE countries (estimated combined value of =C30 billion), with a promising roster of start-ups currently on the rise (e.g. UIPath in
robotics, Kiwi.com in transport, Prezi in software, Flo in health)
and around =C700 million invested in CEE start-ups in 2018
alone (e.g. Taxify, Citybee)6. The startup ecosystem in SEE
is alive and kicking too, with over 5,000 SEE start-ups estimated to have raised more than $50 million by 2016, and a
surge of new start-ups, support actors and schemes in 20187.
Moreover, the CE and SE Europe are abundant with talent offering a highly skilled and educated tech workforce with great
potential for space innovation. Over the last twenty years, the
regions of interest are showing positive signs in terms of labour
productivity growth, leading to economic growthii, and therefore converging to the EU average8. The same also applies to
the emerging levels of Total Factor Productivity (TFP) in the
region, which have been also significantly improved. After all,
higher TFP is linked with economic growth and reflects ability of an economy to effectively apply innovative techniques
and produce products and services with high added value. The
new era and the transformation of many CE and SE European
economies requires that innovation will be a key component
in their new growth models to foster prosperity even further.
More importantly, given that the core of the space sector is
comprised mostly of development of cutting-edge technologies
and applications, it becomes apparent that the advancement
of the space ecosystem in the CE and SE Europe will be positively impacted by an enabling environment for the overall
innovation landscape in the region, be it support to local innovation intermediaries or the establishment of new initiatives
to support space innovators.
At the same time, it is also essential to ensure that these innovation intermediaries have all the necessary knowledge, skills,
resources and tools in place to effectively organise their local
initiatives and support their portfolio of innovators, and enable
them to engage and drive value from space innovation, bringing
the EU at the forefront of the global new space race. Towards
this direction, this manuscript, which covers work performed

under the EU-funded H2020 InnORBIT project (GA No
101004212)iii, introduces a thorough investigation on the skill
levels and knowledge gaps of such innovation intermediaries
that provide support to innovators of the SE and CE European
space sector. To the authors’ knowledge such investigation
in the region examined, has not been performed before, and
it offers valuable insight not only for the direct market actors
involved, but the overall New Space community, with emphasis on
European policy makers.
This study offers novel findings to the existing literature that
can be organised in 4 main pillars. First, we consider the implications for potential training programmes of an extensive analysis of the current skill levels and knowledge gaps of members
of innovation intermediaries. We offer new insights for the
market-driven design and development of new initiatives to
build the business skills and space knowledge capacity of
trainers, coaches and innovation advisors in the CE and SE
Europe and beyond. Secondly, and in line with the previous pillar, we also offer practical suggestions for new courses
and training content that could be consider in future capacity
building initiatives in the area. In parallel, our third pillar
of findings, deals with investigation of the current perceptions of innovators in the CE and SE Europe and the analysis
of their inherent needs in terms of business support. By doing
so, we provide new insights and suggestions on the desired
content for the design and development of new business support programmes that will target space innovators in the CE
and SE Europe and beyond. Lastly, our study also contributes to design tools for supporting space entrepreneurship
in the CE and SE Europe, via analysing the needs and challenges that the local innovation ecosystem phases and by offering potential skill areas (be it business skills or knowledge
areas in the domain) for improvement.
The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2
presents some insight regarding similar endeavours in the literature. Section 3 introduces the methodology that was followed,
including related work on the field that was analysed at that
point. Results of this methodology, along with an extended discussion over the main findings are documented in Section 4.
Finally, Section 5 concludes the manuscript with some key
insights derived from the presented analysis.

2 Related work

The role of innovation intermediaries in bridging the knowledge gaps is widely discussed internationally, either via bringing together different actors of the innovation ecosystem (e.g.,
innovators, investors, funding providers, advisors, policymakers,
etc.) or via supporting the diffusion of technology and novel
ideas across different industries and technological domains. An
intermediary facilitates the knowledge exchange and knowledge transfer to a customer through various channels such as
(i) direct knowledge transfer; (ii) exchange of experience;
(iii) networking; and (iv) consulting on companies’ innovation
needs and available solutions9. After all, technology transfer is a
time-based process involving multiple actors, while innovation

A key component of the existing literature that is currently missing, to the authors’ knowledge, is a dedicated analysis of the
knowledge and skill levels that innovation intermediaries believe
they possess when they provide business support services.
In parallel, it is also interesting to investigate the intermediaries’ skills needed by the corresponding counterparts who act
as receivers of these skills and services (innovators). Intermediaries’ knowledge and skill levels in certain business areas
exert a key influence on the quality of the services they provide
towards innovators.
Motivated by this, Gassman et. al. (2011)10 have analysed
107 European manufacturing companies and six collaborative
cross-industry innovation projects to reveal the core skills and
expertise an effective intermediary should possess in supporting cross-industry innovation. Based on their results, effective
intermediaries should have the adequate capacity to identify
the companies’ core competencies, technological capabilities, and problems in a more abstract yet standardised manner
(compared to internal company experts). On top of that, the study
of Gassman et. al. (2011)10 revealed that technical know-how
and networking skills are also desired elements in the service
portfolio of an intermediary, based on the needs of innovators.
Using regression analysis, Ibrahim et al. (2017)11 highlight
the importance of employees’ soft skill acquisition. They conclude that innovators and managers need to induce new training
methods and curricula in order to enable an effective framework that reinforces soft skills in the company. Given that such
training programmes are provided, most of the time, by specialised business trainers and coaches, the aspect of soft skills
should be considered as a desired element of an effective
innovation intermediary.
Piispanen et al. (2017)12 indicate that entrepreneurs should
possess core business knowledge and skills to enhance their
business development. In a similar vein, Anwar (2018)13 identifies the Business Model Innovation (BMI) as a core driver for
an innovator’s better performance, influencing positively its
comparative advantage, especially in growing industries like the
space industry. Many studies also pinpoint that entrepreneurship is significantly boosted when innovators adopt new digital technologies, upgrade their digital capabilities, and follow
a concrete digital strategy, helping them to become innovation
leaders (Khin and Ho, 201914, Ismail et al., 201715, Sousa and
Rocha, 201916). These findings suggest that digital and organisational skills should constitute a core area of the effective
innovation intermediary.
Within the space domain, clusters, the most common form of
innovation intermediary, are an integral aspect that evolves rapidly along the space economy. In a recent thorough international comparison on space cluster development17, the authors
have found that “skills are ‘sticky’ and drive the space economy in the long term”. Nevertheless, in most if not all cases
examined, the space clusters are highly dependent on external
entities, such as universities, and even in huge organisations, such
as NASA, there is a risk of skills shortage due to an ageing

workforce. With all of that in mind, it is difficult to imagine
how space clusters can effectively support innovation without
being up-skilled or re-skilled, not only to remain competitive
within an erupting market, but also to act as drivers and
enablers for the innovator ecosystem. Moreover, according to the
Space Skills Allianceiv, SMEs in the downstream sector, are in
need of a more diverse skill set in their workforce, which in
most cases cannot be found, mainly due to the lack of resources
to invest in up-skilling, but also due to the limited capacity
of the available trainers.
There are sparse findings in the literature regarding skills assessment in the space industry. A recent skills gap assessment in
Australia18, revealed quite interesting findings, starting with
the fact that there is a clear shift from technical to soft skills
and space sector enabling skills (specialised business, finance,
regulatory, legal skills), which are even considered critical for
the current market. This, along with the shortages identified
in almost all of the skills categories in this study, highlights
the necessity for up-skilling and knowledge transfer.
The existing literature, although not specifically addressing the
issue of intermediaries’ skills in the space industry, offers a variety of desired attributes and skill sets that an effective business trainer/coach should possess. Despite the several factors
that could be considered in a framework model for the assessment of the skill perceptions of such trainers, we can mostly
identify four key aspects. Namely, these aspects refer to the
business skills of intermediaries, their capacity to support
innovators at embracing digital transformation and designing
a digital strategy, as well as their ability to improve the soft
skill of innovators and their networking with relevant innovation actors. This analysis of our framework model for intermediaries’ skills is further upended by also studying the key insights
stemming from other relevant EU-funded R&D projects (see
Table 1), focusing on innovation and business support.

3.2 Step 2: Semi-structured interviews
The second step in our approach further calibrates the initial
baseline framework and distinguishes between the critical
and non-critical skills of innovation intermediaries. The goal
here is to identify any skill areas and dimensions not previously
envisaged in our initial framework (Step 1). Therefore, we validate our initial findings, based on the views of experts belonging in our two stakeholder groups (innovation intermediaries
and innovators). This exercise reveals which specific skills and
skill areas should be included in the questionnaire of the online
survey, fuelling the activities of Step 3. Since detailed information is not always available online, we pursued close consultations with various public and private stakeholders from
the targeted regions via a series of semi-structured interviews.
To start the process, an interview guideline that comprised of a
summary of the InnORBIT project, an overview of the objectives of the interview and a baseline framework to guide the
discussion, was prepared. The baseline framework ensures that
the conversations stay focused on the topic and that no important aspects are neglected, but at the same time, it allows for
sufficient room for discussion and refinement of the framework.
By engaging InnORBIT partners´ networks we identified
highly experienced innovation intermediaries and innovators,
which we sought to interview about their perspectives on innovation intermediaries´ required skills for delivering business
support services, focusing on the space industry dimension
and its applications. Two interview questionnaires were developed, facilitating consultations with the groups of innovators
(namely: entrepreneurs, start-ups, and scale-ups) and innovation
intermediaries, respectively.
During interview sessions, the baseline framework was presented to experts, with a view to defining the types of skills
innovation intermediaries need to possess in order to provide
effective business support to entrepreneurs. Depending on the
stakeholder group, experts were asked to validate the baseline
framework from the point of view of either (i) innovation intermediaries; as providers of support services, or (ii) innovators;
as recipients of such services.
The validation process has been carried out via quantitative
and qualitative means. More specifically, interviewees were
asked to rate the significance of skills categories and specific
skills included in our baseline framework based on a scale
from 1 to 5, with 1 assigned to “not important” skills and 5 to
“absolutely important” skills. A skill is considered validated,
as long as its average rating and its mode (i.e., the value that
appears most frequently in a data set) exceed the value of 3 (average level). This thresholds derives naturally from the design
of the Likert scale response, under which any value above 3
is perceived positively. At the same time, respondents were
requested to identify those skills or skills categories missing
from our baseline framework as well as to highlight any changes
needed in the definition and classification of skills included.

In total, we performed 15 interviews (9 with innovation intermediaries and 6 with innovators across the EU) via digital
means; interviews’ insights are collected, synthesized, and
analysed further, ultimately leading to the update of the initial
baseline framework. The fine-tuned version of our baseline
framework built the premises for developing and deploying
an online survey under Step 3.

3.3 Step 3: Online survey
In the third step, we deploy an online survey to reveal the skill
levels of space-related innovation intermediaries based on
(i) their own perceptions and (ii) the perspectives of innovators who received these business support skills and services.
While this exercise has mostly a geographical focus on the ecosystems of CE and SE Europe, we are also keen to capture
the broader perspective of stakeholders from more developed
ecosystems (EU level), who either provide business support
services to innovators in the region of our focus or have business relationships with innovators in the region. Thus, the
broader perspective of our study offers richer insights on the
business support landscape and the desired skills of innovation intermediaries that are currently valued by innovators
and by market forces in space-related domains.
We divide each sub-section of the current manuscript based
on each skill level category that is investigated in the survey,
namely (i) Knowledge in the Space Industry, (ii) Business Assessment Knowledge, (iii) Business Support Skills, (iv) Organisational and Digital Skills, and (v) Soft Skills. To study in-depth
each core skill area, we used a set of well-tailored Likert-scale
survey questions, addressing specific business sub-element of
each core skill area. Specifically, we employed a 5-level scale,
with 1 indicating low levels of skills and 5 indicating high
levels of skills.
The selected skill areas and questions of the survey are based on
the fine-tuned framework developed under Step 2. The survey
was implemented online via a survey tool (i.e. EUSurveyvi) to
ensure a broad and effective data collection process. In particular, two separate and anonymous survey questionnaires were
developed. The first addresses the perceived skills and needs of
innovation intermediaries, namely the perceptions of advisors,
trainers, coaches, and mentors belonging to clusters, incubators,
accelerators and SME support organisations in space-related
and other sectors. The second addresses the views of innovators; we asked them to indicate their experiences and knowledge
received from their participation in training with innovation
intermediaries, and more specifically, we investigate the skill
levels of innovation intermediaries from an innovator’s point
of view. In essence, we analyse the same factor (namely, innovation intermediaries’ skills and needs) from two different
angles: the service provider side (innovation intermediaries) and
the service recipient side (innovators). This approach enables us to reveal any differences in the perceptions of the two
groups under investigation, examine how these perceptions
vary, and extract informed implications for the core objectives

3.4 Step 4: analysis
The last part of our approach delves into insights stemming
from the online survey. Our objective was to identify deficit patterns amongst innovation intermediaries (current vs. necessary
skills/knowledge) that need to be addressed via capacity building
services, as well as to use inferential analytics exploring potential relations and patterns to produce intelligence for fine-tuning
the business support services to be provided by space innovation
related local support initiatives.
Survey results were gathered, consolidated, and analysed. We
employed relevant statistical tools (i.e., GRETLviii) to identify patterns and relationships among the different sets of skill
areas. To start, we conducted a correlation analysis, to reveal
how each skill area in our survey is associated with others.
While correlation quantifies the direction and strength of the
relationship between two skill areas, we also introduced and
estimated a multiple linear regression model, based on the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method. By doing so, we assessed
the association among the different skill areas and needs using
survey data. Our statistical approach models the various skill
areas (treating them as a set of variables) through a stochastic
equation of the form:
Y = a + b1 X1 + b2 X 2 + b3 X + b4 X 4 + e

4 Results and discussion
4.1 Initial baseline framework and fine-tuning

We first gathered desk research findings from other EU-funded
R&D projects (see Table 1, in order to construct a baseline
framework of the most essential skills innovation intermediaries
should possess to effectively train and provide business support
services to innovators active in the space and space-related sectors. This exercise was a key component of our research because
it gave us a starting point to rely on our next steps. By utilising
this initial framework, we were able to depict the key skill levels
that were to be investigated, while it also enabled us to fine-tune
these skills based on partners’ perspectives and experts’ views.
Our preliminary research findings yielded several skills that
were grouped in five knowledge and skill areas. These areas
include:
• Knowledge of the space industry
• Knowledge to perform business assessment
• Organisational and digital skills
• Business support skills
• Soft skills
The five knowledge and skill areas along with a more detailed
enumeration of each of them are illustrated in Figure 2.
Subsequently to the first step, we implemented a series of semistructured interviews with senior experts in the field, representing both stakeholder groups - innovation intermediaries and
innovators.
Our sampling approach yields 15 suitable candidates spread
across five countries in CE and SE Europe. More specifically,
the majority of interviewees are based in Greece and Croatia,
with 6 and 5 experts respectively, followed by Romania with
2 and the Czech Republic and Hungary with 1 interviewee
each. In total, 9 experts of innovation intermediaries and 6
innovators from start-ups and SMEs were interviewed. All
interviewees hold top-level positions in their respective organisations based on their job description. The breakdown of
interviewees per target group (innovation intermediary or
innovator) in each country is presented in Figure 3.

support businesses with activity in technical domains such as

4.2 Statistical analysis of survey results
The online surveys (see the Appendix) run for a period of
approximately one month, namely from the 24th of May 2021
until the 18th of June 2021. In total, we received 104 valid
answers from innovation intermediaries and entrepreneurs,
start-ups and scale-ups based in Europe, focusing on the CE
and SE Europe (58 from innovation intermediaries and 46
from innovators). During the survey, we adhered to the GDPR
provisions, while no personal data were collected, processed,
and/or generated. The complete dataset of the survey performed
is available on Zenodo27.

4.2.2 Overall knowledge and skill areas assessment. In this
section, we analyse the survey data to extract meaningful insights
for the skill levels of the trainers/coaches/mentors of innovation intermediaries at providing business support to space-related
innovators. We investigate the skill levels from two angles; on
the one hand, from the skill levels that the innovation intermediaries believe they have (perceived skills), and on the other
hand, from the perspective of innovators concerning the skill
levels of innovation intermediaries (business support services
provided to innovators). This approach enables us to conduct
a skill level analysis relying on both stakeholder groups, allowing a direct comparison, as can be seen by the results in
Table 3.

56% of them were classified as innovation intermediaries,

our sample covers innovation intermediaries and innovators

also ensured representation of the CE and SE European regions
since 79% of responses stem from this area (Figure 5).
Out of all innovators involved in the survey, almost 54% indicated that they have received in the past any kind of business
support and training. Notably, most of them, have collaborated
with business incubators and accelerators, followed by clusters

evaluated close to “strong competence” or “very strong competence” clearly point that there are no significant training needs
in this skill area (see the Figure 15). Respondents highlight
that they do not need any training support in any of the

constituent competences of this segment – services like the
design of incubation programmes, the management and operation of new initiatives, as well as the scouting of innovative
ventures to get business support stand out as the highest scoring
skill levels of innovation intermediaries.
Our analysis of innovators’ perspective leads to the same conclusion: innovation intermediaries mostly possess very strong
skills and competences in their organisational skills portfolio(see

skills, meaning that they can effectively use the right digital
tools to provide support services to innovators. The only area
that could potentially be improved, according to innovators’
perspective, is the way that trainers and advisors build business and financial skills. The figure that follows illustrates the
outcomes of our survey and validates the statements above.
4.2.7 Soft skills. Soft skills received the highest acceptance of
all survey respondents, having the highest scoring in the different skill areas under this study. More specifically, under
this area, we trace some of the highest-scoring trainers’ skills,
including their ability to establish trust with SME members, the
ability to share know-how and skills with innovators, as well as
their levels of emotional and social intelligence. Intermediaries
(Figure 17) also feel that they can understand the needs and

5 Conclusion

We have synthesized the key inferences that stem from the
online survey, building on the outcomes of the quantile analysis, the correlations among various skill areas, and the regression
analysis we employed. In this section, we present the conclusions of our study concerning the (i) available and missing
skills of local innovation intermediaries with respect to effectively supporting space innovation as well as (ii) needs of
local ecosystems of entrepreneurs, startups, and scale-ups in
terms of successfully driving value from space innovation.
The key insights of this report are summarised below (see also
Figure 20 and Figure 21 for an illustration of the key insights
stemming from this study).
We conclude that innovation intermediaries under investigation
have the adequate capacity to effectively train and support
innovators at improving primarily their organisational skills
and soft skills. Along the same lines, trainers are also competent
at assessing the investment readiness levels of innovators, and
their pitching skills, as well as they can also provide tailored
business support services to innovators. After all, we have identified a positive correlation between the soft skills of innovation intermediaries and their inherent ability to evaluate
the business potential of innovators.

On the other hand, a key finding of this study is that space innovators need to receive better training to improve their business modelling skills, support the management of their business
Intellectual Property, and understand key technologies of the
space market. Based on the latter, space innovators need also
to better identify how specific space technologies can be used
in other domains and industries, while innovation intermediaries, having a more horizontal knowledge on the potential
applications of technological innovations, could act as facilitators in this process. Interestingly, the key role of financing
as a core need of space entrepreneurs is validated, since most
of the innovators in our study highlighted that they need to
receive better support from innovation intermediaries at better
identifying funding and financing opportunities that are available. For an illustration of the key outcomes and conclusions
of this study, please also refer to Figure 19.
The results of this study are, of course, not exhaustive and need
to be interpreted with caution. The assessment of innovation
trainers’ and coaches’ skill and knowledge levels is a difficult
and highly dynamic exercise. There is no inherent homogeneity, nor specific norms in the core competencies of business
trainers across the CEE/SEE, since this aspect significant
differentiates form one person to another, and from one innovation intermediary to another. However, our analysis has tried to
conceptualise the core elements of innovation intermediaries
business skills and attributes when they train innovators, so as
to provide an indicative framework of core competencies an
missing skills in order to properly inform the development of
future business support and capacity building programmes,
with specific focus on space industry and space entrepreneurs.

ordered probit or ordered logit model or structural equation modelling (see a standard
econometrics textbook, for instance Wooldridge or Greene). Given the sample size, I
suggest to use the ordered probit or ordered logit model.
2. The quoted literature only partly motivates the empirical model. This paper is about
competencies and skills in the space industry, and not about performance.
3. The empirical model and the specification are not satisfactory. One type of skill cannot be
related to other types. Of course, different types of skills are correlated. I cannot see the
