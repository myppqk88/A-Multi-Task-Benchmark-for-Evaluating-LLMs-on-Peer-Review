They constitute complex phenomena that cut across sectors\nand disciplines and give rise to other elements that simultaneously\nreject and encourage them. For many formal stakeholders in the\neconomy, they are an enemy to be beaten; for the authorities,\ninformal activity is seen as a loss of revenue for the state coffers; for\nthe Sustainable Development Goals, by implicitly recognizing them in\ngoal 8, they constitute a paradigm shift. Meanwhile, the reality for\nthose involved in the informal economy is that it is a way of life and\nnot a mere choice, one that leads to the most social of all economies:\nthat of necessity. There is no consensus among academics on\ninformality and its ramifications, hence the need to analyze the\nprocesses of informal economy from its theoretical construction with\nthe purpose of discovering its range and depth, as well as its\ninterrelationships and theoretical implications. To achieve this, 102\ndefinitions of informal economy were analyzed by identifying and\ndeconstructing their dimensions and performing a frequency count of\ntheir citation in Google Scholar. This analysis demonstrated the lack of\ncultural elements in the definitions, which are the true underlying\ncause of the phenomenon, and the over-prominence afforded to legal\ndimensions.\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nThe world progresses, but its principal problems of poverty\nand inequality remain. Much of globalized society is engrossed\nin what they see as the pressing reality of climate change,\nderived from the exhaustion and mistreatment of the earth, and\nexacerbated by the unprecedented consumption of goods and\nservices. Meanwhile, another part lives in an abyss of labor\nexploitation that is either overt and unabashed, or disguised\nthrough the legal processes of outsourcing, relocation, and\ninternational fragmentation of the value chain. Corruption\nand economic domination have produced a new de facto social\nclass in developed countries: the precariat (Guamán & Luque,\n2019; Luque et al., 2016; Standing, 2013). A great part of the\nworld’s citizens finds themselves in this chasm, having been\nforced out of their natural ecosystem (Sassen, 2015); in this\nabyss, any means of survival is valid including the processes\nof the informal economy. These comprise more than 90% of\nmicro and small enterprises (MYPE) globally (International\nLabour Organization [ILO], 2019) as well as a significant part\nof the rural economy in developing countries (ILO, 2020b). In\nfact, the informal economy accounts for 10–20% of GDP in\nwell-established economies and up to 60% in emerging economies (Schneider & Enste, 2002), and two billion people worldwide are employed informally, which represents more than\n60% of the global workforce. 93% of all informal employment is in emerging and developing countries: in Africa,\n85.8% of jobs are informal; the proportion is 68.2% in\nAsia and the Pacific, 68.6% in the Arab States, 40.0% in the\nAmericas, and 25.1% in Europe and Central Asia (ILO, 2018).\nIn Latin America and the Caribbean, about 140 million workers\nare in a situation of informality; according to Salazar-Xirinachs\nand Chacaltana (2018, p.15):\n\u0007“These are workers who are not covered by labor law and\nare not, therefore, subject to the relevant formal regulations of the world of work or employment justice; they make\nno social security contributions and, consequently, do not\nparticipate in the welfare state or the social contract that\ncharacterizes modern societies; they mostly have lowproductivity jobs and, as a result, their incomes are low.\nAt worst, many of them live in poverty or extreme poverty;\nand at best, they comprise what analysts call \"vulnerable\ngroups”.\nIn response to this panorama, the member states of the United\nNations agreed on the creation of the 2030 Agenda ([external resource identifier omitted]\nun.org/2030agenda), which covers the three dimensions of\nsustainability (the economic, the social, and the environmental), and includes 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)\n([external resource identifier omitted] The eighth of these SDGs has the\naim of promoting sustained, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth, full and productive employment and decent work\nfor all. Nonetheless, economic deprivation conditions much of human existence,\nabove all, quality of life and the prospects of the next generation. Poverty has detrimental consequences even for the lives of\nthe rich: a society in which greater equality is achieved on the\nbasis of the processes of true equity and a state that implements\nstrong social policies will tend to be more peaceful and settled and\nobtain greater cost savings (Wilkinson & Pickett, 2011). Such models\nrely entirely on goodwill but lack any enforceability (Ruggie,\n2001). At the\nsame time, processes of corruption are occasionally sponsored\nby (usually transnational) companies in order to undermine\nthe labor market, while monopsony stretches the very fabric of\nsmall and medium-sized enterprises (Luque, 2018). From new forms of poverty emerge new classes\nof the poor: the victims of climate, politics, economics and\nhunger, they range from refugees to academics (Oliván &\nLuque, 2019). The constructs themselves include aspects far removed from the needs or existing\nrealities of the phenomenon, making them complex to understand (Han, 2020). The study undertook an analysis of reasoned\ntheoretical approaches that contain epistemological problems,\nwhich highlights the difficulty of providing generalized solutions\nto the many questions and dilemmas that surround this topic\n(Fourez, 1994). The study consisted of three steps: 1) A search\nwas carried out for definitions of the informal economy and\nrelated constructs through the existing literature using indexed\ndatabases, which in turn were part of Google Scholar (https://\nscholar.google.com/). The selection of the indexed academic\ndatabases to be used were: Web of Science, Scopus and Latindex with no time limit on date of publication or type of\n\n\npaper. Both English language and Spanish publications were\nincluded in the search between 12/09/2020 and 24/11/2020. 102\ndefinitions were found by this method between 12/09/2020 and\n24/11/2020 (see Table 8; Luque, 2021). Figure 1 provides a\nsummary of the steps carried out in the process of definition\nselection with inclusion criteria and subsequent categorization. From this, a large group of 102 linked units of analysis\n(UA) was obtained, which was used to define the concept under\nstudy (see Table 8; Luque, 2021) and to convert verbal or\nvisual data into numerical data for the purposes of analysis\n(Bourque, 2004).\n2) Seven dimensions were identified through content analysis\nby applying a manual coding-approximation process, from\nwhich the general description and, subsequently, the scope of\neach dimension were obtained as outlined below. 3) A frequency\ncount in Google Scholar of all definitions was performed,\nmeasuring the impact through citations to then calculate the\nrelative use of each dimension. It is necessary to understand\nthat the relationship of the identified constructs is not of equal\nsignificance, the most widely used having a greater impact\n(Donahue et al., 2015; Howes & Solomon, 1951; Kageura &\nUmino, 1996; Luque & Herrero-García, 2019; Murphy, 1992).\nThe relative usage of each definition can be obtained by comparing the frequency counts in an internet search engine. It is\nempirically evident that the internet provides frequency counts\nthat are of great validity compared to other linguistic databases.\nGoogle Scholar, an engine specialized in scientific-academic\nsearches that orders results by relevance, is one of the most\neffective free-access academic search engines globally.\n\nEstablishing the dimensions and manual coding\nscheme in informality processes\nThe definitions were gathered through an extensive literature\nreview, which consisted of journal articles, technical reports, and\nweb pages. Dimension selection and classification was undertaken keeping in mind the depth, scope, and interconnectivity\npresent in informal economic processes (Luque & Herrero-García,\n2019). This was achieved through the following procedure:\na) The asic ideas to be used in the structuring of informality processes were defined, which in turn would serve to guide\nthe research design; b) Once all of the definitions and approximations had been read, their depth and implications were\nobserved and noted (Bulmer, 1979; Nosofsky, 1986); c) From\nthere, a theoretical framework was built for each dimension, justified by their treatment in the existing academic literature: in\nthis case, seven were required. The same systematization can be\nreplicated for other research, adapting the number of dimensions\nin relation to the existing literature that justifies them; d) Each\ndefinition of informality was analyzed at a conceptual level to\ndiscover whether it contained the preconfigured implications in each one of the dimensions; if so, they were included\nin Table 8 for subsequent counting and analysis. See Luque\n(2021).\n\n\nThe economic dimension is directly related to the living\nconditions of people engaged in informal activities and,\ntherefore, to informal economic processes. It must be made\nreasonably easy for all public-private actors to introduce more\nequitable and progressive aspects at the fiscal and legal level,\nand to articulate direct relationships between all types of companies and the immediate environment in which goods are\nproduced or services are managed. The situation must also be\nopen to regulation by understanding enterprises to be one more\nliving element of the community rather than immovable and\nsacrosanct institutions.\nThe legal dimension considers the development and establishment of legislative and regulatory measures, especially those\nthat have a bearing on the general interest. Among the most\nimportant are the processes of liberalization, with clear benefits\nfor the ruling classes, such as: deregulation of markets; collusion of supranational bodies; lack of competition; protection of\nmonopolies; creation of bespoke legislation; veiling of the activities of lobbies; caps on democratic processes; privatizations of\nprofitable public enterprises; tax reforms in favor of the prevailing economic model; protection of economic rights over and\nabove human rights. To these can be added regulatory asymmetries in free trade agreements with inadequate means of\nredress or democratic control, the imposition of private arbitration tribunals that undermine domestic jurisdiction as a means of\ndominating nation states, and the imposition of tax-containment\nprocesses tailored to the needs of transnational corporations (Luque & de Pablos, 2016). The social dimension has\na clear relationship with the processes of common welfare.\nThere is an overlap of interests and relationships between\nindividuals and life in society since individuals need others to fulfill their requirements. Therefore, it is necessary to\ndevelop all available tools, both those that examine the endogenous factors inherent to continuous learning processes, and\nthose of an exogenous nature derived from the environment\nand based on continuous feedback; both have quality of life\nas their common denominator. Society responds to threats\nand aggressions of all kinds, such as the processes of exclusion and expulsion, gentrification, and economic domination;\nthe informal economy is a clear expression of a social\nresponse to these abuses that is both rapid and proportionate. The political dimension is based on the social organization of coexistence. Both individuals and their environment are\nplaced at the center of the social construction and form its\nnucleus. The interests of any society share common elements\nsuch as beliefs and values but also dissonant aspects such as the\norganizational model, processes of exclusion (by act or omission), or priorities implicit in government interventions. Within\nthis context, civil society articulates its mechanisms of action,\nincluding civil disobedience as legitimate protest; these\ninclude unlicensed selling, peaceful resistance or the adoption\nof policies within the regulated social economy. Indeed, these\npolicies seek to address real needs rather than maximize\nthe economic benefit of the democratic majority. The ruling\nclasses tend to regard informal economic processes as a source\nof illicit income and a feature of those who abuse their citizenship status, even to the extent of seeing them as an anti-system\nelement, yet the great majority carry out informal activities as a\nform of subsistence to avoid poverty or as a response to exclusion.\n\nThe cultural dimension is not confined to intellectual aspects\nand also not reserved for the economic elite or well-off. Rather,\nit is a construct of accessible elements in continuous motion.\nCulture is not always clearly defined, but it constitutes all kinds\nof expressions and is a clear element of vitality in society,\na predictor of freedom and development. It may be true that the\ninformal economy has an element of the anti-establishment,\nbut informality as a recourse to covering basic needs is a clear\nexample of the strength of a democracy. The ethical dimension is a permeable membrane between effective decisionmaking by citizens involved in the informal economy and\ntheir practical consequences. There is clear freedom of action\nwhen it comes to choosing what to produce, how to produce\nit, and how it will be distributed with a clear social commitment. This is based less on accumulative processes of wealth\nand more on the common good. However, three underlying limitations emerge: 1) The personal, based on less\navaricious imperatives that are limited by the legal system, the\nfamily, and environment; 2) The organizational, derived from\ncorporate and public policies exempt from limitations or control and enjoying regulations tailored to their interests; 3) The\nsocial, in which many of the mechanisms for the detection and\ncontainment of systemic problems are ineffective. These limitations persist despite the knowledge of such pathologies within\na society that, recognizing its own deficiencies, is lacking\nin the tools for their remedy (Bauman, 1999). The labor dimension not only analyzes the labor market or its characteristics, but\nthe qualitative aspects of conditions for carrying out work for\nwhich legitimate access is denied by regulations. This dimension departs from the structuralist vision of development,\ntaking an inclusive view of all kinds of activities that generate\nvalue within an ethical framework. Measures should be adopted\nto bring informal activities within the fold of the common good\nand in line with existing circumstances such as family and the\nancestral traditions and practices of ethnic groups. All societies experience\nthem and suffer their consequences to a greater or lesser\nextent, although it should be remembered that the vast majority of people are immersed in them not out of choice, but\nrather as a means of subsistence. Criminalizing informality\nwithout providing assistance or solutions only highlights the\nfailings of a current globalized society beset with vested interests and lacking the means of redress or misusing the available resources in developmentalist projects such as strengthening\nthe army (together with the constant drain of buying materiel),\nroadbuilding, and tax exemptions for polluting vehicles. This\nis compounded by the establishment and defense of policies\nthat move away from the processes of social economy in favor\nof protectionist legislation that force many out of the system\nand necessitate their involvement in the informal economy.\nThe promulgation of the sustainable development goals is a\nfirst step in establishing decent work as the standard and not the\nexception. The political and social commitment they represent\nshould be striven for, so that this battery of measures might not\nbe corrupted by the surge of vested interests, as has happened\nwith the processes of corporate social responsibility (full\nof good intentions without any enforceability beyond its\npositive impact on corporate dividends or the public image of\nmanagement).\nThe categorization of informality was saturated in the seven\nbasic areas of economic, legal, social, political, cultural, ethical, and labor dimensions. From the amalgamation of these, the\nmeta-concept of the informal economy—not yet described in the\nliterature—can be derived. However, not all categories contribute equally. The results show that a tetrad model is best suited to\n\ndescribe the sophisticated connections that exist between the\ndifferent categories. Of these, the cultural dimension has been\nthe most overlooked in academic publications, confirming\nits devaluation in productivist views, despite the myriad opportunities it offers; meanwhile, the legal aspect is most strongly\nrooted in published conceptualizations (excepting the economic\ndimension, which dominates for obvious reasons) in trying to\neliminate informality through the exercise of the rule of law and\nregulatory frameworks, including the occasional promotion of\ninstitutional violence. These results are consistent with both\nthe number of connections that are established between these\ncategories and their individual inputs. Perhaps most surprising\nis the relative lack of importance attached to the social and labor\ndimensions, which highlights the contradiction of an academic\ncommunity that represents the civilized world yet appears to be\ncloser to the abyss than to the solution.\n\nData availability\n\nUnderlying data\nFigshare: Analysis of the concept of informal economy through\n102 definitions: legality or necessity. All societies experience\nthem and suffer their consequences to a greater or lesser extent,\nalthough it should be remembered that the vast majority of\npeople are immersed in them not out of choice, but rather as\na means of subsistence. Criminalizing informality without\nproviding assistance or solutions only highlights the failings of\na current globalized society beset with vested interests and lacking the means of redress or misusing the available resources\nin developmentalist projects such as strengthening the army\n(together with the constant drain of buying materiel), roadbuilding, and tax exemptions for polluting vehicles. This is compounded\nby the establishment and defense of policies that move away\nfrom the processes of social economy in favor of protectionist\nlegislation that force many out of the system and necessitate\ntheir involvement in the informal economy. The promulgation of\nthe sustainable development goals is a first step in establishing\ndecent work as the standard and not the exception. The political and social commitment they represent should be striven for,\nso that this battery of measures might not be corrupted by the\nsurge of vested interests, as has happened with the processes\nof corporate social responsibility (full of good intentions without any enforceability beyond its positive impact on corporate\ndividends or the public image of management).\n\nThe categorization of informality was saturated in the seven\nbasic areas of economic, legal, social, political, cultural, ethical, and labor dimensions. From the amalgamation of these, the\nmeta-concept of the informal economy—not yet described in the\nliterature—can be derived. However, not all categories contribute equally. The results show that a tetrad model is best suited\nto describe the sophisticated connections that exist between\nthe different categories. Of these, the cultural dimension has\nbeen the most overlooked in academic publications, confirming\nits devaluation in productivist views, despite the myriad opportunities it offers; meanwhile, the legal aspect is most strongly\nrooted in published conceptualizations (excepting the economic\ndimension, which dominates for obvious reasons) in trying to\neliminate informality through the exercise of the rule of law\nand regulatory frameworks, including the occasional promotion\nof institutional violence. These results are consistent with both\nthe number of connections that are established between these\ncategories and their individual inputs. Perhaps most surprising\nis the relative lack of importance attached to the social and\nlabor dimensions, which highlights the contradiction of an\nacademic community that represents the civilized world yet\nappears to be closer to the abyss than to the solution.\n\nData availability\nUnderlying data\n\nFigshare: Analysis of the concept of informal economy through\n102 definitions: legality or necessity.
