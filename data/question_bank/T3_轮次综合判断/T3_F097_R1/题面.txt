We describe how ARIs are produced, and how they are used to\nsupport this aim.\nAims and objectives: In this paper we offer a description of ARIs and\ntheir development by UK governmental departments, and an\nassessment of how different stakeholders, including academia and\nfunders, have responded to or otherwise used the ARIs.\nKey conclusions: ARIs are a mechanism for organisations to share\ntheir research interests with external audiences in the form of a\npublished document. In addition to this primary aim, they also have a\nmuch broader set of uses, including connecting departments with\neach other and helping intermediaries shape engagement plans. All\ngroups would benefit from more robust evidence to choose effective\nengagement mechanisms, and more can be done to make the ARIs\ndiscoverable and useable. Overall, the ARIs are a useful tool to\nilluminate, and begin to connect different parts of the research-policy\nsystem.\n\n\nIntroduction: what are areas of research interest?\nAlthough governments, funders and researchers often share a desire for more evidence to support social change, there\nare significant organisational and procedural barriers in the way. One often-mentioned barrier to evidence use in policy\nis a lack of policy relevant research (Armstrong et al., 2006; Oliver et al., 2014; Cairney, 2016; Wye et al., 2019).\nOne way of addressing this barrier is to make it easier for researchers to know about policymakers’ research priorities.\nIn the UK, government departments identify priority research gaps called Areas of Research Interest (ARIs). The Nurse\nReview into the UK Research Councils (Nurse, 2015) identified a strategic need across government departments to\n“maintain[] ‘statements of need’, in terms of the most important research questions confronting the Departments”\n(p.3, Nurse, 2015). By enabling other government departments, funders, and researchers, ARIs were intended to enable\npolicymakers to access relevant evidence and expertise more easily. A similar initiative has been proposed in the US,\ncalled the Government Learning Agendas1, where all departments and arms-length bodies are required by the Evidence\nAct (2019) to publish their knowledge needs.\nMany policy and practice organisations including government departments of course communicated their evidence\nneeds before these initiatives (e.g. in Defra’s evidence plan from 2006). However, the ARIs are the first time that every\ndepartment has been required to publish these publicly, update them annually, and invest resources into their production\nand use; in effect, 'systematising and embedding the articulation of research interests as part of the fabric of policy\nmaking.\nThe role of our team\nIn the UK, the Government Office for Science (GOS) supports the Government Chief Scientific Adviser (GCSA), and\nholds a cross-government remit to support science capability across departments. It relies on 'soft power' networking and\ninfluencing rather than mandating. It also holds the policy for Areas of Research Interest. As part of this remit, Giulia\nCuccato led a team of civil servants in the Government Office for Science (GOS) to develop guidance for departments\nin developing their ARIs, and supporting and tracking their ARI-related activities. Alongside this work, Kathryn Oliver\nand Annette Boaz have been embedded in GOS to explore and support better production of ARIs, and more effective\nengagement with them by funders, researchers and intermediaries. We have been involved in supporting the development\nof ARIs across the UK government, in helping departments use ARIs to access relevant evidence and expertise, and in\nresearching how ARIs could be optimised to support the research-policy system.\nOur reflections here are based on our work since 2018 (GC) and 2019 (KO and AB) which has involved working\nclosely with analyst and policy teams in departments across government, and with intermediaries such as the University\nPolicy Engagement Network (UPEN), the National Academies, the What Works Network, and individual universities\nand researchers, in a practical knowledge mobilisation programme. We draw on these experiences to discuss the strengths\nand weaknesses of ARIs, and our recommendations to improve their effectiveness as a systems-level intervention.\nPolicy outcomes and implications\nThe ARIs have great potential to enable funders, government, research organisations, researchers, and intermediaries to\nwork together in a more effective way. We have observed that merely producing ARIs is not a sufficient intervention;\ninstead, it requires skilled mobilisation work by people within all these organisations to be able to optimise their\nproduction and use. Below, we describe how ARIs are produced, what ARI-related engagement occurs, and how they\nfunction as a systems level intervention.\nEmbedding production of ARIs across government\nWhen GCSA Patrick Vallance joined GOS in 2018, one of his main goals was to review and increase the scientific\ncapability within government (Government Office for Science, 2019). This review published recommendations, one of\nwhich set out the requirements for annually-updated ARIs to be published by each department. There was considerable\nvariation between government departments. Some departments had existing strong science systems and plans which\nincluded an articulation of their research needs, and others had never attempted to map or describe their internal science\nsystems before.\nThe team with GOS (GC, plus a full-time intern and resources from HEO and SEO level staff) worked with departments\nto identify the maturity of their science systems, establish their history of developing science plans or equivalents, the\nconnectedness of their science and analysis teams with the policy function within each department, and their consequent\nARI support needs. The ARI Fellows (KO and AB) worked with departments offering tailored advice about how to\nproduce and publish ARIs, and how to engage effectively with academics during and after the production process.\n1\n\nwww.evaluation.gov/evidence-plans/learning-agenda/\n\n\nAs of summer 2022, all departments and arms-length bodies have published at least one version of their ARIs, with at least\nten updated in the last two years. Over 1500 ARIs have been published which vary greatly in terms of content, production\nand associated activities (Haddon and Sasse, 2018).\nHow do departments produce ARIs?\nFor most departments, the production of ARIs was a primarily internal process. For example, the Departmental Chief\nScientist might task the science and analysis team within a department with drafting a set of ARIs. Some departments\nconnected the development of ARIs with their departmental science strategy which set out projected R&D spending\nfor the financial year, and with policy priorities for the department. Some departments engaged with external experts\nto frame, prioritise and shape their ARIs, for example through their Science Advisory Councils. Others took a highly\nproactive approach. For instance, Defra held a two-day conference in 2018 with representatives from industry, research\nand practice to develop their initial ARIs. Once drafted, ARIs are signed off by the relevant directors, the minister, and\nthen published on the UK government website: gov.uk2.\nWhat are ARIs for?\nARIs were intended to identify strategic research priorities for departments, but in practice we have found that they\nhave a much broader set of uses. We found that departments use them to improve internal working and relationships,\nto implement the agenda of the Chief Scientist, to support other governmental processes such as spending reviews,\nthe Integrated Review, and the Science Capability review. For some they are a reflection of their policy priorities;\nfor others an articulation of the activities and structures of their internal science system; a statement of likely research\ncommissioning priorities; and/or a statement of research areas around which they would welcome collaboration or input.\nHowever, the end products appeared to be mostly appropriate for the departments in question. By and large, they were\nseen as useful internal tools to negotiate and communicate with policy colleagues around budgets and priorities, and\nuseful external tools to solicit help.\nUniversities and academics find them useful to plan engagement activities such as workshops and fellowships, but often\ntend to view them as poorly-written research questions. ARIs can help the research community to understand what\ngovernment departments want from them. This happens most effectively when there are opportunities for dialogue or a\nclear narrative about the policy history behind each ARI.\nAddressing ARIs: what engagement methods are best?\nIdentifying relevant expertise and research is a real challenge for government departments, particularly where resources\nare limited. Framing problems is an important step for departments, because it dictates what research and which\nexperts are considered relevant and appropriate. We found that officials in government departments were committed\nto addressing the challenge of diversity and inclusion in academic-policy engagement, but we unsure how best to go\nabout improving practice in this area. Departments took different approaches to identifying relevant experts and evidence,\nranging from using existing formal structures such as Scientific Advisory Committees and Chief Scientific Advisors,\nto effectively outsourcing the selection of candidate experts to partners such as the UK Universities Policy Engagement\nNetwork. We also found that universities and researchers often reached out directly to departmental officials. While wellintentioned, these approaches often created very significant amount of engagement work for officials on top of their\nusual engagement activities such as advisory councils. We observed that this had the potential to create competition,\nduplication and wasted resource on all sides, which is consistent with the literature on academic-policy engagement\n(Oliver et al., 2022).\nDepartments had no clear process to identify which engagement mechanism was the most appropriate for each ARI or set\nof ARIs. Using a survey of the officials most frequently engaged with ARI-related work, we found over 22 engagement\nmechanisms were used by departments (see Table 1). Most departments tend to rely on tried and tested approaches (such\nas commissioning research projects).\nWe observed that there was scope to broaden the menu of options available to departments when planning and\nimplementing their ARI-related engagement. For example, officials reported primarily using ARIs to commission\nnew research projects or support the generation of new research in other ways. In these cases, evidence syntheses or interactive\nroundtables might be more appropriate and useful methods for gathering relevant research. They offer funders,\nintermediaries and researchers’ insights into what departmental research agendas. Universities and intermediaries in\nparticular have used ARIs to develop their own strategic engagement plans (see, e.g. Heckels, 2020). For some departments, this is due to political or operational sensitivity, and for others, they prefer to only publish\nARIs on topics where they are currently seeking external input. For example, they may be\nmore useful at capturing topics which could inform policy over the medium to long-term than for short term problems.\nShared problem-framing and priority-setting: ARIs offer an opportunity for government to think and articulate its\nstrategic aims. This model has been widely criticised as being based on some fundamentally flawed assumptions about how\ndecision-making works (Jones and Crow, 2017) and on how evidence informs that process (Locke, 2002). We have seen the impact of ARIs in:\n• Improved understanding of what resources are required, and where, to optimally support ARI work and R&D\nacross government\n• Identifying opportunities to connect academics with government officials, e.g. from Wales Centre for Public\nPolicy with colleagues in DHSC and DWP; What Works Centre for Cities connecting with government officials\nacross departments\n• Influencing new Strategic Priority Fund bids (e.g. NERC SPF programme shaped by the Land Use group to\nbroaden beyond biodiversity to include more social and urban research)\n• Enabling discussion between funders and government, e.g. STFC with GOS work on security and resilient;\nDWP with ESRC grant managers; Innovate UK with GOS colleagues to work on cross-government and UKRI\nresponses to the spending review\n• Influencing future funding programmes which follow the themes of our knowledge mobilisation events\n(e.g. Just transition/vulnerable communities by the British Academy) or drew on our knowledge of ARI\nmobilisation to inform design (ESRC Policy Fellowship scheme)\nThe true value of ARIs may be in illuminating the ways in which the research-policy system is connected, and how we can\nintervene most effectively to support this system.\nWeaknesses of the ARIs: systems challenges\nNot everything can be or is articulated as an ARI: ARIs are not able to articulate the totality of departmental research\nneeds. For some departments, this is due to political or operational sensitivity, and for others, they prefer to only publish\nARIs on topics where they are currently seeking external input. It would be a mistake, therefore, to think of ARIs as a\ncomplete and exhaustive list of the topics on which government is seeking input.\nDepartmental ARI documents may, or may not signal the most important research topics relevant to that departmental\nportfolio. It is not reasonable to expect the ARIs to act as a magic bullet, stimulating the generation or mobilisation of\nevidence relevant to the most important policy needs. ARIs should therefore be seen as a starting point of a discussion,\nwhich can help to build partnerships within which candid discussions about evidence needs can be had.\n\n\n\nARIs are not research questions: Academics frequently describe ARIs as poorly written research questions. An\nalternative, more useful phrase might be “research needs”, which helps to give the impression that there is a process\nattached to them, that they are valued, and broader than research questions. They are more usefully thought of as topics for\nconversation.\nARIs are difficult to access and analyse: By 2018, most departments had published at least one version of their ARIs,\nwhich then sat on the government website in pdf or html formats. Through the fellowships, the ARI team worked with\nOverton to design, test and launch a searchable database of all ARIs5 by topic, department or year, in 2023. This greatly\nimproved the ability of researchers to identify relevant topics or potential collaborators, and meant departments are easily\nable to identify shared cross departmental areas of interest.\nFinding relevant evidence and expertise takes time and work: While some departments had resources dedicated to\nengagement around the ARIs, others did not. While relevant research often exists (as bodies of primary research, in\nresearch and practice communities, or in ongoing funding investments), this knowledge is often inaccessible, being\nbehind paywalls or requiring time and skill to find and absorb.\nActionable recommendations\nAreas of Research Interest are a helpful intervention, as a cog in the system which may help us make and use evidence\nmore effectively to inform policy. They are a positive development, without being a silver bullet solution to the knotty\nproblem of evidence production and use.\nIt is likely that having two knowledge mobilisation experts embedded in GOS to support the production and use of ARIs\nenabled the ARIs to maximise their potential as a systems intervention. By having embodied resource available within\nGOS to support ARI-related work, the team was able to move beyond transactional, linear approaches to knowledge\nexchange and instead build long-term, meaningful partnerships with teams across government, funders and within the\nresearch system. These relationships were critical to establishing feasible approaches to deliver policy-relevant evidence\nmore successfully.\nHowever, even without this ongoing support, much could be done to optimise their production and use.\nFunders need to support knowledge exchange and evidence synthesis as well as primary research relevant to these areas.\nAt present, funders are often not able to easily access their portfolios of existing investments, which means it is difficult\nfor them to help departments access ARI-relevant existing research or relevant experts. Funders do not appear to be able to\ntrack use of ARIs in proposals, and it is not clear how they shape future investments. Finally, funders do not appear to be\nusing ARIs as a basis to identify shared interests, and where they do fund knowledge exchange activities, they do so in an\nuncoordinated way which does not usually seek to respond to government priorities such as ARIs or missions. Hardly any\nfunders invest in research about evidence production and use, which would produce empirical evidence to guide these\nactivities.\nIntermediaries: Intermediaries (such as the National Academies, selected What Works Centres such as the Wales Centre\nfor Public Policy) often do have both skilled and resourced individuals who work across academic-policy boundaries.\nFurther investment in brokers and intermediaries would be an effective way to strengthen research-policy engagement.\nWhere there is significant staff turnover in government (as civil servants move roles). Intermediaries can become the\nrepository for organisational memory and could advise departments on their own policy and research engagement history.\nHowever, this work is often essentially unpaid, and there is no easy way for the skills and careers of knowledge mobilisers\nin these organisations to be nurtured. Intermediaries occupy an important place in the academic policy landscape,\nenabling them to convene and facilitate cross-field and interdisciplinary discussions. We found that Intermediaries were\nreally valued, able to access experts, bring in diverse voices and knowledge types. However, the brokering organisations\nwe saw (National Academies, What Works Networks, UPEN) did not represent all academics and communities.\nResearchers and universities need to incentivise effective policy engagement. Individual researchers often lack structures\nto help them understand policy need, so despite the existence of the ARIs, they struggle to address policy agendas.\nResearchers are not always well-trained or incentivised to engage well, and where they do, they tend to actively push\nexperts and expertise in a linear fashion, rather than thinking about the overall science system. This leads to competition\nbetween them for officials’ time, which is inefficient and drives inequity and a lack of diversity in the science advisory\n\n5\n\nwww.ari.org.uk\n\n\nsystem. Training is often about increasing volume and impact of individual researchers or projects. We observed that\nalmost all the university-led projects and engagement activities sought to push university agendas (e.g. institutional\nprofile) or researcher agendas (e.g. individual careers, or importance of particular pieces of evidence). This is not helpful\nfor policy colleagues, who then have to analyse where engagement is most useful and least costly. There is also a growing\ninfrastructure to support knowledge mobilisation within universities, and interestingly across them too. The University\nPolicy Engagement Network is an opportunity to collaborate on and share knowledge exchange training, best practice,\nand a chance to offer government a more diverse set of experts to draw on.\nGovernment departments: need to be clear what is being asked and offered for each ARI. Their current resources\nand histories of engagement shape their ARI planning, but a broader range of engagement mechanisms would make\nacademic-policy engagement more efficient and effective. Departments may benefit from more support to develop and\nimplement engagement plans effectively, minimising duplication and maximising value for money. There is also a need\nto track the use of ARIs to understand how they are used and demonstrate to departmental colleagues the value of\ninvesting time in ARI development and academic engagement.\nAll groups would benefit from a more robust evidence base about how to engage most effectively There is considerable\nscope to improve the process of selecting appropriate next steps, and to expand the range of research-policy engagement\nmechanisms used. In general, seeking to complement existing structures and processes is most efficient and leads to least\ndisruption.\nMaking ARs discoverable: As published ARIs documents from each government department ARIs were be hard to search\nand access (especially for cross departmental issues). Making the ARIs accessible has been possible through the new ARI\ndatabase, which has enabled researchers and funders to identify trends, themes and topics to inform their future and\npresent work. Government officials would be able to discover areas of shared interest with other departmental colleagues\nand work on shared priorities together.\nFocusing knowledge mobilisation activities on ARIs: There is an ever-increasing amount of knowledge mobilisation\nactivity in the UK, much generated from within the UK higher education sector. It would be helpful for government, and\ncertainly would reduce duplication and cost, if these activities were coordinated and focused around the ARIs. A really\nuseful offer from the higher education sector would be a to bring together experts and expertise relevant to ARIs across\nuniversities, synthesising evidence, gaps, and key messages for discussion with policymakers on key themes; themes now\neasily identifiable via analysis of the ARI database. This activity would beed to be resourced, and collaboration and\nleadership would need to be incentivised by funders.\nUnderstanding the impact of ARIs: The impacts we identified from our ARI-related knowledge mobilisation are not a\ncomprehensive overview of how they are shaping research, engagement and investment activities. It would be useful to\nhave a more exhaustive exploration of who is using ARIs, how, and for what purpose. However, better data about the\nways in which ARIs stimulate activity in parts of the research system (including research production, publication,\nengagement activities, and funding) would help us to understand where their value lies.\nAs we note above, it is unlikely that ARIs will ever be able to capture all governmental research needs or engagement\nactivities. For example, they may be more useful at capturing topics which could inform policy over the medium to longterm than for short term problems. It is important to understand more about what can and cannot be captured via ARIs in\norder to inform more effective academic-policy engagement around priorities articulated through ARIs and those which\nare not.\nShared problem-framing and priority-setting: ARIs offer an opportunity for government to think and articulate its\nstrategic aims. To operationalise these aims takes organisational capability which needs to be built over time, both within\ngovernment and in its external partners. This might be achieved by a cross-government initiative or department taking\nresponsibility for identifying areas of shared interest, a process for prioritisation, and/or a place to optimise engagement\nbetween academia and government.\nConclusions\n• By stating their evidence needs, government departments make it easier for researchers to produce or provide\nrelevant research.\n• ARIs perform a range of useful functions helping to connect parts of the policy research system in\ndifferent ways.\n\n\n• Improving the accessibility of ARIs through a searchable database would increase their utility to all users.\n• Government, researchers, funders and intermediaries would benefit from a more robust evidence base about\nhow to engage most effectively to optimise the use of ARIs.\nThe ARIs are a useful step forward to a more research-informed policy culture. We have highlighted some of the work\nwhich is needed to make ARIs useful, and identified points within the system where intervention and support would be\nbeneficial. To make this happen, investment and resource will be required.
