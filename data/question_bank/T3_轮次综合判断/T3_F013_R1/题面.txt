The case studies were selected\nto showcase practical implementations of rights-based DRM\napproaches. Inclusive governance was effective in fighting\nCOVID-19, ensuring evidence-based policies15. The Building\nResilience through Inclusive and Climate-Adaptive DRR\n(BRDR) program in the Asia-Pacific prioritizes gender equality and rights-based approaches in disaster risk reduction.\nTools like the Framework for Integrating Rights and Equality\n(FIRE) guide risk assessments and land-use planning, considering the needs of women, persons with disabilities, and other\nat-risk groups16. A consortium including the\nMozambican government, the World Bank, and NGOs like\nHumanity & Inclusion (HI) implemented an inclusive rebuild\nprogram in hard-hit communities, constructing new housing\nfor survivors with a focus on accessibility and dignity. HI teams\nconducted accessibility audits of infrastructure and provided\ninput to adapt schools, clinics, and homes to meet universal\ndesign standards. The project also trained 200+ community\nmembers and humanitarian staff on inclusive practices, reinforcing the rights to adequate housing and equality for vulnerable\nsurvivors17.\nThese case studies demonstrate that applying human rights\nprinciples in disaster contexts is feasible and beneficial.\n\nApproaches that uphold dignity, non-discrimination, and participation through inclusive planning, targeted protections for\nthe vulnerable, and community engagement have resulted in\nmore just and effective outcomes.\n\n5.2 Key challenges in DSS integration and\nimplementation\nDespite significant advancements, several challenges persist in\nthe development and implementation of DSS for multi-hazard\nDRM. One key issue is data interoperability, where disparate\ndatasets from different agencies and sources often lack standardization. Ensuring seamless integration of diverse data\nstreams remains an ongoing research priority. Another limitation\nis the computational cost of high-resolution simulations, which\ncan limit real-time applicability in resource-constrained settings. DSS frameworks need to evolve to incorporate climate\nadaptation strategies dynamically, given the increasing frequency and intensity of climate-induced disasters. Future\nresearch must explore the use of hybrid AI models that combine deep learning with expert-driven rule-based systems to\nenhance predictive reliability and decision support.\nData integration and interoperability issues are among the\nmost cited barriers in implementing DSS for multi-hazard\nmanagement. A multi-hazard DSS must pull together information from numerous sources, such as meteorological data,\nseismic sensors, social datasets, and infrastructure networks,\nwhich often exist in incompatible formats and siloed systems.\nStudies highlight that many failures in disaster coordination\nstem from different agencies and systems being unable to\nseamlessly share data and models. Without interoperability,\ncrucial information can fall through the cracks, undermining\nthe very purpose of a DSS. Data quality and reliability present\nconcerns, as incoming streams may be incomplete, outdated,\nor incorrect, especially in chaotic disaster conditions. DSS\ndevelopers must grapple with uncertainties and ensure the system can function with imperfect data. There is also the risk\nof information overload: a comprehensive DSS might flood\ndecision-makers with more data and model outputs than they\ncan interpret under time pressure. Machine learning can filter\nout “unrelated data” and flag the truly relevant signals,\nmitigating cognitive overload during crises18.\nAnother set of challenges lies in the ethical and social domain.\nAs AI-driven and data-intensive DSS become commonplace,\nthey raise questions about bias, transparency, and accountability. Algorithms trained on historical data may inadvertently perpetuate biases, such as underestimating risks to\nunder-served communities if those areas were historically\nunder-reported. The often “black box” nature of advanced\nAI models can make it unclear how a DSS arrived at a given\nsuggestion, creating a transparency problem19.\nPractical implementation barriers can impede DSS deployment\neven when technical issues are solved. Resource constraints\nare a common hurdle, as developing and maintaining a sophisticated DSS requires funding, skilled personnel, and infrastructure. Interdisciplinary and inter-agency coordination is another\n\n\npractical challenge, as a multi-hazard DSS often requires collaboration between meteorologists, engineers, planners, first\nresponders, government officials, and communities. Developers must address human-factor issues like interface design,\ntraining programs, and change management to make DSS\nwork in practice.\n\n5.3 Emerging trends and innovations in DRM Decision\nSupport Systems\nTechnological innovations, such as artificial intelligence\n(AI) and blockchain, have significantly accelerated the capabilities of DSS for DRM, making them indispensable for\nreal-time data processing, risk modeling, and scenario-based\ndecision-making. However, the efficacy of these tools depends\non their ethical and legal foundations, such as data privacy, algorithmic fairness, and stakeholder inclusion. An\n“ethics-by-design” approach to DRM involves embedding\nethical considerations into the system development lifecycle,\nincorporating safeguards, and addressing legal requirements\nlike GDPR. This approach maintains public trust and ensures\ntechnology-driven disaster management does not compromise\nhuman rights.\nAI is being harnessed to improve risk assessment and early\nwarning, with AI-assisted emergency response plans being\ndeveloped by UN-led initiatives. AI is being used to quickly\nanalyze multi-hazard data while maintaining transparency\nand bias mitigation. Pilot applications include predictive analytics for flood forecasting, computer vision for damage\nassessment via satellite imagery, and optimization algorithms\nfor relief logistics. These tools can greatly improve the speed\nand accuracy of decisions, but human oversight and fairness\ncriteria must guide their deployment20.\nBlockchain technology is being used to enhance trust and data\nintegrity in DRM. It provides a decentralized, tamper-proof\nledger for critical data like resource inventories, aid distribution, and identity records. Studies during the COVID-19 era\nsuggest that blockchain can build trust, transparency, and\naccountability in complex disaster operations. Projects have\nused blockchain to track aid funds and maintain secure medical\ndata exchanges during disasters21,22.\nGeospatial technology and open data are enabling more\ninclusive, bottom-up risk mapping through tools like\nOpenStreetMap, mobile data collection apps, and participatory GIS platforms. These tools allow local communities to\ncontribute directly to mapping their environment and hazards,\nimproving data availability for early warning systems and capturing community knowledge in risk models. This approach\nfosters inclusivity, transparency, and public engagement, leading\nto more effective local risk reduction measures.\nComprehensive DRM platforms that integrate multiple hazard\nmodels, big data analytics, and user-friendly interfaces for\ndecision-makers are emerging trends that promote transparency\nand inclusive governance. As these innovations mature, they\nare likely to become integral to how we approach multi-hazard\nrisk management in an inclusive manner.\n\n6. Data processing agreements\nwould need to be in place among the project consortium to\ncover any exchange of personal data. Transparency is still\nkey for trust, as the public should know that any personal\ninformation gathered for DRM is handled lawfully and not\nretained indefinitely or misused.\n\n4.2 Security, resilience and free movement\nThe security of the Disaster Risk Management System (DSS)\nand its data is crucial for safeguarding citizens’ rights and\nfreedoms. Unreliable or insecure systems can lead to false\nalarms, endangering lives, and undermining the right to security.\nThe EU’s Network and Information Systems (NIS) Directive\nemphasizes that disruptions in network/information systems\ncan have societal effects and even impede the movement of\npeople across borders. In a disaster context, an outage of a\nDRM DSS in one country might affect neighboring countries,\nleaving others blind to a creeping threat. Therefore, ensuring\n\nhigh availability, integrity, and cybersecurity of the MEDiate\nDSS is not only a technical requirement but a fundamental part\nof safeguarding the community’s rights.\nThe NIS Directive and its successor (NIS2) encourage Member States to classify such systems and apply rigorous security\nmeasures. Even if the MEDiate DSS is not formally an “essential service” yet, its functions are part of critical infrastructure. Following NIS guidance, such as risk assessment, incident\nreporting, and cooperation with national Computer Security\nIncident Response Teams (CSIRTs), would be prudent. This\nincludes strong encryption of data in transit and at rest, authentication protocols for users, firewalls and intrusion detection\non the system’s servers, and failover plans. Organizationally,\nregular security audits and drills can ensure the system’s\nresilience.\nA robust and secure DSS indirectly supports the free movement of persons, another fundamental value in the EU. A\nwell-informed response, aided by a DSS, can minimize unnecessary travel restrictions and ensure that any limitations on\nmovement are proportionate and brief. By including mobility\nand transport data, the DSS can help target warnings to travelers, which is important for free movement and tourism. From\na rights perspective, any measure that the DSS recommends\nwhich could limit movement should be based on solid data\nand risk analysis to ensure it’s truly necessary. The MEDiate\nDSS, by providing evidence-based risk assessments, can\ninform such decisions, balancing individual freedoms with\ncollective safety.\n\n4.3 Safeguards and ethical oversight\nImplementing a Disaster Risk Management System (DSS) in\nline with fundamental rights involves not only software but also\norganizational safeguards and ethical oversight. EU-funded\nprojects like MEDiate are required to address ethics and\ndata protection from the start, including dedicated work on\nlegal/ethical compliance, appointing an ethics advisor or board,\nand delivering on requirements such as an Ethics Deliverable or Data Management Plan. In MEDiate, these instruments ensure that data subjects’ rights and freedoms are central\nconsiderations in system design.\nStakeholder engagement and inclusive governance are crucial\nethical requirements for DRM tools. Engaging diverse stakeholders in planning ensures disaster policies reflect collective\nvalues and avoid discrimination. Participatory mechanisms,\nsuch as consulting local officials and NGOs, uphold procedural\nfairness and yield more culturally appropriate outcomes13.\nMEDiate’s participatory co-design approach helps mitigate this\nrisk by incorporating diverse stakeholder input, while ethical\noversight guards against any infringement of the principle of\nequal dignity.\nOn the data side, organizational measures such as training staff\nin data protection, having a clear process for individuals to\nquery or correct their data, and appointing a Data Protection\nOfficer for the authority using the DSS are important. Each\n\n\ntestbed authority should integrate the DSS into their existing\nGDPR compliance structure, updating their public privacy\nnotices to mention the DSS and its purposes.\nConsent and community engagement are another safeguard\narea. If the DSS includes optional public-facing components,\nobtaining informed consent and giving users control is key.\nThe Sendai Framework emphasizes people-centered approaches,\nempowering citizens with information while respecting their\nprivacy choices. As the MEDiate DSS is designed to work\nin multiple countries, interoperability of legal norms is essential. International guidelines for disaster data sharing that\ninclude harmonized privacy and ethics rules help facilitate the\nMEDiate consortium’s work and ensure that fundamental\nrights travel with the data.\n\n4.4 Promoting trust and future policy directions\nThe MEDiate DSS, a disaster risk management system, is based\non the fundamental rights principle of trust. Citizens need to\ntrust that technologies used to protect them will respect their\nrights, which can lead to increased cooperation with authorities during emergencies. Trust in the system can be built\nthrough public communication about its capabilities and its\nresponsibility in handling personal data. Local authorities can\ninform residents about the system’s capabilities and clarify that\nit does not surveil or collect personal details. This can increase\nthe community’s sense of safety and reduce anxiety.\nMEDiate’s approach of democratizing access to scenario-based\ntools and bridging knowledge gaps is aimed at empowering\ncommunities, which is linked to fundamental rights such as\nthe right to information and participation in societal efforts to\nreduce risk. By involving community representatives in scenario planning, the project acknowledges citizens as stakeholders and enhances legitimacy and compliance with disaster\nmeasures.\nThe successful integration of fundamental rights into a DRM\nDSS can influence broader legislative or policy developments.\nOne potential evolution is the formal recognition of “data\nfor disaster management” as a category warranting special attention. Policymakers might consider creating explicit legal provisions that clarify how personal data can be used in disasters,\nstreamlining GDPR’s existing clauses into a more straightforward emergency data-sharing protocol. Standardizing ethical requirements for AI or decision-support in the public sector\nis another area that could benefit from MEDiate’s work.\nFrom a free movement standpoint, more formalized cross-border\ncooperation agreements may be seen, allowing the flow of not\njust data but also people during emergencies. The EU Civil\nProtection Mechanism14 already facilitates this, but fundamental rights considerations like handling personal data of\nforeign citizens or respecting the rights of evacuees crossing\ninto another country might drive further refinement. A future\nEU guideline could detail how to manage transboundary evacuation operations, ensuring individuals maintain their rights\nwhile moving for safety.\n\n5. Further case studies, challenges, and emerging\ntrends\n\nReal-world cases since 2019 demonstrate how DRM frameworks can be implemented in ways that uphold human rights\nand social equity. Section 5.1 presents a few illustrative examples spanning hazard types, climate-related disasters, pandemics, and humanitarian crises. The case studies were selected\nto showcase practical implementations of rights-based DRM\napproaches. Meanwhile, Section 5.2 touches on key challenges\nof implementing DSS for DRM, and Section 5.3 describes\nhow DRM is rapidly evolving with new technologies and\nmethodologies that aim to enhance decision support while\npromoting inclusivity and transparency.\n\n5.1 Case studies of rights-based DRM implementation\n(2019–Present)\nAcademic literature categorizes rights-based DRM mplementation approaches by the decision methodologies they employ.\nFrameworks range from multi-criteria decision-making methods\nto mathematical optimization models and hybrid techniques.\nSome systems prioritize solutions by scoring alternatives\nagainst weighted criteria, such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or the Best-Worst Method, which has been applied\nin regions where stakeholder preferences and local knowledge must be factored explicitly into the prioritization of\ncommunities for evacuation or resource distribution.\nHazard contexts inspire different DSS methodologies. For\ninstance, the European Flood Awareness System (EFAS) emphasizes continent-wide early warning and scenario modeling\nsystems15. EFAS is relevant because it demonstrates effective cross-border collaboration essential for MEDiate’s transboundary data-sharing needs. In drought and food security\nmanagement, systems like the Famine Early Warning Systems\nNetwork (FEWS NET) have been established to aggregate climate data, crop forecasts, and socio-economic indicators into\ndecision support for aid agencies. FEWS NET’s integration of\nclimate forecasts and socio-economic indicators offers insights\nfor MEDiate’s adaptive learning algorithms.\nDSS for rights-based DRM help to process data and present\nactionable insights, reducing the time needed for critical decisions on resource deployment. Case studies like the 2017\nOroville Dam crisis in California and India’s post-disaster\nDSS for resource allocation have validated these benefits.\nMulti-hazard platforms like the Global Disaster Information\nSystem (GDACS) have proven instrumental in major crises,\nproviding rapid impact estimates, early warnings, anticipatory\naction, and streamlined international coordination.\nThe COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need to incorporate\nhuman rights into disaster management, with international\norganizations advising against betraying rights through public\nhealth measures. Inclusive governance was effective in fighting\nCOVID-19, ensuring evidence-based policies16. The Building\nResilience through Inclusive and Climate-Adaptive DRR\n(BRDR) program in the Asia-Pacific prioritizes gender equality and rights-based approaches in disaster risk reduction.\n\n\nTools like the Framework for Integrating Rights and Equality\n(FIRE), developed under the Building Resilience through Inclusive and Climate-Adaptative Disaster Risk Reduction (BRDR)\nprogramme, guide risk assessments and land-use planning by\nconsidering the needs of women, persons with disabilities,\nand other at-risk groups. FIRE comprises six dimensions:\nfundamental rights and equality, non-discrimination, participation and access to information, governance systems and\nstructures, social norms and context, and agency and empowerment. These were synthesized from international law and\noperational guidance such as the Sphere Handbook17 and IASC\nGuidelines18. Applying FIRE in MEDiate would help align\nDSS design with international best practices for equitable\ndisaster response ([external resource identifier omitted] Communities are\nengaged in identifying hazards and shaping emergency\npreparedness plans, ensuring their voices guide the process.\n\nseismic sensors, social datasets, and infrastructure networks,\nwhich often exist in incompatible formats and siloed systems.\nStudies highlight that many failures in disaster coordination\nstem from different agencies and systems being unable to\nseamlessly share data and models. Without interoperability,\ncrucial information can fall through the cracks, undermining\nthe very purpose of a DSS. Data quality and reliability present\nconcerns, as incoming streams may be incomplete, outdated,\nor incorrect, especially in chaotic disaster conditions. DSS\ndevelopers must grapple with uncertainties and ensure the system can function with imperfect data. There is also the risk\nof information overload: a comprehensive DSS might flood\ndecision-makers with more data and model outputs than they\ncan interpret under time pressure. Machine learning can filter\nout “unrelated data” and flag the truly relevant signals,\nmitigating cognitive overload during crises21.\n\nThe Cyclone Idai recovery in Mozambique (2019–2024)\nfocused on human rights and inclusion, particularly for\ndisplaced and persons with disabilities. A consortium including\nthe Mozambican government, the World Bank, and NGOs like\nHumanity & Inclusion (HI) implemented an inclusive rebuild\nprogram in hard-hit communities, constructing new housing\nfor survivors with a focus on accessibility and dignity. HI teams\nconducted accessibility audits of infrastructure and provided\ninput to adapt schools, clinics, and homes to meet universal\ndesign standards. The project also trained 200+ community\nmembers and humanitarian staff on inclusive practices,\nreinforcing the rights to adequate housing and equality for\nsurvivors in situations of vulnerability20.\n\nAnother set of challenges lies in the ethical and social domain.\nAs AI-driven and data-intensive DSS become commonplace,\nthey raise questions about bias, transparency, and accountability. Algorithms trained on historical data may inadvertently perpetuate biases, such as underestimating risks to\nunder-served communities if those areas were historically\nunder-reported. The often “black box” nature of advanced\nAI models can make it unclear how a DSS arrived at a given\nsuggestion, creating a transparency problem22.\n\nThese case studies demonstrate that applying human rights\nprinciples in disaster contexts is feasible and beneficial.\nApproaches that uphold dignity, non-discrimination, and participation through inclusive planning, targeted protections for\npeople in situations of vulnerability, and community engagement\nhave resulted in more just and effective outcomes.\n\nPractical implementation barriers can impede DSS deployment\neven when technical issues are solved. Resource constraints\nare a common hurdle, as developing and maintaining a sophisticated DSS requires funding, skilled personnel, and infrastructure. Interdisciplinary and inter-agency coordination is another\npractical challenge, as a multi-hazard DSS often requires collaboration between meteorologists, engineers, planners, first\nresponders, government officials, and communities. Developers\nmust address human-factor issues like interface design, training\nprograms, and change management to make DSS work in\npractice.\n\n5.2 Key challenges in DSS integration and\nimplementation\nDespite significant advancements, several challenges persist in\nthe development and implementation of DSS for multi-hazard\nDRM. One key issue is data interoperability, where disparate\ndatasets from different agencies and sources often lack standardization. Ensuring seamless integration of diverse data\nstreams remains an ongoing research priority. Another limitation\nis the computational cost of high-resolution simulations, which\ncan limit real-time applicability in resource-constrained settings. DSS frameworks need to evolve to incorporate climate\nadaptation strategies dynamically, given the increasing frequency and intensity of climate-induced disasters. Future\nresearch must explore the use of hybrid AI models that combine deep learning with expert-driven rule-based systems to\nenhance predictive reliability and decision support.\n\n5.3 Emerging trends and innovations in DRM Decision\nSupport Systems\nTechnological innovations, such as artificial intelligence\n(AI) and blockchain, have significantly accelerated the capabilities of DSS for DRM, making them indispensable for\nreal-time data processing, risk modeling, and scenario-based\ndecision-making. However, the efficacy of these tools depends\non their ethical and legal foundations, such as data privacy, algorithmic fairness, and stakeholder inclusion. An\n“ethics-by-design” approach to DRM involves embedding\nethical considerations into the system development lifecycle,\nincorporating safeguards, and addressing legal requirements\nlike GDPR. This approach maintains public trust and ensures\ntechnology-driven disaster management does not compromise\nhuman rights.\n\nData integration and interoperability issues are among the\nmost cited barriers in implementing DSS for multi-hazard\nmanagement. A multi-hazard DSS must pull together information from numerous sources, such as meteorological data,\n\nAI is being harnessed to improve risk assessment and early\nwarning, with AI-assisted emergency response plans being\ndeveloped by UN-led initiatives. AI is being used to quickly\nanalyze multi-hazard data while maintaining transparency\n\n\nand bias mitigation. Pilot applications include predictive analytics for flood forecasting, computer vision for damage\nassessment via satellite imagery, and optimization algorithms\nfor relief logistics. These tools can greatly improve the speed\nand accuracy of decisions, but human oversight and fairness\ncriteria must guide their deployment23.\nBlockchain technology is being used to enhance trust and data\nintegrity in DRM. It provides a decentralized, tamper-proof\nledger for critical data like resource inventories, aid distribution, and identity records. Studies during the COVID-19 era\nsuggest that blockchain can build trust, transparency, and\naccountability in complex disaster operations. Projects have\nused blockchain to track aid funds and maintain secure medical\ndata exchanges during disasters24,25.\nGeospatial technology and open data are enabling more\ninclusive, bottom-up risk mapping through tools like\nOpenStreetMap, mobile data collection apps, and participatory GIS platforms. These tools allow local communities to\ncontribute directly to mapping their environment and hazards,\nimproving data availability for early warning systems and capturing community knowledge in risk models. This approach\nfosters inclusivity, transparency, and public engagement, leading\nto more effective local risk reduction measures.\nComprehensive DRM platforms that integrate multiple hazard\nmodels, big data analytics, and user-friendly interfaces for\ndecision-makers are emerging trends that promote transparency\nand inclusive governance. As these innovations mature, they\nare likely to become integral to how we approach multi-hazard\nrisk management in an inclusive manner.\n\n6. Recommendations and future directions\n\nTo truly be people-centered, MEDiate’s DSS should facilitate\nmeaningful stakeholder engagement not only in its development but also in its deployment. Building on the PAR approach\nalready used in testbeds, MEDiate can set up formal mechanisms for participatory governance. One approach is to\nestablish local stakeholder committees in each pilot region\n(including community representatives, NGOs, and civic leaders) who regularly interact with the DSS team. These committees could co-design scenario assumptions, validate data\ninputs, and discuss the DSS’s recommendations, ensuring local\nknowledge and values are incorporated. Establishing regular participatory workshops for scenario validation involving\nlocal stakeholders and emergency managers and organizing\nmandatory comprehensive training in GDPR compliance, data\nprotection practices, and ethical principles for all DSS users\nand administrators would be beneficial for technology adoption. As noted in disaster ethics research, involving community\nstakeholders leads to more fair, culturally appropriate decisions.\nFuture research extending beyond the scope of MEDiate\nshould focus on implementing standardized data-sharing protocols and interoperability guidelines across DRM platforms.\nAdditionally, ethical considerations, such as bias in predictive\nmodels and privacy concerns in data collection, require\nfurther investigation. Research could be focused on AI-driven\nDSS to improve computational efficiency, enhance real-time\n\ndata integration, and increase explainability of decision-making\nalgorithms.\nThe integration of blockchain technology for secure, decentralized data sharing is another promising avenue that could\naddress trust and data integrity challenges possibly leading to\ntamper-proof data exchanges and enhancing trust in DRM\npractices. AI for risk assessment tools may be useful but can\ninadvertently reproduce societal biases in training data, leading to unfair outcomes. This raises equity issues in disaster\nresponse. To address this, ethical DRM DSS require bias mitigation measures, such as auditing algorithms for biased patterns, using diverse datasets, and applying fairness metrics.\nOngoing monitoring and transparent validation are recommended to detect and correct any unfair impacts. This might\ninvolve algorithmic impact assessments or incorporating ethical\nAI frameworks that uphold principles of justice and respect\nfor human rights. Ensuring explainability of AI decisions is crucial for stakeholder understanding and challenge26. Moreover,\nexplainable AI (xAI) technology can generate synthetic data\nto enhance predictive models, aiding in disaster risk identification. However, ethical concerns arise regarding the accuracy\nof synthetic data in models and concerns about data privacy and\nsecurity. The use of synthetic data in predictive models may\nnot accurately reflect real-world conditions, and its application\nin disaster response raises questions about data security27.\nEnsuring long-term ethical adherence and privacy protection\ncould be accomplished with data anonymization techniques\nincluding aggregation and pseudonymization of sensitive\ninformation and algorithm transparency checks through regular fairness audits, impact assessments, and xAI integration.\nOrganizational mechanisms, such as appointing dedicated Data\nProtection Officers (DPOs) and Ethics Advisory Boards, overseeing compliance and ethical practices should be imperative\nfor widespread MEDiate DSS adoption.\nLooking ahead, the integration of broader fundamental rights—\nparticularly social and economic rights—will be a focus\nin refining the DSS. This includes embedding frameworks\nlike FIRE to assess both technical outputs and their ethical\nimplications. MEDiate will also explore participatory protocols\nto ensure that automated recommendations do not replace\ncommunity input, especially in decisions affecting relocation,\naccess to services, and prioritization of assistance. Ensuring\ntransparency in decision logic and human accountability remains\na guiding principle.\n\n7. Conclusion\n\nSustaining and enhancing MEDiate’s DSS is critical for\nlong-term disaster resilience and sustainability, as it ensures\nthe preservation of ethical and human rights principles, prevents discontinuity of research projects, and fosters institutional\nmemory and capacity building among local authorities, civil\nprotection agencies, and community stakeholders. This fosters\nfluency in MEDiate’s modeling techniques, ethical constraints,\nand scenario-planning capabilities, which can then be transferred to other regions or institutions. The MEDiate DSS\nis also versatile multi-hazard preparedness, as it adapts to\nnew threats through software updates, new data streams, and\nstakeholder feedback. This flexibility positions MEDiate as a\n\n\nliving system, constantly updated to address emergent risks\nlike pandemics, wildfires, or extreme weather events.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nEthical approval and consent were not required.\n\nDisclaimer\n\nThe views expressed in this article are those of the author.
