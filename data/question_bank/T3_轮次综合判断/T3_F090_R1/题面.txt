      "text": "Abstract\nBackground\nFailures to obtain a desired reward, such as losing money in\ngambling, can lead to frustration. In gambling, this frustration has\nbeen shown to take the form of faster responses after losses\ncompared with wins and non-gambling trials. In addition, reward\nomission or blockage can lead to more forceful responses. Yu and\ncolleagues (2014) showed that the proximity to a reward and the\neffort already expended to acquire the reward increased participants’\nresponse force and their retrospective self-reported frustration when\nthe reward was blocked.\nMethods\nIn this study, we attempted to replicate the findings of Yu and\ncolleagues (2014) using the same experimental procedure. In each\nschedule, participants (N = 32) needed to complete an arrow direction\ntask for varying numbers of times to win a reward but could be\nblocked at any stage. The response time (RT) and force of confirming\nthe outcomes were used as indicators of ‘frustration’. In addition, to\nobtain a more real-time and objective measure of (negative) emotion,\nwe measured facial electromyographic (EMG) activity over the\ncorrugator supercilii (frowning muscle) and the zygomaticus (smiling\nmuscle).\n\n\nO\n\nResults\nDue to technical problems, our data on response force were invalid. In\nline with the original study, both goal proximity and exerted effort\nincreased participants’ self-reported motivation in the task and\nfrustration after being blocked. An exploratory analysis showed that\nparticipants were slower in confirming an outcome when they were\nblocked closer to the reward, while exerted effort did not influence\nconfirm RT. These RT data were consistent with self-reported surprise\nratings, suggesting an orienting response. In the facial EMG data, we\nobserved no difference between wins and losses in activity over the\ncorrugator or the zygomaticus.\nConclusion\nTaken together, these data suggest that reward blockage does not\nnecessarily lead to behavioral or psychophysiological expressions of\nnegative emotions such as frustration.\nPlain language summary\nSometimes we get blocked from reaching our goal (e.g., getting a\nreward) and that can make us feel frustrated. This frustration may\nmanifest in faster and more forceful responses. For instance, people\nwho lose in gambling tend to start the next gamble faster than after\nwinning or not gambling. People also respond with more force when\nthey do not get the reward they expect. A study by Yu and colleagues\nfurther shows that if people are close to getting a reward and they\nhave already put in a lot of effort to get it, they feel more frustrated\nwhen that reward is blocked. Here, we conducted the same study, to\ntry to replicate these findings. Like in the original study, we found that\nwhen people were close to getting a reward and had already worked\nhard for it, they reported feeling more motivated in the task and more\nfrustrated when the reward was blocked. Interestingly, when they\nwere blocked closer to the reward, they took longer to react (opposite\nto the ‘frustration’ effect), but how much effort they had put in did not\naffect how fast they responded. This suggests instead that they were\nsurprised when they were blocked close to the reward. We also\nmeasured people's facial muscle activity as indicators of negative\nemotions like frustration. Surprisingly, there was no difference in the\nmuscle activity between when people won or lost. This suggests that\nnot getting a reward is not only frustrating, but also surprising, and\nthe frustration may not always show up in our behavior or facial\nexpressions.\n\n\nIntroduction\n\nHumans and non-human animals generally try to achieve desired\ngoals. Along the way, however, they may encounter obstacles\nand be prevented from achieving these goals: for example,\nin birds, a competitor may take a valuable piece of food\nbefore the individual has the opportunity to eat; or in humans,\nwe may not get the promotion that we think we deserve.\nTo understand goal-directed behaviour, it is important to\nunderstand how individuals respond to such failures to achieve\ndesired goals.\nBoth examples above may lead to ’frustration’: When an\nexpected reward is omitted, individuals sometimes respond with\nmore vigor (e.g., speed, force), which is called the ’frustration’\neffect. For example, Amsel (1958, 1992) observed that rats\nthat were typically rewarded in a first runway of a maze\nran through the second runway faster if the reward was\nnot given in the first runway. Furthermore, he observed\nmore aggressive behavior, such as urinating or biting the\nexperimenter, after reward omission. Similar effects have been\nobserved in other species, such as fish (Vindas et al., 2012),\nmice (de Almeida & Miczek, 2002), laying hens (Zimmerman\n& Koene, 1998) and pigeons (Zentall, 2011). Variations of this\neffect have been observed in humans as well. For example,\nVerbruggen et al. (2017) showed that participants tended to\ninitiate a new trial more quickly after losses in gambling tasks\ncompared to wins and non-gambles, which was interpreted\nas a manifestation of the ’frustration’ effect (for direct and\nconceptual replications of this finding, see Eben et al., 2020).\nSpeeding up after losses has recently also been observed\nin large behavioral tracking data from a real online gambling\nproduct (Chen et al., 2022).\nMany goal pursuit activities require individuals to carry out\nmultiple successive actions to obtain a reward. Accordingly,\nindividuals may fail at any stage of the goal pursuit process,\nwhich in turn may influence the magnitude of the ’frustration’\neffect triggered by reward omission or blockage. For instance,\nHaner and Brown (1955) found that children responded with\nmore force when a failure occurred close to success, compared\nto when they were still far from achieving their goal. As individuals make progress in their goal pursuit, their motivation\nto obtain the goal increases (i.e., the goal gradient hypothesis;\nHull, 1932), and the resulting response vigor (i.e., ’frustration’)\nalso increases when they are eventually blocked from the\nreward (Amsel, 1958). Outside the laboratory context, the\ninfluence of (real or perceived) goal progress on motivation\nhas also been documented. For instance, customers bought\ncoffee more frequently the closer they were to getting a free\ncoffee by collecting enough stamps in a reward program (Kivetz\net al., 2006).\nThe ubiquitous influence of goal progress on motivated\nbehavior requires a better understanding of how such influence\narises. Here we specifically focus on how goal progress\nmay influence the magnitude of the ’frustration’ effect when\nindividuals are blocked from a reward. Two closely interrelated\nfactors seem crucial in the effects of goal progress, namely\n\nexpended effort (Pompilio et al., 2006) and goal proximity\n(Shidara & Richmond, 2002). As individuals make progress\nin their goal pursuit, the effort already invested increases (i.e.,\nexpended effort), while they also get closer to the end reward\n(i.e., goal proximity). In many situations, these two factors are inherently confounded, but the psychological mechanisms underlying the effects of these two factors may differ. For\ninstance, expended effort is retrospective in nature as it concerns\nthe effort already invested, while goal proximity is prospective as it orients towards an upcoming reward (Yu et al., 2014).\nDisentangling the effects of expended effort and goal proximity\nwill therefore be informative in understanding successive goaldirected behaviors, and how individuals react when they fail.\nYu et al. (2014) developed an experimental task to disentangle these two typically confounded factors (see Method for\nmore details). The task consisted of multiple ’schedules’. In\neach schedule participants could win a monetary reward, indicated by an image of a coin divided into four parts. To get the\nmonetary reward, they needed to obtain all four parts of the\ncoin, thus each schedule constituted one goal-pursuit attempt\nwith multiple successive actions. In each schedule, some parts\nof the coin were already given to participants for ’free’, so the\nnumber of ’trials’ varied from one (i.e., only one quarter of the\ncoin was missing) to four (i.e., the whole coin was missing).\nTo obtain a missing part of a coin in a trial, participants had\nto do a task in which they indicated the left versus right\ndirection of arrows via button presses. At any stage however,\nthey could get blocked. That meant that even when the response\nwas correct, the participants were told that they were too slow\nand that they would not get the reward. By manipulating the\nnumber of trials in a schedule and at which stage participants\nwere blocked, Yu et al. (2014) were able to disentangle expended\neffort and goal proximity. For example, when participants were\nblocked at the very first trial out of four trials, both goal proximity and expended effort were low; when they were blocked in\nthe last trial out of four trials, both goal proximity and expended\neffort were high (i.e., the typical situation with the two\nfactors confounded). However, when participants were blocked\nin the first trial in a one-trial schedule (i.e., three quarters of\na coin were already available, the schedule consisted of only\none trial), goal proximity was high (as participants were close\nto obtaining the reward) but expended effort was low (as they\nonly did one trial). Using this procedure, Yu et al. (2014)\nshowed that goal proximity and expended effort independently\ninfluenced how much force and how quickly participants\nresponded to the task when they were blocked from obtaining\na reward. Generally, participants responded with more force\nand faster the closer they were to their goal and the more effort\nthey had expended to reach the goal. This finding on response\nforce and response times (RT) was further corroborated by the\nself-reported frustration and motivation at the end of the\nexperiment, with both showing an increase as goal proximity\nand expended effort increased. Taken together, this study\nshows that goal proximity and expended effort, two frequently\nconfounded factors, independently contribute to the motivational\nand emotional influences of reward blockage in successive\ngoal-pursuit behaviors.\n\n\nThe task by Yu et al. (2014) successfully disentangled the\nmotivational and emotional influences of expended effort\nand goal proximity during reward blockage, two important\nyet frequently confounded factors. As this could be a very\nvaluable tool for studying reward blockage and frustration,\nwe aimed to replicate and extend the original study by Yu et al.\n(2014). Yu et al. (2014). As an exploratory analysis to\nassess both effects simultaneously, we analyzed both arrow\nRTs and confirm RTs using Bayesian mixed-effects models in\nbrms (Bürkner, 2021). For both analyses, the RT data were first\nlog transformed. We simultaneously included proximity level\n(from 1 to 4, with a larger value standing for more proximity\nto a reward) and effort level (from 1 to 4, with a larger value\nstanding for more expended effort) as predictors, with random\nintercepts and random slopes per participant. Both proximity\nand effort were modelled as monotonic predictors (Bürkner &\nCharpentier, 2018). The Student’s t distribution was used as the\nlikelihood function rather than the default normal distribution,\nas the latter did not fit the arrow RT data well. Default\npriors in brms were used. For MCMC sampling, we ran 4\nparallel chains with 3000 iterations in the warm-up phase and\n5000 iterations in the sampling phase per chain. We inspected\nthe trace plots, R hat values, effective sample sizes and\nposterior predictive checks to ensure that there were no\nconvergence issues and that the estimates were stable.\nFor the arrow RT, we observed a negative coefficient for\nproximity (estimate = -0.017, 95% CI = [-0.024, -0.011]). This\nindicates that participants responded more quickly in an arrow\ntask when they were closer to the end of a schedule. The coefficient for effort was also negative, estimate = -0.008, 95%\nCI = [-0.013, -0.004], indicating that participants responded\nmore quickly in an arrow task when they had exerted more\neffort (i.e., completed more trials) in a schedule so far.\nFor the confirm RT, we observed a different pattern: the\ncoefficient for proximity was positive, estimate = 0.017, 95%\nCI = [0.001, 0.350], while the coefficient for effort did not\ndiffer from 0 credibly, estimate = 0.003, 95% CI = [-0.019,\n0.027]. The exploratory analysis thus showed that contrary\nto the effect of proximity on arrow RTs, participants\nconfirmed an outcome more slowly when the reward blockage\nwas closer to the end of a schedule. This was in contrast\nto the original study, which did not find any effect on the\nconfirm RT. Note that when we used the same data analysis\napproach above as in the original study, we also did not observe\nstatistically significant effects of proximity or effort. This\ndiscrepancy in results might arise because the exploratory\nanalysis here included proximity and effort simultaneously\nin one model and analyzed data on each trial (rather than\naggregated means in each condition), and thus might have\nmore statistical power to detect an effect. These exploratory\nresults on confirm RTs were also in line with our finding that\nparticipants were more surprised by reward blockage when\nproximity was high, but expended effort did not influence selfreported surprise. The positive correlation between self-reported\nsurprise and the mean RT of confirming a blocked outcome\n(Figure 3) further corroborated the idea that being blocked\n\nwhen getting close to a reward might be a surprising event\nthat slowed responses down.\n\nfacial EMG analyses\nWe tested whether the zygomaticus major and corrugator supercilii muscles were differentially activated in win versus blocked\ntrials. In the current study, we investigated the influence of\ngoal proximity and expended effort on frustration by trying\nto replicate and extend the study by Yu et al. (2014). For this,\nparticipant completed multi-trial schedules in order to obtain\na reward. On each trial they had to indicate the direction of\narrows by a left or right button press, and had to complete one\nto four arrow trials successfully to obtain a reward, depending on the schedule. Importantly, they could get blocked\nat any stage of the schedule, resulting in different levels of\ngoal proximity (i.e., the number of trials that still needed\nto be completed to obtain the reward) and expended effort\n(i.e., the number of trials that were already completed). In\nline with Yu et al. (2014), we measured the RT in the arrow\ntask, the RT and force with which participants confirmed the\noutcome of the current schedule, and self-reported surprise,\nmotivation and frustration for each schedule stage at the end\nof the experiment. As an extension, we also measured the\nactivity over the corrugator supercilii and the zygomaticus\nmajor as online measures of emotional valence. Note that our\nforce measure was most likely underestimated. Thus, we will\nnot discuss any theoretical implications related to these force\nresults and will instead focus on the remaining measures.\nIn the self-report measures, we were able to replicate Yu\net al. (2014)’s finding that the closer participants were to the\nreward and the more effort they had exerted, the more motivation they felt in the arrow task. In line with their self-reported\nmotivation, participants responded faster to the arrows the\ncloser they got to the expected reward and the more effort they\nhad exerted. When they were eventually blocked from a\nreward, they reported more frustration when getting blocked\ncloser to the reward and with more effort exerted. Using the\n\n\nFigure 4. In sum, we were able to\nreplicate the effects (or the absence of the effects) of proximity\nand effort on arrow RTs and confirm RTs, and self-reported\nmotivation and frustration at the end of the experiment.\nSome results differed from those of Yu et al. (2014) though.\nFirst, participants were more surprised by a blocked outcome\nwhen they were getting closer to the end reward, while exerted\neffort did not influence surprise. This pattern of results was\ncorroborated by the results of an exploratory analysis on\nconfirm RT, in which we found that higher proximity (but not\nhigher effort) increased the RT of confirming an outcome.\nFurthermore, self-reported surprise also correlated with\nconfirm RT, so that the more surprised participants felt in a schedule, the slower they were in confirming the outcome. Lastly,\nin the facial EMG data, we found no difference in activation\nwhen winning compared to getting blocked, neither in the\ncorrugator supercilii nor in the zygomaticus major.\nIn general, our replication results seem to be valid despite the\nunderestimation of the force data: the self-report measures\nshow that we were able to induce frustration in participants\nand that they were generally more motivated to successfully\nfinish the task in later schedule stages. Furthermore, our RT\ndata are likely valid. The mean arrow RTs in the 10 schedules were negatively correlated with the mean self-reported\nmotivation, suggesting that participants indeed responded\nmore quickly when they were more motivated to complete\na schedule. The finding that participants got slower in confirming their outcome after they had been blocked at later\nschedule stages is in contrast to the original study, but in line\nwith the self-reported surprise ratings. In the original study, Yu\net al. (2014) did not find an effect of surprise. Here however,\nwe found that participants are more surprised the later they got\nblocked. This surprise might have induced an orienting response\n(Notebaert et al., 2009; Wessel, 2018) leading to slower\nresponse times in the confirm task after getting blocked at a\nlater schedule stage. In summary, it seems that participants\nwere highly motivated, which was reflected in the response\nspeed as long as they thought they had control over the\noutcome. Once they got blocked, participants were surprised and\ntook longer to confirm this outcome (Notebaert et al., 2009).\nNote that other other research using gambling tasks found\nspeeding after reward blockage (see Eben et al., 2020;\nVerbruggen et al., 2017). Since earlier research however\nsuggests that perceived control over the outcome plays an\nimportant role in whether participants speed up or slow down\nafter sub-optimal outcomes (Eben et al., 2023), we assume that\nhere, participants might have felt in control over the outcome\nand hence showed an orienting response after being blocked.\nOur facial EMG results did not show the expected results.\nWhile some participants showed the expected effects most of\nthe participants did not show much activation difference over\nthe corrugator supercilii or the zygomaticus major for wins\n\nand blocked trials. However, given that some participants\nshowed more activation of the zygomaticus major than over the\ncorrugator supercilii in the win trials, we assume that fEMG\nin general seems to be a suitable online measure of emotional\nvalence (Mauss & Robinson, 2009). Here we assume that the\nsample size might have been too small to detect reliable\ndifferences in psychophysiological measurements. After all,\nthe sample size was calculated based on a behavioral medium\neffect size.\nAs already mentioned, we ran into post-hoc problems with our\nforce measurements, presumably because not all participants\nplaced their fingers directly and constantly on top of the sensors. Unfortunately, we have to assume that all force data are\nunderestimated in our experimental set-up, even in participants\nwho do not have 0 values in their force measurement. Note that\nsimilar set-ups with sensors on top of keys have been used\nsuccessfully in previous studies to measure force (e.g., Pickering\net al., 2020), albeit not in the context of examining ’frustration’.\nIn hindsight, the requirement for participants to keep their\nfingers constantly on the sensors (and not moving their fingers)\nmay be inappropriate in the current study, as the ’frustration’\ntriggered by blocked reward may lead participants to move\ntheir fingers despite the instructions to not do so. This may be\nthe reason why the force measures with our custom box were\ninvalid. Alternative ways of measuring force exist, such as by\nattaching buttons to a leaf spring, and measuring the force\napplied via strain gauges (e.g., Ko et al., 2012). In such a set-up,\nforce is determined by measuring how much the leaf spring is\nbent (which in turn is measured by strain gauges). There will\ntherefore be no requirement for participants to keep their\nfinger locations constant on the keys. While we initially\nexperimented with this idea, we eventually decided to not\nuse such a set-up, because the tactile feedback of keys being\npressed down was much less clear in this set-up than in our\nadopted set-up. Since we used rigged feedback to introduce\nblocked reward, such uncertainty in whether a key had indeed\nbeen pressed down or not might lead participants to adjust\ntheir subsequent responses, such as pressing keys harder\nto make sure that a response was registered. To reduce this\npotential influence, we therefore did not use this alternative\nset-up in our study. Yu et al. (2014) did not provide technical details on the custom box they used, so it is unclear whether\nthese differences in the boxes may explain the different results.\nDespite the force measurements being invalid, in light of\na continued publication bias in psychological research\n(Scheel et al., 2021), we would like to follow the call of\nLakens and Ensinck (2024) and make this research available,\nhoping it might be helpful for others who want to use the\nmeasure of response force or try to replicate the original study (see Pennington et al., 2023, for a similar take\non publishing results that might not answer the research\nquestion).\nIn conclusion, although the self-report measures suggest that\nfrustration was induced in our participants by blocking them\nfrom an expected reward, we were unable to replicate the\n\n\nbehavioral findings of Yu et al. (2014). Our RT data showed\nparticipants sped up in the arrow task the closer they got to the\nreward. Once they were blocked, participants seemed to be\nsurprised about the outcome and confirmed the outcome with\na slowed response. Given that our current results are not in\nline with the original study, yet plausible, we call for further\ninvestigations of frustration and the effects of goal proximity\nand exerted effort on frustration.\n\nEthics and consent\n\nThe research was approved by the Ethics committee of the Faculty\nof Psychology and Educational Sciences of Ghent University\n(ethical approval number 2022–135 approved 17/01/2023).\nParticipants provided written informed consent before starting\nthe experiment.\n\nData availability statement\n\nOpen Science Framework: “Escalating frustration - A replication\nattempt and extension of Yu et al. In the current study, we investigated the influence of\ngoal proximity and expended effort on frustration by trying to\nreplicate and extend the study by Yu et al. (2014). For this, participants completed multi-trial schedules in order to obtain a\nreward. On each trial they had to indicate the direction of arrows\nby a left or right button press, and had to complete one to\nfour arrow trials successfully to obtain a reward, depending\non the schedule. Importantly, they could get blocked at any\nstage of the schedule, resulting in different levels of goal\nproximity (i.e., the number of trials that still needed to be completed to obtain the reward) and expended effort (i.e., the\nnumber of trials that were already completed). In line with\nYu et al. (2014), we measured the RT in the arrow task, the RT\nand force with which participants confirmed the outcome of\nthe current schedule, and self-reported surprise, motivation\nand frustration for each schedule stage at the end of the experiment. As an extension, we also measured the activity over\nthe corrugator supercilii and the zygomaticus major as online\nmeasures of emotional valence. Note that our force measure\n\n\n\nFigure 5. However, each schedule contained varying number of trials, and the participants did experience many\ncorrect trials on those schedules where they were eventually\nblocked. Those correct responses potentially influenced the participants expectation to win on the\nnext trial. Thus, this surprise might have induced an orienting\nresponse (Notebaert et al., 2009; Wessel, 2018) leading to\n\n\nslower response times in the confirm task after getting blocked\nat a later schedule stage. In summary, it seems that participants\nwere highly motivated, which was reflected in the response\nspeed as long as they thought they had control over the outcome. Once they got blocked, participants were surprised and\ntook longer to confirm this outcome (Notebaert et al., 2009).\nNote that other other research using gambling tasks found\nspeeding after reward blockage (see Eben et al., 2020;\nVerbruggen et al., 2017). Since earlier research however suggests that perceived control over the outcome plays an important role in whether participants speed up or slow down after\nsub-optimal outcomes (Eben et al., 2023), we assume that here,\nparticipants might have felt in control over the outcome and\nhence showed an orienting response after being blocked.\n\nTo reduce this potential influence, we therefore did not use\nthis alternative set-up in our study. Yu et al. (2014) did not\nprovide technical details on the custom box they used, so it is\nunclear whether these differences in the boxes may explain the\ndifferent results.\n\nOur facial EMG results did not show the expected results.\nWhile some participants showed the expected effects most of\nthe participants did not show much activation difference over\nthe corrugator supercilii or the zygomaticus major for wins and\nblocked trials. However, given that some participants showed\nmore activation of the zygomaticus major than over the corrugator supercilii in the win trials, we assume that fEMG in general seems to be a suitable online measure of emotional valence\n(Mauss & Robinson, 2009). Here we assume that the sample\nsize might have been too small to detect reliable differences\nin psychophysiological measurements. After all, the sample\nsize was calculated based on a behavioral medium effect size.\n\nIn conclusion, although the self-report measures suggest that\nfrustration was induced in our participants by blocking them\nfrom an expected reward, we were unable to replicate all\nthe behavioral findings of Yu et al. (2014). As in the original\nstudy, our RT data showed participants sped up in the arrow\ntask the closer they got to the reward. Once they were blocked,\nhowever participants seemed to be surprised about the outcome and confirmed the outcome with a slowed response. These\nresults were in contrast to the original findings, which did not\nfind any effects of surprise or confirm RT. Given that our current results are not fully in line with the original study, yet\nplausible, we call for further investigations of frustration and\nthe effects of goal proximity and exerted effort on frustration.\n\nAs already mentioned, we ran into post-hoc problems with our\nforce measurements, presumably because not all participants\nplaced their fingers directly and constantly on top of the sensors. Unfortunately, we have to assume that all force data are\nunderestimated in our experimental set-up, even in participants\nwho do not have 0 values in their force measurement. Note that\nsimilar set-ups with sensors on top of keys have been used successfully in previous studies to measure force (e.g., Pickering\net al., 2020), albeit not in the context of examining ’frustration’. In hindsight, the requirement for participants to keep\ntheir fingers constantly on the sensors (and not moving their\nfingers) may be inappropriate in the current study, as the\n’frustration’ triggered by blocked reward may lead participants to move their fingers despite the instructions to not do\nso. This may be the reason why the force measures with our\ncustom box were invalid. Alternative ways of measuring\nforce exist, such as by attaching buttons to a leaf spring, and\nmeasuring the force applied via strain gauges (e.g., Ko et al.,\n2012). In such a set-up, force is determined by measuring\nhow much the leaf spring is bent (which in turn is measured\nby strain gauges). There will therefore be no requirement\nfor participants to keep their finger locations constant on the\nkeys. While we initially experimented with this idea, we eventually decided to not use such a set-up, because the tactile\nfeedback of keys being pressed down was much less clear in\nthis set-up than in our adopted set-up. Since we used rigged\nfeedback to introduce blocked reward, such uncertainty in\nwhether a key had indeed been pressed down or not might lead\nparticipants to adjust their subsequent responses, such as pressing keys harder to make sure that a response was registered.\n\nDespite the force measurements being invalid, in light of a\ncontinued publication bias in psychological research (Scheel\net al., 2021), we would like to follow the call of Lakens and\nEnsinck (2024) and make this research available, hoping\nit might be helpful for others who want to use the measure\nof response force or try to replicate the original study (see\nPennington et al., 2023, for a similar take on publishing results\nthat might not answer the research question).\n\nEthics and consent\n\nThe research was approved by the Ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences of Ghent University\n(ethical approval number 2022–135 approved 17/01/2023).\nParticipants provided written informed consent before starting\nthe experiment.\n\nData availability statement\n\nOpen Science Framework: “Escalating frustration - A replication\nattempt and extension of Yu et al.
