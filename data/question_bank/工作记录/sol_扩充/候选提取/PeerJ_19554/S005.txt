Background: Self-compassion plays a vital role in emotional well-being, especially for individuals with
disabilities who often face unique psychological challenges. When people treat themselves with kindness
instead of harsh self-criticism, they tend to cope better with diﬃculties and maintain a more positive
outlook. However, self-compassion isn’t the same for everyone as it can be shaped by many factors like
gender, disability history (congenital vs. acquired), and participation in leisure-time physical activity.
Understanding these inﬂuences can help develop better mental health support strategies and more
personalized rehabilitation programs.
Objectives: This study explored self-compassion levels among adults with disabilities and examined how
gender, disability history, and leisure-time physical activity inﬂuence self-compassion.
Methods: A cross-sectional study was conducted with 162 adults with disabilities (85 men and 77 women;
average age = 31.3 ± 10.69). Participants completed the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) and reported how
often they engaged in leisure-time physical activity each week.
Results: On average, participants showed moderate self-compassion levels (M = 3.49, SD = 0.58).
Among the six components of self-compassion, self-judgment (M = 3.69, SD = 0.90) and overidentiﬁcation (M = 3.71, SD = 0.92) were the highest, suggesting a tendency toward self-criticism and
emotional overwhelm. Self-kindness had the lowest score (M = 3.19, SD = 0.82), indicating that many
participants struggled to be compassionate toward themselves.
Our further analysis showed that leisure-time physical activity and disability history were signiﬁcant
predictors of self-compassion. People who regularly engaged in sports or exercise and those with
congenital disabilities tended to have higher self-compassion levels. Gender, however, was not
signiﬁcantly related to self-compassion. The overall model was signiﬁcant, F(3, 158) = 10.833, p < .001,
R² = 0.171, explaining 17.1% of the variance in self-compassion.
Conclusions: Many adults with disabilities struggle with self-criticism and emotional distress, but
engaging in regular leisure-time physical activity appears to support greater self-compassion. This
suggests that making sports and recreation more accessible could help promote emotional resilience in
this population. Additionally, the diﬀerences observed based on disability history highlight the need for
personalized mental health support, as individuals born with a disability may experience self-compassion
diﬀerently from those who acquired a disability later in life. Future research should further explore these
patterns to help improve well-being and psychological support systems for adults with disabilities.

Introduction

42

Self-compassion is an important psychological concept that involves being kind and

43

understanding toward oneself during times of struggle or failure, rather than engaging in harsh

44

self-criticism (Neff, 2003). It includes three key aspects: self-kindness, common humanity, and

45

mindfulness. Self-kindness means offering support and care to oneself in difficult moments

46

instead of self-judgment. Common humanity refers to recognizing that challenges and setbacks

47

are a shared part of being human. Mindfulness involves maintaining a balanced perspective on

48

painful emotions, avoiding both ignoring and exaggerating them (Neff, 2003). These elements

49

help individuals face challenges with greater resilience and acceptance.

50

Research consistently demonstrates the positive effects of self-compassion on various emotional

51

and psychological domains. It fosters positive emotions such as happiness, gratitude, and

52

optimism, which contribute to a more favorable outlook on life (Albertson et al. 2015),

53

Moreover, self-compassion enhances resilience, enabling individuals to effectively manage stress

54

and adversity (Neff & Germer, 2013). These benefits underscore the critical role of self-

55

compassion in promoting psychological well-being and emotional strength.

56

Self-compassion is strongly associated with life satisfaction, a fundamental measure of overall

57

wellbeing and quality of life (Diener et al., 1985). Extensive research demonstrates a positive

58

relationship between these constructs, showing that individuals with greater self-compassion

59

tend to report higher levels of well-being (Neff, 2011; Zessin et al., 2015)

60

This connection may arise from self-compassionate individuals' ability to manage stress and

61

negative emotions, fostering a more positive and optimistic perspective on life (Breines & Chen,

62

2012). These attributes are especially relevant for individuals with disabilities, as they assist in

63

accepting limitations and coping with challenges. Additionally, self-compassion is associated

64

with heightened motivation and personal growth, which further contribute to life satisfaction and

65

overall well-being (Neff & Germer, 2013)

66

For individuals with disabilities, self-compassion holds unique significance. They frequently

67

encounter societal barriers and challenges that can negatively impact their psychological health.

68

In recent years, there has been growing attention to the psychological well-being of people with

69

disabilities, with research aiming to identify factors that enhance mental health and self-

70

perception in this population (Alsamiri et al., 2024; Babik & Gardner, 2021).

71

Self-compassion can be a powerful tool for individuals with disabilities, helping them embrace

72

their limitations while building emotional resilience. Research shows that self-compassion is

73

linked to lower levels of depression and anxiety, providing individuals with effective ways to

74

manage stress and negative emotions, especially when facing disability-related challenges (Neff

75

& Germer, 2013). It also plays a crucial role in adjusting to changes in physical or psychological

76

abilities, making it easier to navigate and overcome obstacles (Leary et al., 2007).

77

Beyond emotional resilience, self-compassion helps reduce feelings of internalized stigma and

78

encourages a more positive self-image. It also strengthens social connections, as self-

79

compassionate individuals are more likely to seek support and accept help from others,

80

expanding their social networks (Gilbert & Procter, 2006). For individuals with disabilities, this

81

sense of connection is especially valuable, as it fosters a supportive environment that contributes

82

to overall well-being.

83

Self-compassion also plays a role in promoting physical health by encouraging behaviors that

84

support well-being, such as regular physical activity and adherence to medical treatments (Sirois,

85

& Hirsch, 2019). Notably, leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) has been found to enhance

86

psychological well-being by building emotional resilience and reducing stress. The interaction

87

between self-compassion and LTPA is particularly significant, as engaging in LTPA may further

88

cultivate self-compassion by providing individuals with opportunities to care for their bodies and

89

minds (Wong et al., 2021)., while self-compassion can motivate consistent participation in LTPA

90

by fostering a kinder, more supportive attitude toward personal health and fitness.

91

Building on this evidence, the present study investigates the relationship between engagement in

92

LTPA and self-compassion among individuals with disabilities. While existing research

93

highlights the positive effects of physical activity on life satisfaction and quality of life in this

94

population (e.g., Martin Ginis et al., 2010), little attention has been given to its specific influence

95

on self-compassion. Recognizing the established role of LTPA in fostering psychological

resilience, this study seeks to explore how LTPA may contribute to self-compassion in

97

individuals with disabilities. By addressing this gap, the research aims to deepen our

98

understanding of the psychological benefits of LTPA and its potential to enhance self-

99

compassion and overall well-being in this population.

Participants

103

104

105

106

110

Measuring self-compassion

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2024:10:107843:1:1:CHECK 26 Mar 2025)

Manuscript to be reviewed
122

Self-compassion was assessed using the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS), originally developed by

123

Kristin Neff (2003). The SCS is a well-established tool that evaluates how individuals respond to

124

personal struggles, capturing both self-compassionate and self-critical tendencies. The full 26-

125

item scale measures six core components of self-compassion: self-kindness (e.g., "I’m kind to

126

myself when I am experiencing suffering"), self-judgment (e.g., "I’m intolerant and impatient

127

toward those aspects of my personality I don’t like"), common humanity (e.g., "When I’m down

128

and out, I remind myself that there are lots of other people in the world feeling like I am"),

129

isolation (e.g., "When I fail at something I tend to feel alone in my failure"), mindfulness (e.g.,

130

"When I’m feeling down I try to approach my feelings with curiosity and openness"), and over-

131

identification (e.g., "When something upsets me I get carried away with my feelings").

132

Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = rarely to 5 = almost always), with

133

higher scores reflecting greater self-compassion.

134

The SCS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with high internal reliability

135

(Cronbach’s α > 0.86) and robust construct validity (Neff, 2003). It has been widely used to

136

examine the role of self-compassion in well-being, resilience, and mental health. While an

137

Arabic adaptation of the SCS exists (Alabdulaziz et al., 2020), we did not use this version

138

directly. Instead, we modified the scale by considering both the original English version and the

139

available Arabic adaptation, ensuring that the wording was linguistically and culturally

140

appropriate for our study population.

141

To assess the reliability of the modified version, we conducted a test-retest reliability analysis

142

with 16 disabled adults, who completed the scale twice over a two-week period. The Intraclass

143

Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was 0.84, demonstrating strong reliability and stability within this

144

population. Additionally, the scale exhibited high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha

145

of 0.882 for the overall measure in our study.

146

Several shorter versions of the SCS exist, including the 18-item and 17-item versions (Neff et al.,

147

2021), which retain the original six-factor structure but with fewer items, and the 12-item Short

148

Form (SCS-SF) (Raes et al., 2011), which provides a quicker assessment but has lower reliability

149

for individual subscales. Given the objectives of this study, we selected the full 26-item scale, as

150

it provides the most comprehensive and psychometrically sound assessment of self-compassion.

Measuring Leisure-Time Physical Activity (LTPA)

159

To understand participants' engagement in leisure-time physical activity (LTPA), we asked them

160

to report how often and for how long they exercised or participated in sports during their free

161

time. Specifically, they provided information on:

162



Frequency: How many times per week they engaged in sports or exercise activities.

163



Duration: The estimated average time (in minutes) spent on each session.

164

From these responses, we calculated each participant’s total weekly LTPA by multiplying the

165

number of sessions per week by the average duration per session. The total was expressed in

166

minutes of physical activity per week.

167

To classify activity levels, we used the physical activity guidelines set by the World Health

168

Organization (WHO):

169



Regular engagement: At least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week.

170



Moderate engagement: Less than 150 minutes of physical activity per week.

171



No engagement: No participation in any physical activity during leisure time.

172

These guidelines provide a useful benchmark to assess whether participants met the

173

recommended activity levels for maintaining overall health and well-being.

Discussion

222

This study explored self-compassion in adults with disabilities, examining how psychological

223

and social factors contribute to self-perceptions and emotional well-being. Findings revealed that

224

participants exhibited a moderate level of self-compassion, consistent with previous research

225

suggesting that individuals with disabilities often face unique psychological and social

226

challenges that influence their self-compassion (Neff, 2023). This aligns with studies indicating

227

that individuals exposed to external pressures, such as social stigma or accessibility barriers, tend

228

to experience heightened self-judgment and over-identification with their difficulties (Yarnell, et

229

al., 2015). The observed self-compassion levels were also partially consistent with findings from

230

non-disabled adults in the same cultural context (Jansen et al., 2020), suggesting that while some

231

aspects of self-compassion may be universal, others are shaped by the specific experiences of

232

living with a disability.

233

An analysis of self-compassion components revealed that over-identification and self-judgment

234

were the highest-scoring factors, indicating a tendency among participants to engage in harsh

235

self-criticism and emotional over-involvement with negative thoughts. These findings suggest

236

that many individuals in the study struggled to adopt a balanced perspective when facing

237

personal difficulties, reinforcing prior research that links high levels of self-judgment with

238

negative mental health outcomes in populations experiencing chronic adversity (Yarnell, et al.,

239

2015). Feelings of isolation were also relatively high, highlighting the importance of social

240

connection in fostering emotional resilience. In contrast, self-kindness was the lowest-scoring

241

component, indicating that participants found it particularly difficult to respond to themselves

242

with warmth and understanding. This pattern aligns with Gilbert’s model of self-compassion,

243

which suggests that persistent exposure to stressors, such as physical limitations or social

244

marginalization, can hinder the development of self-kindness (Gilbert, 2021). Mindfulness and

245

common humanity scores fell within the moderate range, suggesting that while participants were

246

sometimes able to maintain emotional balance and recognize shared human experiences, these

247

tendencies were not consistently strong. The findings emphasize the need for interventions that

248

target self-criticism and promote self-kindness, as these may be key areas for improving self-

249

compassion among adults with disabilities.

250

The regression analysis provided further insight into the factors influencing self-compassion,

251

identifying disability history and leisure-time physical activity as significant predictors. Notably,

252

individuals with acquired disabilities exhibited higher self-compassion than those with

253

congenital disabilities. This finding supports the argument that individuals who develop a

254

disability later in life may cultivate resilience and adaptive coping mechanisms over time,

255

allowing them to integrate self-compassion into their self-perception (Stuntzner & Hartley,

256

2015). Conversely, those born with a disability may experience prolonged exposure to societal

257

stigma or internalized self-judgment, which could contribute to lower self-compassion levels

258

(Gilbert, 2021). This highlights the need for early interventions that foster self-compassionate

259

perspectives in individuals with congenital disabilities, particularly in addressing the potential

260

long-term impact of societal attitudes and personal expectations.

The association between leisure-time physical activity and self-compassion further reinforces the

264

277

The findings of this study have important implications for developing interventions and policies

278

279

280

281

282

283

285

308

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant role of leisure-time physical activity and

310

311

that interventions targeting self-criticism, promoting self-kindness, and increasing access to

312

physical activity could be effective strategies for enhancing emotional resilience in this

313

314
