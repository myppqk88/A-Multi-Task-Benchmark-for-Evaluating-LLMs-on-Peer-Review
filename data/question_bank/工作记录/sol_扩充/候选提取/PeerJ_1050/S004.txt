Attitudes of medical students in Lahore, Pakistan towards the
doctor-patient relationship
Waqas Ahmad, Edward Krupat, Yumna Asmaa, Noor-E- Fatima, Rayan Attique, Umar Mahmood, Ahmed Waqas

Background. A good doctor-patient relationship is the centre stone of modern medicine.
With each passing day patients are getting more and more aware about exercising their
autonomy and thus modern medicine cannot deliver all its advances to the patients if a
good doctor-patient relationship is not established. We initiated this study with the aim to
assess the leaning of medical students, who are the healers of tomorrow, towards either a
doctor-centered or a patient-centered care and explore the effects of personal attributes
on it like gender, academic year etc. Materials & Methods. A cross-sectional study was
conducted between July-Sep 2013. CMH Lahore Medical and Dental College Ethical Review
Committee approved the study questionnaire. The study population consisted of 1181
medical students in years 1-5 from two medical colleges. English version of PPOS was used
to assess attitudes of medical students towards doctor-patient relationship. The
relationship between PPOS scores and individual characteristics like gender, academic
year etc. were examined by multiple regression. Results. A total of 783 students formed
the final sample (response rate=92%). The total PPOS score of the entire sample was 3.40
( ± .49 S.D). Total sharing sub-scale score was 3.18 (± 0.62 S.D. Total caring sub-scale
score was 3.63 (± 0.56 S.D). Characteristics associated with most patient-centered
attitudes were advanced academic year, having a clinical rotation, having a foreign
background and studying in a private college. Gender, having doctor parents, relationship
and accommodation status had no bearing on the attitudes (p>0.05). Conclusion. Despite
ongoing debate and emphasis on a patient-centered curriculum, our study suggests that
current curriculum and its teachings are not producing the results they are designed to
achieve. Students should be adequately exposed to the patients from the beginning of
their medical education in clinical settings which are more sympathetic to a patientcentered care.

Introduction:

50

“A chain is only as strong as its weakest link”. In today’s healthcare system, the doctor-patient

51

relationship is the weak link which bears the weight of all the advances of health care system and its

52

delivery to the patient. The stronger the relationship, the better are the patient’s compliance to the

53

treatment (Safran et al., 1998), disease outcomes and satisfaction (Simpson et al., 1991; Stewart, 1995).

54

Just like a weak link in a chain, the doctor-patient relationship is under most strain when kept vertical

55

(doctor-centered or paternalistic) which doesn’t allow the patient any control over the flow of

56

information or treatment. On the other hand when it is kept horizontal (patient-centered or egalitarian)

57

the patient is encouraged to play the role of a partner (Campbell & McGauley, 2005) and takes greater

58

responsibility for his own health (GROL et al., 1990). It benefits doctors by decreasing the incidence of

59

complaints and litigation (Beckman et al., 1994) and enables them to work at an optimum level to

60

attain the four prima facie maxims (beneficence, non-maleficence, respect for autonomy and justice)

61

(Tor, 2001) of modern medicine.

62

Patients in the modern era are becoming more and more autonomous (Shankar & Piryani, 2009),

63

an entity seldom considered in the past but modern medicine cannot advance without incorporating this

64

essential ethical necessity (Tor, 2001). Medical education in many parts of the world is still very much

65

disease-oriented (Hafferty, 1998) with hours and hours of lectures dedicated to the management of

66

diseases instead of patients as a whole. Studies have shown that good communication skills can be

67

achieved by structured training, which runs contrary to past beliefs that good communication is an

68

intrinsic quality of a doctor and cannot be taught (Smith et al., 2000).

69

Like clay that is cast in a mould to produce beautiful sculptures, medical students are also molded

70

in a cast, baked under intense pressures and finally come out as the healers who embody all the

71

properties that the cast, made by medical educators, has to offer. As healers of the future it is logical to

72

see what they think about the very foundation of modern medicine, doctor-patient relationship. A

73

growing body of research has demonstrated that medical students around the globe show wide

74

difference in their attitudes towards the doctor-patient relationship. Researchers have used a valid and

75

reliable scale called the Patient Practitioner Orientation Scale (PPOS) (Haidet et al., 2002) to measure

76

this attitude in countries like Nepal (Shankar et al., 2006), Korea (Choi, Kim & Park, 2004) and Greece

77

(Tsimtsiou et al., 2005). Medical students in Brazil have highly positive beliefs about patient centered

78

care (PPOS score of 4.66 ± 0.44 S.D) (Ribeiro, Krupat & Amaral, 2007), followed closely by

79

American medical students (PPOS score is 4.57 ± 0.48 S.D) (Haidet et al., 2002).

80

As indicated by a study in Nepal (PPOS score of 3.71 ± 0.48 S.D), medical students in Asia

81

have a tendency towards Doctor Centered care (Haidet et al., 2002; Shankar et al., 2006), which is

82

associated with decreased patient satisfaction (Krupat et al., 2000) in many of the countries this

83

relationship has been studied.

84

Paucity of knowledge on dynamics of doctor-patient relationship in Pakistani medical schools

85

warranted this study which has been designed with two aims: 1) to assess the leaning of Pakistani

86

medical students towards either a doctor or patient centered care 2) to analyze associations of

87

demographic characterstics, year of study and student-patient interaction with patient centered care as

88

assessed with PPOS.

Materials & Methods:

91

Study Sample:

92

Descriptive, cross-sectional study design and convenience (non-probability) sampling technique was

93

employed. In Pakistan undergraduate medical education lasts 5 years. This includes 2 pre-clinical years

94

and 3 clinical years. Students from 1st and 2nd academic year (pre-clinical years) do not have a clinical

95

rotation in their curriculum. The dominant form of teaching in the medical colleges across the country

96

is conventional consisting of didactic lecture techniques, non-problem-based learning (PBL) teaching

97

methods, long lectures, tutorials and practical tasks (Waqas et al., 2015). The attitudes of medical

98

students of academic year 1 to 5 from two medical colleges, a Government College (Allama Iqbal

99

Medical College) and a Private College (CMH Lahore Medical College), were assessed towards

100

doctor-patient relationship between July 2013 and Sep 2013. A standardized questionnaire with

101

English version of PPOS and a series of demographic questions was used. Forms were distributed to

102

1274 students (858 in govt. and 416 in private) out of which 1181 responded [collective response rate

103

92% (91% and 94.2 % respectively)]. Out of 1181, 398 forms were discarded due to incomplete

104

demographics and more than 3 missing responses in PPOS (final sample N=783). Total number of

105

students in Allama Iqbal Medical College, Lahore is 1,650 and 650 in CMH Lahore Medical College.

106

Instrument:

107

The doctor-patient relationship was assessed by using a reliable instrument called Patient Practitioner

108

Orientation Scale (PPOS) (Haidet et al., 2002). The PPOS contains 18-items and uses a Likert-scale

109

format and measures the respondent’s leaning towards a doctor-centered or a patient-centered belief.

110

Each item has 6 possible responses ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 6 (strongly disagree). The scale

111

has two subscales which measure two domains of doctor-patient relationship: Sharing and Caring.

112

Sharing refers to an individual’s belief that a patient should share the power, control and flow of

113

information equally with their doctor. Caring refers to an individual’s belief that a patient should be

114

treated as a whole and treated with good emotional rapport rather than as a condition or disease. Both

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:04:4693:1:0:NEW 5 May 2015)

Reviewing Manuscript

115

sub-scales have 9 items each. All the scores are reported as mean score ranging from 1 (doctor

116

centered) to 6 (patient centered).

117

Statistical Analysis:

118

SPSS 21 was used for analysis. Descriptive statistics and frequencies were calculated for subscale

119

scores on PPOS and demographic variables respectively. Multiple regression analysis (backward

120

method) was run to predict PPOS scores, sharing and caring subscale scores from gender, age, study

121

year, rotation (Outpatient department, inpatient department and not applicable) and nationality

122

(Pakistani/overseas). The assumptions of linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, unusual

123

points and normality of residuals were assessed.

CMH Lahore Medical and Dental College Ethical Review Committee approved the study

127

College, Lahore.

Results:

129

Students from academic year 1-5 of both colleges participated in this research (N= 783). There were

130

226 (28.9%) males and 557 (71.1%) female students. The sample distribution by gender, college,

131

academic year etc. is shown in Table-1. The mean PPOS score of the entire sample was 3.40 (±0.49

132

S.D). Mean sharing sub-scale score was 3.18 (± 0.62 S.D). Mean caring sub-scale score was 3.63 (±

133

0.56 S.D). Multiple regression analysis yielded significant models for mean PPOS scores, sharing and

134

caring scores (Table 2). Mean PPOS scores were positively associated with students from privately

135

financed medical college and foreign background. Students rotating in inpatient or outpatient

136

departments (OPDs) scored significantly higher on PPOS scale than those who were not rotating in a

137

clinical setting at present. Similar trends were observed in sharing and caring domains. Students having

138

a foreign background, currently in a higher academic year and studying in a privately financed medical

Discussion:

146

Our findings suggest that Pakistani medical students very much believe in the taboo of “Doctor Knows

147

Best” (Tor, 2001). They scored even lower than their Nepali counterparts (Shankar et al., 2006), except

148

in caring domain, making them the most doctor-centered of those samples of medical students in

149

several studies done around the world. Female gender, which is traditionally associated with patient-

150

centered care and is shown to have leaning towards it (Haidet et al., 2002), had statistically the same

151

distribution of PPOS scores as males of this sample (p>0.05). This finding although contradictory to

152

the studies conducted in America and Brazil is consistent with findings in Nepal (another Asian

153

country) (Haidet et al., 2002; Shankar et al., 2006; Ribeiro, Krupat & Amaral, 2007). This consistency

154

might be due to social, religious and cultural differences present in the two continents i.e. Americas

155

and Asia.

156

No clear pattern was established in consecutive academic years in terms of mean PPOS scores or

157

caring sub-scale scores. However sharing domain scores showed a clear positive association with

158

higher academic years which suggests that students, as they go into higher academic years, become

159

more aware about the rights of the patients and are willing to share the power of treatment choices with

160

them. This finding is similar to the study done in Brazil (Ribeiro, Krupat & Amaral, 2007) but

161

contradictory to the ones done in USA where good patient-centred care is associated with early

162

academic years (Haidet et al., 2002). Attributes associated with leaning towards patient-centred care

163

were: studying in a privately financed medical college, having a foreign background and rotating in

164

outpatient or inpatient departments as opposed to those who were not currently rotating in a clinical

165

setting. These attributes were consistent throughout the mean PPOS scores and sharing sub-scale.

166

Privately financed medical colleges have a better teacher to student ratio with a lesser patient load in

167

attached private hospitals as opposed to government financed colleges. This might explain the higher

168

scoring of students of private medical school. Another interesting finding in our study was that students

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:04:4693:1:0:NEW 5 May 2015)

Reviewing Manuscript

169

who had a clinical rotation (students in clinical years), either in outpatient or inpatient departments,

170

scored better than those who did not have a rotation (students in pre-clinical years). These finding

171

clearly divide our sample into the ones who just see patient on pages of books while the others who

172

interact with them and see them as a whole. It also illustrates the importance of patient interactions and

173

necessitates the student-patient interaction to begin at an early stage of medical training. Students who

174

rotated in an inpatient setting showed a stronger positive association with mean scores on PPOS than

175

those who rotated in an outpatient setting which could be due to a continuous flow of patients in

176

outpatient setting who get a very brief interaction with doctor/students as opposed to patients in wards

177

who stay there for long durations and offer a better chance for students to get to know them and see

178

them as a whole rather than a disease. These findings can also be partly explained by the introduction

179

of the subject of Behavioral sciences in clinical years at Pakistani medical schools which emphasize the

180

importance of a good doctor-patient relationship and a holistic approach towards patients. Better

181

performance by foreign students might be due to not sharing the Asian culture which is associated with

182

doctor-centered care (Haidet et al., 2002).

183

When a cast of clay comes out of a mould, it bears all the qualities of that mould. Before baking,

184

the clay is pliable and can be bent into any shape but once it is cast in the mould and is baked then it is

185

difficult to impart changes in it because it is shaped forever in the way that mould was designed. That’s

186

exactly what medical education does to medical students who come to this field to heal patients

187

(Lloyd-Williams & Dogra, 2004) but instead are taught to heal the disease only. The mould they are

188

put in has no room for the development of characteristics like good communication skills etc. which

189

are necessary for a good patient-centered care. The pressures they are exposed to (academic, psycho-

190

social and health related) further retard their shaping into a patient-centered practitioner (Waqas et al.,

191

2015).

192

However, this does not mean that medical students after leaving medical school cannot modify their

193

attitudes but it is much more beneficial to the patients and healthcare system if they are taught to focus

194

on the patient as a whole sooner than later in their medical career. Another reason for medical students

195

to be more doctor-centered could be due to the teaching style of practicing doctors who teach them

196

while attending to their patients. The environment they teach in is contradictory to the ideal, students

197

are taught in lecture theatres (Grilo et al., 2014). Humayun A et al. have found that Pakistani doctors

198

did not take informed consent from more than 71% patients and provided adequate confidentiality to

199

less than 24% of their patients (Humayun et al., 2008). Informed consent is defined as “patients’

200

autonomy in decisions and right to complete information” and confidentiality entails right of the

201

patient to informational privacy (Humayun et al., 2008). When medical students are taught in such a

202

doctor-centered environment it is natural for them to embody such practices because when a student

203

realizes that doctors, not following the prima facie maxims (Tor, 2001), are still able to have a very

204

healthy practice then he wonders if formalities like consent or confidentiality even matter in the real

205

world medicine. Doctors in the government owned hospital did not take consent from more than 90%

206

patients and provided adequate confidentiality to less than 11% of their patients (Humayun et al.,

207

2008). Teachings of such doctors could explain our finding that medical students from government

208

owned medical school scored lower on PPOS than of private medical school (p<0.05).

209

Shaikh et al. have reported the prevalence of stress to be 90% in Pakistani medical students (Shaikh et

210

al., 2004). Further studies have shown that psychological stress can cause poor attitudes towards the

211

chronically ill, decreased empathy and high levels of cynicism (Crandall, Volk & Loemker, 1993)

212

which together amounts to a less favorable patient care. Students who experience these stresses seldom

213

seek help because of the stigma revolving around psychiatric illnesses (Waqas et al., 2014). For this

214

reason, medical educators should make it mandatory to see the prevalence of such psychiatric

215

illnesses/stressors during the course of medical education and should take prompt actions to protect

216

students from their harmful effect and “nip the evil in the bud”.

218

Conclusion

219

If we want to produce healers who treat the patient as a whole then medical educators would have to

220

incorporate much more space in the mould of medical education for the essential characteristics (like

221

good communication skills, empathy etc.) necessary to achieve the prima facie maxims of modern

222

medicine. Students should be adequately exposed to patients from the beginning of their graduate

223

program and in clinical settings which are more favorable to a patient-centered care. Continuous

224

monitoring of the students should be done to identify and mend the factors which push them away from

225

a patient-centered caring attitude and patient-centered role models should be sought for students to

226

observe and follow. Most of what is learned during the graduate program is through “Hidden

227

Curriculum” which is a set of influences functioning at the level of organizational structure and culture

228

(Hafferty, 1998). This is mostly true for Pakistan since its biggest medical university (UHS) has, in

229

recent years, introduced Behavioral Sciences as an integral part of the curriculum in 2007 which is yet

230

to produce its effects in medical practice of Pakistan. To fight hidden curriculum both the curriculum

231

designers and college administration would have to act to mitigate its toxic effects on the development

232

of doctor-patient relationship.

Limitations and suggestions for future research:

235

Despite our efforts to completely explore the attitude of medical students towards doctor-patient

236

relationship we strongly believe that additional factors should be incorporated into further research

237

done in the future in this domain.

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:04:4693:1:0:NEW 5 May 2015)

Reviewing Manuscript

238

I.

Academic staff from the respective colleges and hospitals was not included in this study which
could have aided in measuring the extent of the problem.

239
240

II.

We conducted our study in just one city. Further studies should include a broader sample

241

comprising of medical students from all the four provinces and all religious and ethnic groups

242

to see if these factors have any effect on Patient-Centered Care.

243

III.

The cross-sectional design of this study limits inferences about causality and temporality.

244

IV.

Since PPOS just measures the orientation and not the behavior of medical students towards

245

Patient-Centered care, future researchers should include means to see the behavior of medical

246

students toward this entity.
