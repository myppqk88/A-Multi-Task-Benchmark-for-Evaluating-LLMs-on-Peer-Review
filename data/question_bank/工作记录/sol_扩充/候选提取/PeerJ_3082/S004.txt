Analysis of different vibration patterns to guide blind people
Juan V. Durá-Gil Corresp., 1 , Bruno Bazuelo-Ruiz 1 , David Moro-Pérez 1 , Fernando Mollà-Domenech 1
1

Instituto de Biomecánica de Valencia, Universitat Politècnica de València, Valencia, Spain

Corresponding Author: Juan V. Durá-Gil

The literature indicates the best vibration positions and frequencies on the human body
where tactile information is transmitted. However, there is a lack of knowledge about how
to combine tactile stimuli for navigation. The aim of this study is to compare different
vibration patterns outputted to blind people and to determine the most intuitive vibration
patterns to indicate direction for navigation purposes through a tactile belt. The vibration
patterns that stimulate the front side of the waist are preferred for indicating direction.
Vibration patterns applied on the back side of the waist could be suitable for sending
messages such as stop.

Abstract

2

The literature indicates the best vibration positions and frequencies on the human body

3

where tactile information is transmitted. However, there is a lack of knowledge about how

4

to combine tactile stimuli for navigation. The aim of this study is to compare different

5

vibration patterns outputted to blind people and to determine the most intuitive vibration

6

patterns to indicate direction for navigation purposes through a tactile belt. The vibration

7

patterns that stimulate the front side of the waist are preferred for indicating direction.

8

Vibration patterns applied on the back side of the waist could be suitable for sending

9

messages such as stop.

10

Introduction

11

Visual sensory substitution can take a variety of forms and may be mediated by a

12

device, such as a global positioning system (GPS), a cell phone (Ranjbar & Stenström,

13

2013) or a PDA (Ghiani, Leporini & Paternò, 2009). However, their use is limited to familiar

14

environments (Kärcher et al., 2012) or museums (Ghiani, Leporini & Paternò, 2009).

15

Faugloire & Lejeune (2014) compared spatial language with tactile guidance and obtained

16

better responses with vibrations. Other relevant aspects are the number of vibrating

17

elements, known as tactors, 6 to 64, (Cholewiak & Craig, 1984; Cholewiak, Brill &

18

Schwab, 2004; Faugloire & Lejeune, 2014) and the body site where the vibration is

19

applied. Typical body sites include the thigh (Cholewiak & Craig, 1984), fingers

20

(Cholewiak, 1999; Ghiani, Leporini & Paternò, 2009), palm (Cholewiak & Craig, 1984),

21

back (Srikulwong & O’Neill, 2010) and waist (Faugloire & Lejeune, 2014). A disadvantage

22

of the devices worn on the hand or fingers is that they limit the freedom to take or

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:07:12191:1:1:NEW 14 Dec 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
23

manipulate something with the hand itself. Placing tactors on the waist frees up the upper

24

limbs to perform any act freely. Due to the torso being relatively flat, stable, large and

25

easily accessible compared to the limbs, it is a good option for indicating direction

26

(Johnson & Higgins, 2006).

27

Although the amount of information that can be perceived through touch is less

28

than that which can be perceived through vision (Ghiani, Leporini & Paternò, 2009), haptic

29

devices have the advantage that they can be useful in noisy environments (Marston et

30

al., 2006).

31

However, there is a lack of information in the literature about what vibration

32

patterns are more intuitive for navigation purposes. This paper presents a preliminary

33

study regarding the feasibility of guiding blind people with a belt that applies tactile stimuli.

34

This study is part of a project that aims to develop a running facility embedded in a 400m

35

athletic track for visually impaired people to run independently without the assistance of

36

others. If tactile guidance is feasible, the project expects to develop real-time tracking of

37

blind runners based on an Radio-frequency position detection technology (RFID), which

38

can be deployed in a stadium around a running track and is able to operate in real-time.

39

A belt is considered a feasible solution for runners because it will not disrupt their

40

movements. The aim of the present study is to compare different vibration patterns in

41

order to define design criteria for guiding blind people by means of tactile navigation

42

devices. Moreover, considering that recruitment of an adequate number of blind people

43

might be difficult in future project phases, this preliminary study analyses the feasibility of

44

undertaking tests with blindfolded participants that have no sight impairments. This data

45

will allow us to determine if the vibration patterns designed are able to provide tactile

46

information so as to continuously specify the intended direction relative to the current

47

destination of the user.

Methods

49

Participants

50

Twenty people participated in a study that includes three experiments. The main

51

criterion used to select blind people was that they needed to use a long cane to find their

52

way and avoid obstacles. Sighted people were blindfolded during the experiments. None

53

of the participants had previously worn or had any experience with vibrotactile displays.

54

In the first and second experiments, there were a total of twelve subjects: six blind

55

(three women and three men) and six sighted people (three women and three men); age:

56

32.17±9.92 years; mass: 65.75±10.73 kg; height: 1.71±0.05 m; BMI: 22.4±3.1; waist

57

circumference: 82.2±10.3 cm.

58
59

Participants did the first and second experiment consecutively on the same day
under laboratory conditions (indoors).

60

The third experiment is performed after one month (outdoor). Different participants

61

are recruited because the previous participants were not available. Eight people took part

62

in the study: four blind (three man and one woman) and four sighted people (two women

63

and two men); age: 33.57±10.11 years; mass: 65.57±10.40 kg; height: 1.69±0.05 m; BMI:

64

22.81±2.81; waist circumference: 79.66±4.03 cm.

65

Each participant volunteered and the information and informed consent was

66

provided and signed by all subjects. The study protocol received approval by the Ethics

67

Committee (Universitat Politècnica València): approval number: 765 - 16/07/2013.

Materials and Procedures

69

The study consisted of three experiments lasting 30 minutes each. A belt with eight

70

tactors was placed on each subject. All the tactors were placed at the same height. Tactor

71

1 was placed 25 mm over the navel. Tactor 5 was placed on the spine. The other tactors

72

were equally spaced between tactor 1 and tactor 5 (Figure 1). We selected this placement

73

for the belt because it has been used in the literature to transmit tactile information

74

successfully (Cholewiak, Brill & Schwab, 2004). The waist circumference was measured

75

at this level. Tactors were equally distributed. The stimuli transmitted wirelessly consisted

76

of 200 ms ON and 500 ms OFF, with frequency, amplitude and dimensions as indicated

77

in the technical specification data sheet of the “Science Suit” provided by Elitac:

78



Vibration frequency of 158.3 ± 2.4 Hz.

79



Maximum vibration strength: 55.5 ± 9.5 m/s².

80



Tactor outer dimensions (lxwxh): 34x16x11 mm.

81

Subjects choose vibration intensity according to preference. The vibrotactile signal was

82

perceived well by all participants.

In the first experiment, 13 vibration patterns were outputted and the subjects do a

86

multiple choice questionnaire: left, right, go ahead, other, or not answer. The subject

87

provides an oral description if he selects “other” (i.e. go back, stop, etc.). The patterns are

88

chosen in order to analyse differences between: (i) stimuli applied in the anterior region

89

vs. dorsal region, (i) stimuli applied in sequences vs. fixed area. The vibration patterns

90

presented are the following: A01: tactor 1 (navel); A02: tactor 5 (spine); A03: tactors 3

92

A07: sequence 8-7-6; A08: sequence 4-3-2; A09: sequence 6-7-8; A10: sequence 1-2-3;

94

The tactors of the patterns A01 to A05 (no sequence) were 200ms active and

95

500ms off. Patterns A06 to A13 were a sequence repeated a number of times, with each

96

tactor active 200ms. The subjects were prevented from hearing the vibratory stimuli. The

97

vibration patterns were outputted to the participants in a randomized order.

98

For each subject in the second experiment, the previous results were classified by

99

the responses given (left, right, continue ahead, other and no answer). The aim of this

100

phase was to compare the vibration in pairs. The subjects do pairwise comparisons,

101

through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (Saaty, 1990). The subject selects the best

102

pattern to indicate “turn left”, or “turn right”.

103

The first and second experiments were designed to choose the most suitable

104

vibration patterns for future experiments and to confirm that tests with blindfolded sighted

105

people were feasible.

106

The third experiment was performed outdoors on a real scale (1:10, length 40m,

107

0.90 m. width) athletic track. Three vibration patterns (Table 1) were outputted and the

108

subjects had to perform two laps walking for each pattern to determine whether a learning

109

process occurs between the first and the second lap. The vibration patterns were

110

outputted to the participants in a randomized order. Blind people could feel insecure if

111

there is no tactile feedback. For this reason, if the participant walks in in the right direction,

112

tactor 1 (navel) is always active. The vibration patterns were designed on the basis of the

113

results obtained in the previous experiments. The belt was connected to a laptop by

114

Bluetooth. A researcher controlled the pattern transmitted using the laptop. During the

115

laps, the number of times that the subject leaves the track was recorded. After performing

116

the two laps, the researcher asks the participant their thoughts on the pattern. The scores

Statistical analyses

123

In the first experiment, we analysed the differences between blind and sighted

124

people with a Fisher's exact test because the sample size is small. Moreover, in

125

experiment 1 and 3, we performed a frequency analysis with an additional qualitative

126

assessment of the opinion and feelings of all participants.

127

In experiment 2, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (Saaty, 1990) was carried out in

128

order to get the most suitable vibration pattern of the same category (left, right or continue

129

ahead). The subjects do pairwise comparisons. For example: A01 is better than A02 to

130

indicate turn left. This methodology transforms the pairwise comparisons in a score of 0

131

to 100 for each vibration pattern. The score is used as independent variable. Thereafter,

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:07:12191:1:1:NEW 14 Dec 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
132

a one-way analysis of means and a pairwise comparison with the Bonferroni method were

133

performed.

134

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine the influence of the

135

vibration pattern and lap order on the number of times that the subject leaves the track.

136

The statistical power analysis is done for three experiments separately. We use

137

contingency table in experiment 1. Therefore, we used the approach for Chi-square tests:

138

Cohen’s w (effect size). We use anova in experiments 2 and 3, therefore we apply the

139

Cohen’s f for measuring the effect size (Cohen, 1988).

Discussion

193

The aims of this study were to identify what the different vibration patterns

194

outputted suggest to blind and sighted people and to determine the most intuitive vibration

195

pattern to indicate direction through a tactile belt. Furthermore, this data could give us a

196

real insight into the effectiveness of a novel guiding system in a dynamic situation. To the

197

best of our knowledge, there is no other study that investigates the response to different

198

vibration patterns outputted on the back and waist by a tactile device in a static and

199

dynamic situation using blind people as subjects.

200

Regarding differences between blind and sighted people, the results are not conclusive

201

and more research is needed with more subjects.

202

Regarding the vibration rhythm, Faugloire & Lejeune (2014) applied two vibration

203

rhythms: long (1 s ON/ 4 s OFF vibrations) before movement and short (200 ms ON/ 200

204

ms OFF vibrations) during body rotation. They found a higher accuracy with the short

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:07:12191:1:1:NEW 14 Dec 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
205

tactile mode. Similarly, one stimulus (Van Erp, Carter & Andrew, 2006; van Erp, 2008)

206

was activated in a 100 ms ON/ 200 ms pattern. We used the time of activation of these

207

studies but we increased the gap between bursts to the user’s need to feel the intermittent

208

rhythm and not a continuous vibration. Therefore, our vibration rhythm was 200 ms or

209

100 ms ON depending on the degree of turning and 500 ms OFF. We aim to ensure a

210

rapid response by participants in order to correct their orientation at each moment. Our

211

results indicate that single bursts are better to indicate direction through a tactile belt.

212

Faugloire & Lejeune (2014) used a belt with the location of tactile stimulation

213

continuously indicating the requested direction relative to the current orientation of the

214

participants. The location of tactile stimulation is updated along with the body rotation of

215

the user. The authors used all tactors to indicate direction. In contrast, our system also

216

used 8 tactors but only those of the anterior part of the waist are employed to indicate

217

direction. The tactors of the posterior part of the waist may be used to indicate other

218

messages like stop or reduce speed. This is a clear and simple way of offering guidance

219

in a practical application.

220

Finally, we find considerable individual variability in the results of the third

221

experiment. Possible causes of variability are vibration patterns order (training effect),

222

and levels of motivation and concentration.

223

Conclusions

224

The present findings can be of great value in the design of tactile devices for

225

guiding blind people in physical activity like jogging or running. This study indicates that

226

a belt with tactile stimuli is a feasible solution for guiding blind runners on athletic tracks.

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:07:12191:1:1:NEW 14 Dec 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
227

The results indicate that single bursts might be better than sequence vibration patterns.

228

However, this result should be confirmed in further research with more participants. The

229

guiding process could be improved with an adequate training.

230

The information provided by the belt led to a positive emotional impact on

231

participants with enhanced feelings of security. The stimuli might be optimised in terms

232

of number of stimulators, vibration rhythm and frequency, and body locus of stimulation.

233

Further work is necessary for testing the vibration patterns in real conditions

234

including running on a real athletic track. Running might provoke more impacts and

235

stronger movements than walking. A new design of the belt might be necessary to prevent

236

vibration and movements while running.
