Thank you very much for the careful review and positive comments; they have been very helpful in rewriting the article. I have revised the essay addressing the remarks. Concretely, I have made the following changes:
You suggested that the major hypothesis is presented in a rather provocative way in the introduction by pointing at the conceptual shortcomings in the work of others, without adequately presenting the arguments that are later refuted. Indeed, the introduction only mentions the references without elaborating on the arguments. In response to your suggestion, I have rewritten the entire section. As it was not possible, given the length of an article, to discuss each of the authors, I have decided to focus on the work of Andrew Hoskins, both for its central place in the debate and for the extensive reflection Hoskins has undertaken throughout his work. In the current version: a) I have revised the main points he addresses (most of them also addressed in the article) and b) I emphasize what I interpret as the main point of divergence: the stability versus the dynamism of collective memory.
The second suggestion was to work more on the "Rethinking the Social" section. To make this section clearer, especially for a reader not familiar with Tarde, I have rewritten the section into two subsections. In the first, I outline the different notions of Durkheim (the foundational basis of Halbwachs) and Tarde on the social. In the second, I specify why Tarde's conception is useful for rethinking the “the collective” in the digital age and in particular, its importance for rethinking collective memory.
Minor changes:
I have removed the paragraph on the mediation of memory. However, I find it intriguing that, as a media scholar, you do not consider the body as a medium. Many critics do consider it, and I align with that perspective. To cite just two examples, Connerton's notion of "incorporating practices" in his book "How Societies Remember" or Tota's article, "Dancing the Present: Body Memory and Quantum Field Theory," included in the "Technologies of Memory" section of the Routledge International Handbook of Memory Studies (2016), both explore the idea of the body as a medium.

I have corrected the typo. 

Thank you again for reviewing the essay. Your remarks were very helpful, and I hope they are reflected in the new version.
