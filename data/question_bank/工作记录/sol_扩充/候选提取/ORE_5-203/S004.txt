Article: Operationalizing the European sustainability competence framework: Development and validation of learning outcomes for GreenComp
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.20927.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Tom KUPPENS
Status: approved
Date: 2025-11-08
Report ID: 61497
============================================================

The authors operationalised the European sustainability competence framework GreenComp into a validated set of observable, measurable and assessable learning outcomes. The article is one of the outcomes of the Erasmus funded OpenPass4Climate project. The authors adopted a four-phased approach, consisting of (1) a multistakeholder co-design process, (2) followed by a modified Delphi for expert validation, (3) analysis of the full coverage of the GreenComp framework, and (4) a final validation round with experts for the full set of adjusted learning outcomes. This approach resulted in a set of learning outcomes and corresponding assessment criteria that can mainly be used educational stakeholders in upper secondary, vocational and higher education in Western and Southern Europe. Moreover, the authors critically discuss the usability of the assessment tools and its potential risks within the context of open badge initiatives.
The article contains several strengths, including its roadmap to contextualise and develop the assessment tool even further, for instance, w.r.t. different educational cultures, education levels, disciplines or regional contexts. Moreover, the assessment tool goes beyond the tradition of evaluating the cognitive learning outcomes only, by also proposing attitudinal and behavioural learning outcomes and corresponding assessment criteria. Moreover, the authors address the very important tension between the granularity and holism of assessing sustainability competences. Another strength consists of the collaborative multistakeholder process which increases the potential uptake by and impact among educational practitioners.
I would like to suggest the following improvements:
The authors use the concept of ‘education for sustainable development’ which is contested because of the negative connotations of the word ‘development’. Since their approach is student-centred, why not use the concept of ‘learning for sustainability’ proposed by the European Commission?
The translation of the first sentence of the ‘Métodos’ section in Spanish does not fully correspond to the English version. The authors are recommended to check consistency of the translation.
Some descriptions of author roles seem to be rather project roles (e.g. project admin) and hence seem to be of little relevance in the contributions section.
The authors refer to several EU competence frameworks for lifelong learning. It might be inspirational to also mention the FinComp framework because of its progression model, which might reflect potential next steps for building on the authors’ work on the GreenComp operationalisation.
In the introduction, the authors mention that educational institutions fundamentally shifted the conceptualisation of their role, however, it is mainly experts and policy makers who advocate for this shift. The next step is for educational institutions to implement this, but this is far from reality yet. The authors might want to rephrase the first sentence of their introduction.
When referring to Sulitest, it is unclear what the alignment percentages mean. The authors might want to elaborate on the meaning and determination of these percentages.
Other projects that have been working on GreenComp assessment and roadmaps, include GreenScent and ECF4CLIM. Moreover, also the IMPACT project (impactforaction.eu) on impact assessment of the action competence for sustainability might be of interest here. The authors also refer to assessment scales such as SCMM, however, they do not refer to the sustainability consciousness questionnaire (SCQ) of Gericke et al. (2019) yet. I would also like to make the authors aware of developments in the assessment of transformative learning by referring to “A review of transformative learning assessment tools” published by Ngan Ha Nguyen, Thuy Nga Nguyen and Thi Thuy Tang in the Proceedings of the 4
th
Hanoi forum on pedagogical and educational sciences. It might be relevant to refer to these projects, scales and assessment instruments too in the section on “Current state of GreenComp assessment”.
The authors often refer to several tensions. One tension that has not been mentioned yet, is the tension between the normative character of sustainability education and the danger of a too instrumental approach towards sustainability education. It would be highly appreciated if the authors can reflect on how their assessment instrument address this tension as well.
The authors use Bloom's taxonomy to operationalize the diverse KSAs of GreenComp. However, Bloom's taxonomy is mostly known for his taxonomy for cognitive learning activities. One advantage is that it probably helped to make the knowledge (K) descriptions more observable/measurable. Although this can be helpful for developing observable learning outcomes for the knowledge (K) and skills (S) of GreenComp, it might be more problematic for the attitudes (A). Can you clarify how a the use of Bloom's taxonomy might have influenced the learning outcomes? Could you recommend taxonomies that might be helpful for the more attitudinal dimensions of GreenComp?
The authors adopted a modified Delphi approach. It is recommended to explain what the ‘modified’ dimension of the modified Delphi consisted of. How did information from the literature review or the introduction determine the questions asked in the Delphi rounds?
For the readers and future users of your assessment tool, it would be helpful to add the matrix that maps each of the KSA statements with the final learning outcomes as supplementary material to the article.
When the authors explain the revisions of the learning outcomes after the first validation round, they provide examples of improved action verbs. However, for some examples the ‘improvement’ is not really clear. For instance, the verb ‘demonstrate’ has been improved to ‘adapt’, whereas these two verbs actually have different meanings. Maybe it is better to provide less examples and provide just one example with its full learning objectives statement to make the improvement clearer.
Although most learning outcomes in table 1 have a clear connection with sustainability, for some this connection is still missing. Is it possible to reformulate the ones that lack the link to sustainability a little bit in that direction. See for instance the learning outcomes (LO) corresponding to competence 2.2 (LO2), 3.1 (LO2) and 4.3 (LO1). Moreover, it might be relevant to also include the social dimension of sustainability in LO1 of competence 4.2.
In the discussion, the authors refer to Mezirow’s publication from 1981 to argue that the core aim of EfS must be to transform learners’ perspectives. However, to the best of my knowledge, Mezirow himself did not address sustainability education in that paper. The authors might want to reformulate that statement. It would also be of added value if the authors could more deeply argue why and how their learning outcome assessment tool enables to detect potential change of problematic frames of reference.
The worked example for a single learning outcome is of high added value for a potential user. The worked example for the integrated assessment, however, lacks the same amount of detail. To make that worked example more useful for potential users, it is recommended to elaborate on the integrated example more deeply.
When discussing the tensions in the first paragraph of the ‘Challenges and limitations’ section, I would be interested to also see some reflection on the room for open-endedness in transformative sustainability education and how this is reflected in the proposed assessment framework (see also my earlier recommendation to discuss the tension between the normative character of EfS and the risk of a too instrumentalistic approach).
