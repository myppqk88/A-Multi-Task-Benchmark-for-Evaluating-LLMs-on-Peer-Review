Dear Dr Howlin,  Thank you very much for your helpful and constructive comments on our manuscript. We have submitted a revised version, which should be available online soon. Below, we summarise how we addressed your comments.    Introduction:
First paragraph: We agree that the original wording could be misinterpreted to suggest that we measured partner choice or the inhibitory effects of music on romantic feelings. We have therefore rephrased this section to align it more closely with our research questions, emphasising that this was an initial investigation into the perceived relevance of music in romantic relationships, combining quantitative ratings with open-ended questions.
Section The Present Study:
RQ1: We acknowledge that the original wording implied causality, whereas our analyses are correlational. RQ1 has therefore been revised to: "How important do individuals perceive music to be in romantic relationships overall, and how relevant do they consider it to be in fostering intimacy, passion, and commitment at different stages of romantic relationships?" We have also updated the Results section to refer consistently to “perceived importance” and “perceived relevance”.
Preregistration: We clarified that the research questions were not preregistered but were derived from predictions proposed by Bamford et al. (2024).
RQ2: Regarding your comment on RQ2 (whether it was hypothesis-driven or exploratory), we would like to clarify that the main analyses for this question were hypothesis-driven and based on two contrasting predictions. One hypothesis predicts a positive correlation, while the other predicts no correlation:
First hypothesis: Individuals often seek partners who are similar to themselves. Those with greater musical expertise or sensitivity to musical reward may therefore place greater importance on music in romantic relationships.
Contrasting hypothesis: Music may play a role in romantic relationships, regardless of an individual's musical background, since musicality could be considered a generally attractive trait.
Alongside the analyses conducted for RQ2, which were theoretically motivated, we also carried out exploratory analyses to determine whether there was a correlation between the perceived importance of music and demographic variables such as age, gender and education. In the Results section, we clarified which parts of the analyses were hypothesis-driven and which were exploratory. Methods
Dropouts: We have added a footnote to the Participants section providing more details on survey drop-off. This includes information on the number of individuals who only visited the information page and did not continue, the page at which the highest number of dropouts occurred, and the exclusions that determined the final sample.
Quantitative results
Effect size: Thank you for your comment regarding the effect size in our ANOVA results (ηp² = 0.07). We would like to clarify that here we are reporting partial eta squared (ηp²) as an indicator of effect size in the ANOVA results, which, following Cohen’s guidelines, indicates a medium effect size ( ~0.01 = small, ~0.06 = medium, ~0.14 = large)
Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical power analysis for the behavioral sciences. Routledge.
Presentation of RQ1 results: We agree that the previous text did not clearly demonstrate the size and direction of the differences or link the ANOVA results to the descriptive statistics. We have therefore restructured the RQ1 results section:
We now clarify that two ANOVAs were conducted: one to analyse the general perceived importance of music across relationship stages, and another to analyse its perceived relevance to intimacy, passion and commitment across stages.
Descriptive statistics are presented immediately after the respective ANOVAs to link the means directly to the statistical tests.
We now use consistent terminology throughout the paper to distinguish between “perceived general importance of music” and “perceived relevance of music to intimacy, passion, and commitment".
Post-hoc comparisons now include t-values and Cohen's d to indicate the magnitude and direction of differences.
Mean value of 0 for musical expertise: We recognise that the previous explanation in the Methods section may not have been entirely clear, so we have added more information in both the Methods section and the note in Table 2. Musical expertise was calculated as a composite score based on four internally consistent items: musical status (Ollen, 2006), whether participants played an instrument or sang, years of playing/singing experience, and weekly practice hours. As these items were z-transformed prior to computing the composite score, the resulting mean is 0.
Qualitative results
Frequentist analysis of qualitative data: It was not entirely clear whether your comment referred to the frequencies reported in the manuscript for the first-order codes or the second-order themes and aggregate dimensions. That said, while content-focused qualitative analysis methods such as template analysis lend themselves well to quantitative exploration procedures such as frequency reports of first-order codes (see Auer-Srnka & Koeszegi, 2007), we agree that it is perhaps less common to see frequency reports for second-order themes. We decided to include frequency reports for both due to the large number of first-order codes included in the template (55 first-order codes) many of which occurred in less than five participant responses to the three open-ended questions across the relationship stages. We have added a brief sentence in the qualitative analysis subsection of methods section of the manuscript justifying our choice to include frequencies for the aggregate dimensions, second-order themes, as well as the first-order codes.
Auer-Srnka, K. J., & Koeszegi, S. (2007). From words to numbers: How to transform qualitative data into meaningful quantitative results. Schmalenbach Business Review, 59, 29-57.
https://ssrn.com/abstract=960677
We also appreciate your suggestion to include detailed descriptions of the qualitative themes. These descriptions can be found in the extended data (Table S1 in the supplementary materials on OSF). Previously, this table was only referenced in the Methods section. For clarity, we now reference it in the Qualitative Results section too. Due to its length (three pages), we have kept the full table in the supplementary materials rather than including it in the main text.
Discussion
In response to your comment, we have revised the opening lines of the Discussion section to align them more closely with the main quantitative findings. This involved adding more detail to the Results section, as described in our earlier response, and explicitly interpreting the effect sizes in the Discussion section to make the link between the summary and the results clearer.
Regarding the correlations between the BMRQ, musical expertise and the perceived importance of music, we now explicitly state that these correlations were of a small to moderate size. Although anything more than a medium effect size is rare in social psychology, there is an important distinction to be made between musical expertise and musical reward in our results. This distinction has been expanded upon in the discussion.
Limitations
We agree that the depth of information provided by participants in the three open-ended questions was somewhat limited. To make this clearer for readers, we have explicitly stated that only three open-ended questions were asked, and we have added the range and average word count of participant responses to both the Qualitative Results and Limitations sections. We also note that future studies could benefit from asking more precisely worded open-ended questions or using additional methods, such as semi-structured interviews, to gain deeper insights into participants’ lived experiences.
In terms of musical expertise, we described the sample as diverse because 57% of participants were non-musicians, while 43% were amateur or professional musicians. For details of the mean value of 0 for expertise shown in Table 2, please refer to our previous response.
