Emotional competence relating to perceived stress and
burnout in Spanish teachers: a mediator model
Lourdes Rey, Natalio Extremera, Mario Pena

This study examined direct associations between emotional competence, perceived stress
and burnout in 489 Spanish teachers. In addition, a model in which perceived stress
mediated pathways linking emotional competence to teacher burnout symptoms was also
examined. Results showed that emotional competence and stress were significantly
correlated with teacher burnout symptoms in the expected direction. Moreover,
mediational analysis indicated that perceived stress partly mediated the relationship
between emotional competence and the three dimensions of burnout even when
controlling for salient background characteristics. These findings suggest an underlying
process by which high emotional competence may increase the capacity to cope with
symptoms of burnout, by reducing the experience of stress. Implications of these findings
for future research and for working with teachers to prevent burnout are discussed.

Introduction

39

Since the introduction of the concept of burnout by Freudenberger (1976), this syndrome is

40

becoming an important topic in psychological and educational research (Abel & Sewell, 1999;

41

Kyriacou, 2001; Maslach, Schaufeli & Leiter, 2001). An expanding body of research examining

42

the relationship between teaching stressful situations and burnout have found that teachers tend

43

to report higher levels of occupational-related stress and burnout than many other human service

44

professionals (Vandenberghe & Huberman, 1999). According to Maslach and Jackson (1986),

45

burnout syndrome is characterized by three primary dimensions: (1) feelings of emotional

46

exhaustion and being emotionally drained by intense contact with students, (2) depersonalization

47

or negative, cynical attitudes toward students, and (3) a sense of lack of personal

48

accomplishment or of ineffectiveness and low competence in one’s work. This syndrome has

49

been repeatedly described as being the result of a significant accumulation of chronic work-

50

related stress (Maslach, 2003). In identifying important sources of teacher burnout, Travers &

51

Cooper (1996) included a wide diversity of stressors such as students’ misbehaviours and

52

discipline problems, students’ poor motivation for work, role conflict and role ambiguity, and

53

pressure and criticisms from parents, among others. All these factors have been associated with

54

the development and maintenance of teacher burnout (Evers, Tomic, & Brouwers, 2004;

55

Hastings & Bham, 2003; Vandenberghe & Huberman, 1999). Besides, the high levels of stress

56

and burnout experienced by teachers and costly mental health-related problems have also been

57

found to contribute to the growing retention problems within the school setting (Kyriacou, 2001).

58

However, teaching professionals do show marked individual differences in their responses to

59

different teaching stressors, ranging from minor psychological symptoms and mild anxiety to

60

burnout as the more severe negative affective experience (Dunham, 1992). Individual-differences

61

constructs are believed to increase the likelihood that conditions related to the stressful situation

62

will be effectively addressed or resolved and higher levels of such skills will lead to lower

63

reported burnout (Langelaan, Bakker, Van Doornen, & Schaufeli, 2006). Consistent with this

64

view, researchers in the field of individual differences have shown interest in examining

65

mechanisms that might explain what individual factors predispose teachers to burnout (Cano-

66

García, Padilla-Muñoz, & Carrasco-Ortiz, 2005; Chan, 2003; Friedman, 2003).

67

One dimension that has gained growing attention in the field of individual differences as a

68

person-related psychological resource is the people ability to deal effectively with affective

69

information, often referred to as emotional competence (or emotional intelligence) (Mayer,

70

Salovey, & Caruso, 2004; Ciarrochi & Scott, 2006). Some authors prefer the use of the more

71

generic and neutral “emotional competence” term when are used self-report measures similar to

72

those used in this study (Ciarrochi & Scott, 2006; Giardini & Frese, 2006). Emotional

73

competence (EC) is regarded here as an affect-related skill, which refers to the ability to

74

understand, manage, and express the social and emotional aspects of life in ways that enable one

75

to coping with stressful and emotionally laden situations (Ciarrochi & Scott, 2006; Kotsou,

76

Nelis, Grégoire, & Mikolajczak, 2011). In recent years, studies and theories have emphasized

77

that EC has an impact on adaptive functioning at school (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), even in

78

the prediction of prosocial classsroom climate and teacher burnout (Chan, 2006; Fleming & Bay,

79

2004; Platsidou, 2010).

80

Several authors have proposed that EC is a personal coping resource that allows teachers to

81

manage and cope successfully with external and internal demands in a stressful workplace

82

(Brackett & Katulak, 2006; Jennings & Greenberg, 2009). According to Salovey and colbs

83

(1999), it is believed that emotionally competent individuals are better adjusted than their less

84

skilled counterparts due to the former having access to their own feelings and being able to

85

manage their negative states even when emotionally aroused by challenging situations (Salovey,

86

Bedell, Detweiler, & Mayer, 1999). Likewise, Chan (2006) suggested that the integrated

87

operation of these emotional competencies might render teachers less vulnerable to teacher

88

burnout, since they might gain better access to the healthy information and action tendency

89

within emotions, and use the information to make sense of their reactions to stressors as well as

90

to guide adaptive action. In fact, EC has been considered to be particularly relevant to work

91

behaviour in teachers because abilities to manage relationships and to show interpersonal

92

sensibility when needed are the prerequisites for improving increasing classroom management

93

(Sutton, Mudrey-Camino, & Knight, 2009). Consistent with this assumption, accumulating

94

empirical research has shown that EC might explain unique variance of both attitudinal and

95

behavioural work outcomes (Wong & Law, 2002; Giardini & Frese, 2006). Having high EC is

96

one of these personal resources that might act as a protector factor, reducing the effects of

97

teacher work strain (Brackett, Palomera, Mojsa-Kaja, Regina, & Salovey, 2010; Chan, 2006;

98

Jennings & Greenberg, 2009)

99

Although EC appears to be negatively related to burnout, the mechanisms by which EC affects

100

burnout are not clear. Zeidner (2005) have listed several underlying mechanisms which might

101

play a mediating role in the EC–occupational-related stress link, suggesting the levels of

102

perceived stress as an underlying process. Given the emerging evidence of a link between EC

103

and burnout (Brackett et al., 2010; Jennings & Greenberg, 2009; Platsidou, 2010), and the well-

104

established connections between high perceived stress and burnout (Burke, Greenglass &

105

Schwarzer, 1996; Maslach, 2003; Rudow, 1999), it is probable that part of the influence of EC

106

on burnout occurs through the mediating effect of perceived stress. Although these factors are

107

reported to be correlated with each other in the literature, however, such a proposition has not

108

been empirically evaluated. Also, unclear is the relative strength of the direct and indirect

109

relations between EC and burnout. Therefore, there are some reasons to believe that EC may

110

show influence the risk of burnout symptoms through perceived stress. Stress is conceptualized

111

as an experience resultant from an imbalance between demands and resources or as taking place

112

when pressure exceeds one’s perceived ability to cope (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Overall, of

113

relevance to the current study, results of previous research suggest that teachers confused about

114

their emotional knowledge abilities and with no confidence in their own emotional regulation

115

abilities are unlikely to feel in control of stressful situations, showing generally higher perceived

116

stress (Brackett et al., 2010; Jennings & Greenberg, 2009). When these individuals maintain

117

higher stress levels, there is the potential for them to increase the risk of becoming burned out on

118

the job. On contrary, it is tentative to think that teachers who are high on emotional competence

119

would also experience as though they have more control over their stressful environment because

120

they can confer sense and manage their negative moods more adaptively (Salovey et al., 1999).

121

In fact, several researchers have found evidence that EC are negatively associated with

122

experience of stress (Gohm, Corser, & Dalsky, 2005; Mikolajczak, Menil, & Luminet, 2007;

123

Salovey, Stroud, Woolery, & Epel, 2002), supporting its utility as an individual characteristic

124

that helps shape subjective interpretations of stressful daily events. These positive effects of EC

125

on perceived stress may result in reduced levels of burnout. Likewise, EC might influence the

126

manner in which a teacher views and reacts to stressful events and experience of stress which, in

127

turn, may reduce levels of burnout symptoms. Hence, the assessment of perceived stress as a

128

potential mediator of the link between EC and teacher burnout symptoms seems to be warranted.

Additionally, a variety of socio-demographic factors has been associated with symptoms of

130

burnout, including age, sex and grade level taught (Byrne, 1999). In order to ensure that our

131

findings would not be confounded with these predictive dimensions, respondents´s age, gender,

132

and grade level taught were used as control variables in our proposed mediation model of

133

perceived stress on the link between EC and burnout.

134

In summary, a large body of literature deals with the prediction of different indicators of teacher

135

burnout. One of this predictor may be the way in which people identify and manage one’s own

136

feelings and those of others. However, rather little is known about the mechanisms that interlink

137

EC, perceived stress and burnout in teachers. Beyond the mere associations among these

138

dimensions, this study will examine whether there is a mediation effect to gain more insight into

139

possible mechanisms of the development of teacher burnout and of protective resource factors.

140

Although the theoretical assumption of mediation of perceived stress on the interplay between

141

EC and burnout has been proposed by authors (Zeidner, 2005), one finds hardly any formal tests

142

of mediation in the available literature. One valuable exception is the preliminary work of Durán,

143

Extremera and Rey (2009) who examined the relationship of perceived emotional intelligence,

144

stress, and burnout in a sample of high school teachers using the TMMS (i.e. Trait Meta-Mood

145

Scale) to predict teacher burnout and the mediating role of stress. In short, they found partial

146

support for the idea that perceived stress mediated the influence of TMMS dimensions on

147

specific subscales of teacher burnout. However, as authors have pointed out, TMMS only

148

evaluates intrapersonal emotional process, but this does not preclude the importance of

149

interpersonal dimensions not measured by TMMS. Besides, other important socio-demographic

150

dimensions such as sex, age and grade taught level, which are related to stress and teacher

151

burnout, were not controlled for. Finally, the sample was composed of only high school teachers

153

Mediation analysis

226

According to the general guidelines offered by Baron and Kenny (1986) for establishing

227

evidence for a proposed mediation model, four conditions need to be satisfied. First, the

228

predictor (EC) needs to be related to the outcome (burnout subscales). Second, the predictor must

229

be related to the hypothesized mediator (perceived stress). Third, the mediator must be related to

230

the outcome when controlling for the predictor. Fourth, the effect of the predictor on the outcome

231

must be reduced when the effect of the mediator is controlled. As recommended by Preacher and

232

Hayes (2008), bootstrap analysis, a non-parametric sampling procedure, was used to test the

233

significance of the indirect effects. Bootstrapping is an approach that resamples the original

234

sample size from the data multiple times and does not rely on the assumption that data are

235

normally distributed. Current analyses utilized 5000 bootstrap resamples to generate 95%

236

confidence intervals. The indirect effect is significant at p < .05 if zero is not included in the 95%

237

confidence interval for that indirect effect. We conducted a set of path analyses to test separately

238

the potential role of perceived stress as a mediator between EC and each burnout subscale

239

including sex, age, and grade taught level as covariates in the analyses.

Discussion

287

This study expands on previous research literature on EC and teacher burnout (Chan, 2006;

288

Jennings & Greenberg, 2009; Platsidou, 2010) by examining direct and indirect relations

289

between EC, perceived stress and burnout in a sample of teachers. Our underlying reasoning was

290

that EC may work through to the reduction of perceived stress, which in turn would promote

291

lower levels of teacher burnout independently of sex, age and grade taught level.

292

There is accumulating empirical evidence that deficits in emotional abilities are directly related

293

to high levels of burnout (Chan, 2006; Durán et al., 2009; Platsidou, 2010). Consistent with these

294

findings, our research did provide evidence that EC was correlated with both perceived stress

295

and burnout symptoms. Specifically, teachers who report being poor emotionally at perceiving

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:01:8720:1:1:NEW 5 Apr 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
296

and managing emotions also tend to report feeling exhausted more often and being more cynical

297

about their work and about students whom they work with, and a more reduced sense of personal

298

accomplishment. One explanation for these findings is that teaching professionals’ skills at

299

effectively managing emotional challenges are characterized by more constructive thought

300

patterns, and teachers find it easier to catch and to identify faulty appraisals and correct

301

maladapted construals in the workplace (Zeidner, 2005). In addition, it is possible that teachers

302

who are more emotionally competent would also feel that they have more control over their

303

stressful environment because they can confer sense and manage their negative moods associated

304

with stress more adaptively.

305

Similarly, in line with earlier studies, our correlational data found that greater perceived stress

306

scores were related in the expected direction to higher levels of burnout symptoms (Maslach,

307

2003). Specifically, greater perceived stress was significantly associated with greater emotional

308

exhaustion and depersonalization and lower feelings of personal accomplishment. The present

309

study found some preliminary support for the idea that perceived stress mediated the influence of

310

EC on specific subscales of teacher burnout, suggesting a complex process. The mediation

311

analyses revealed that perceived stress partially mediated the relations between EC and all

312

burnout symptoms. The results of this study might point to one plausible explanation for the

313

association between EC and burnout as being related to the reduced perceptions of stress

314

experienced by teachers with high EC. In general, increasing EC in teachers may reduce the risk

315

of job burnout both directly and indirectly by reducing the experience of stress. Emotionally

316

competent teachers may reduce be better equipped to engage with and successfully manage

317

stressful school situations, which in turn would lead to lower symptoms of emotional

318

exhaustation and depersonalization and higher personal accomplishment. More research is

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:01:8720:1:1:NEW 5 Apr 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
319

needed to examine the underlying mechanism by which teachers with high EC reduce their levels

320

of stress, either identifying and avoiding potentially stressful contexts or interpreting stressful

321

conditions at work in a less stressful way.

322

Nevertheless, these findings may be valuable not only for developing theoretical models

323

and for understanding better the link between EC and occupational stress (Zeidner, 2005), but

324

also for developing more effective stress intervention programmes for teachers (Brackett &

325

Katulak, 2006). Based on our results, it is suggested that EC might play an important role in the

326

process of certain responses to job stress in teachers. In terms of intervention implications,

327

teachers could be taught how to employ specific emotional strategies for managing stress; this in

328

turn might help them to reduce their feelings related to teacher burnout. In this sense, our data

329

support the idea that programmes to prevent burnout involving the enhancement of EC might be

330

useful to facilitate an increased perception of self-appraisal in teaching work. Besides, whereas

331

therapeutic efforts to increase EC may reduce symptoms burnout, perceived stress appears to be

332

an important aspect of burnout in teachers, so it is also of great importance to help teachers who

333

are vulnerable for burnout to provide specific strategies for reducing negative moods provoked

334

by stress. Cognitive behavioral therapy has been shown to be an effective way of reducing work-

335

related stress, even more than the other intervention types (Van der Klink, Blonk, Schene, & van

336

Dijk, 2001). Further research will determine whether EC interventions, either alone or in

337

conjunction with other stress management interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapies,

338

will enhance psychological outcomes for the classic symptoms of teacher burnout. Future work

339

should also be addressed to the positive pole of burnout, work engagement (Hakanen, Bakker, &

340

Schaufeli, 2006), to determine when and under what circumstances EC work better for

341

increasing individuals’ positive and fulfilling attitude toward work in teachers.

342

Finally, several limitations of the current study should be mentioned. First, although our data

343

provide preliminary evidence for the mediation model proposed, due to data being collected in a

344

single wave of measurement it is impossible to determine the directionality of any causal

345

relationships between variables. A closer examination of the nature of this relationship should be

346

performed using prospective studies. Second, self-reported measures of EC may not reflect

347

actual emotional ability. To address this issue, further studies might consider the inclusion of

348

other approaches complementary to the use of self-report for measuring EC, such as peer reports

349

or performance measures, which provide valuable information about people’s emotional

350

reactions in the work sphere. Finally, it is important to underline that our participants were based

351

on a purposive sampling and, hence, their representativeness is questionable.

352

Despite these limitations, it appears that EC may have an important place in the educational

353

psychologist´s repertoire of occupational well-being and may suggest possible avenues for

354

improving future stress management programs to assist distressed teachers. Although

355

organizational factors are considered to be one of the prominent causes of burnout (Maslach et

356

al., 2001), our results, in line with prior studies, support the importance of individual differences

357

with regard to EC in the development of teacher burnout. While organization-focused

358

interventions in educational settings are important in preventing burnout, the findings of this

359

study provide some evidences that educational settings should pay more attention to the

360

development of protective personal resources taking into account the perspective of interaction

361

between personal characteristics and contextual variables in teaching (Cano-García et al., 2005;

362
