Emotional competence relating to perceived stress and
burnout in Spanish teachers: a mediator model
Lourdes Rey, Natalio Extremera, Mario Pena

This study examined direct associations between emotional competence, perceived stress
and burnout in 489 Spanish teachers. In addition, a model in which perceived stress
mediated pathways linking emotional competence to teacher burnout symptoms was also
examined. Results showed that emotional competence and stress were significantly
correlated with teacher burnout symptoms in the expected direction. Moreover,
mediational analysis indicated that perceived stress partly mediated the relationship
between emotional competence and the three dimensions of burnout even when
controlling for salient background characteristics. These findings suggest an underlying
process by which high emotional competence may increase the capacity to cope with
symptoms of burnout, by reducing the experience of stress. Implications of these findings
for future research and for working with teachers to prevent burnout are discussed.

Introduction

38

Since the introduction of the concept of burnout by Freudenberger (1976), this syndrome is

39

becoming an important topic in psychological and educational research (Kyriacou, 2001;

40

Maslach, Schaufeli & Leiter, 2001). An expanding body of research examining the relationship

41

between teaching stressful situations and burnout have found that teachers tend to report higher

42

levels of occupational-related stress and burnout than many other human service professionals

43

(Maslach et al., 2001). According to Maslach and Jackson (1986), burnout syndrome is

44

characterized by three primary dimensions: (1) feelings of emotional exhaustion and being

45

emotionally drained by intense contact with students, (2) depersonalization or negative, cynical

46

attitudes toward students, and (3) a sense of lack of personal accomplishment or of

47

ineffectiveness and low competence in one’s work. This syndrome has been repeatedly described

48

as being the result of a significant accumulation of chronic work-related stress (Maslach, 2003).

49

In identifying important sources of teacher burnout, Montgomery & Rupp (2005) included a

50

wide diversity of stressors such as students’ misbehaviours and discipline problems, students’

51

poor motivation for work, role conflict and role ambiguity, and pressure and criticisms from

52

parents, among others. All these factors have been associated with the development and

53

maintenance of teacher burnout (Chang, 2013; Lambert & McCarthy, 2006). Besides, the high

54

levels of stress experienced by teachers and costly mental health-related problems have also been

55

found to contribute to the growing retention problems within the school setting (Sass, Seal, &

56

Martin, 2011).

57

However, teaching professionals do show marked individual differences in their responses to

58

different teaching stressors, ranging from minor psychological symptoms and mild anxiety to

59

burnout as the more severe negative affective experience (Bauer, Stamm, Virnich,Wissing et al.,

60

2006). Individual-differences constructs are believed to increase the likelihood that conditions

61

related to the stressful situation will be effectively addressed or resolved and higher levels of

62

such skills will lead to lower reported burnout (Langelaan, Bakker, Van Doornen, & Schaufeli,

63

2006). Consistent with this view, researchers in the field of individual differences have shown

64

interest in examining mechanisms that might explain what individual factors predispose teachers

65

to burnout (Aloe, Shisler, Norris, Nickerson, & Rinker, 2014; Chang, 2013).

66

One dimension that has gained growing attention in the field of individual differences as a

67

person-related psychological resource is the people ability to deal effectively with affective

68

information, often referred to as emotional competence (or emotional intelligence) (Mayer,

69

Salovey, & Caruso, 2004; Ciarrochi & Scott, 2006). Some authors prefer the use of the more

70

generic and neutral “emotional competence” term when are used self-report measures similar to

71

those used in this study (Ciarrochi & Scott, 2006; Giardini & Frese, 2006). Emotional

72

competence (EC) is regarded here as an affect-related skill, which refers to the ability to

73

understand, manage, and express the social and emotional aspects of life in ways that enable one

74

to coping with stressful and emotionally laden situations (Kotsou, Nelis, Grégoire, &

75

Mikolajczak, 2011). In recent years, studies and theories have emphasized that EC has an impact

76

on adaptive functioning at school (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), even in the prediction of

77

prosocial classsroom climate and teacher burnout (Chan, 2006; Platsidou, 2010).

78

Several authors have proposed that EC is a personal coping resource that allows teachers to

79

manage and cope successfully with external and internal demands in a stressful workplace

80

(Brackett & Katulak, 2006; Jennings & Greenberg, 2009). According to Zeidner (2009), it is

81

believed that emotionally competent individuals are better adjusted than their less skilled

82

counterparts due to the former having access to their own feelings and being able to manage their

83

negative states even when emotionally aroused by challenging situations. Likewise, Chan (2006)

84

suggested that the integrated operation of these emotional competencies might render teachers

85

less vulnerable to teacher burnout, since they might gain better access to the healthy information

86

and action tendency within emotions, and use the information to make sense of their reactions to

87

stressors as well as to guide adaptive action. In fact, EC has been considered to be particularly

88

relevant to work behaviour in teachers because abilities to manage relationships and to show

89

interpersonal sensibility when needed are the prerequisites for improving increasing classroom

90

management (Garner, 2010). Consistent with this assumption, accumulating empirical research

91

has shown that EC might explain unique variance of both attitudinal and behavioural work

92

outcomes (Garner, Moses, & Waajid, 2013; Giardini & Frese, 2006). Having high EC is one of

93

these personal resources that might act as a protector factor, reducing the effects of teacher work

94

strain (Brackett, Palomera, Mojsa-Kaja, Regina, & Salovey, 2010; Jennings, 2011)

95

Although EC appears to be negatively related to burnout, the mechanisms by which EC affects

96

burnout are not clear. Zeidner (2009) have listed several underlying mechanisms which might

97

play a mediating role in the EC–occupational-related stress link, suggesting the levels of

98

perceived stress as an underlying process. Given the emerging evidence of a link between EC

99

and burnout (Brackett et al., 2010; Jennings, 2011; Platsidou, 2010), and the well-established

100

connections between high perceived stress and burnout (Maslach, 2003), it is probable that part

101

of the influence of EC on burnout occurs through the mediating effect of perceived stress.

102

Although these factors are reported to be correlated with each other in the literature, however,

103

such a proposition has not been empirically evaluated. Also, unclear is the relative strength of the

104

direct and indirect relations between EC and burnout. Therefore, there are some reasons to

105

believe that EC may show influence the risk of burnout symptoms through perceived stress.

Stress is conceptualized as an experience resultant from an imbalance between demands and

107

resources or as taking place when pressure exceeds one’s perceived ability to cope (Lazarus,

108

2006). Overall, of relevance to the current study, results of previous research suggest that

109

teachers confused about their emotional knowledge abilities and with no confidence in their own

110

emotional regulation abilities are unlikely to feel in control of stressful situations, showing

111

generally higher perceived stress (Brackett et al., 2010; Jennings, 2011). When these individuals

112

maintain higher stress levels, there is the potential for them to increase the risk of becoming

113

burned out on the job. On contrary, it is tentative to think that teachers who are high on

114

emotional competence would also experience as though they have more control over their

115

stressful environment because they can confer sense and manage their negative moods more

116

adaptively (Zeidner, 2009). In fact, several researchers have found evidence that EC are

117

negatively associated with experience of stress (Collie, Shapka, & Perry, 2012; Garner, 2010),

118

supporting its utility as an individual characteristic that helps shape subjective interpretations of

119

stressful daily events. These positive effects of EC on perceived stress may result in reduced

120

levels of burnout. Likewise, EC might influence the manner in which a teacher views and reacts

121

to stressful events and experience of stress which, in turn, may reduce levels of burnout

122

symptoms. Hence, the assessment of perceived stress as a potential mediator of the link between

123

EC and teacher burnout symptoms seems to be warranted.

124

Additionally, a variety of socio-demographic factors has been associated with symptoms of

125

burnout, including age, sex and grade level taught (Maslach et al., 2001). In order to ensure that

126

our findings would not be confounded with these predictive dimensions, respondents´s age,

127

gender, and grade level taught were used as control variables in our proposed mediation model of

128

perceived stress on the link between EC and burnout.

129

In summary, a large body of literature deals with the prediction of different indicators of teacher

130

burnout. One of this predictor may be the way in which people identify and manage one’s own

131

feelings and those of others. However, rather little is known about the mechanisms that interlink

132

EC, perceived stress and burnout in teachers. Beyond the mere associations among these

133

dimensions, this study will examine whether there is a mediation effect to gain more insight into

134

possible mechanisms of the development of teacher burnout and of protective resource factors.

135

Although the theoretical assumption of mediation of perceived stress on the interplay between

136

EC and burnout has been proposed by authors (Zeidner, 2009), one finds hardly any formal tests

137

of mediation in the available literature. One valuable exception is the preliminary work of Durán,

138

Extremera and Rey (2009) who examined the relationship of perceived emotional intelligence,

139

stress, and burnout in a sample of high school teachers using the TMMS (i.e. Trait Meta-Mood

140

Scale) to predict teacher burnout and the mediating role of stress. In short, they found partial

141

support for the idea that perceived stress mediated the influence of TMMS dimensions on

142

specific subscales of teacher burnout. However, as authors have pointed out, TMMS only

143

evaluates intrapersonal emotional process, but this does not preclude the importance of

144

interpersonal dimensions not measured by TMMS. Besides, other important socio-demographic

145

dimensions such as sex, age and grade taught level, which are related to stress and teacher

146

burnout, were not controlled for. Finally, the sample was composed of only high school teachers

147

which limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized. Future research with a

148

heterogenous sample of grade level teachers, using more comprenhensive EI measures and

149

controlling for classic socio-demographics factors related to teacher burnout is needed to confirm

150

the usefulness and validity of these findings. In light of the above considerations, the purpose of

151

the present study was to examine the role of perceived stress as mediator in the relationship

between EC and teacher burnout. We hypothesized that more emotionally competent teachers

153

would experience less stress, and this reduction in stress might account for their decreased

154

experience of symptoms of teacher burnout, even when controlling for salient background

155

characteristics.

Mediation analysis

229

According to the general guidelines offered by Baron and Kenny (1986) for establishing

230

evidence for a proposed mediation model, four conditions need to be satisfied. First, the

231

predictor (EC) needs to be related to the outcome (burnout subscales). Second, the predictor must

232

be related to the hypothesized mediator (perceived stress). Third, the mediator must be related to

233

the outcome when controlling for the predictor. Fourth, the effect of the predictor on the outcome

234

must be reduced when the effect of the mediator is controlled. As recommended by Preacher and

235

Hayes (2008), bootstrap analysis, a non-parametric sampling procedure, was used to test the

236

significance of the indirect effects. Bootstrapping is an approach that resamples the original

237

sample size from the data multiple times and does not rely on the assumption that data are

238

normally distributed. Current analyses utilized 5000 bootstrap resamples to generate 95%

239

confidence intervals. The indirect effect is significant at p < .05 if zero is not included in the 95%

240

confidence interval for that indirect effect. We conducted a set of path analyses to test separately

241

the potential role of perceived stress as a mediator between EC and each burnout subscale

242

including sex, age, and grade taught level as covariates in the analyses.

Discussion

290

This study expands on previous research literature on EC and teacher burnout (Chan, 2006;

291

Jennings, 2011; Platsidou, 2010) by examining direct and indirect relations between EC,

292

perceived stress and burnout in a sample of teachers. Our underlying reasoning was that EC may

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:01:8720:2:1:CHECK 5 May 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
293

work through to the reduction of perceived stress, which in turn would promote lower levels of

294

teacher burnout independently of sex, age and grade taught level.

295

There is accumulating empirical evidence that deficits in emotional abilities are directly related

296

to high levels of burnout (Durán et al., 2009; Platsidou, 2010). Consistent with these findings,

297

our research did provide evidence that EC was correlated with both perceived stress and burnout

298

symptoms. Specifically, teachers who report being poor emotionally at perceiving and managing

299

emotions also tend to report feeling exhausted more often and being more cynical about their

300

work and about students whom they work with, and a more reduced sense of personal

301

accomplishment. One explanation for these findings is that teaching professionals’ skills at

302

effectively managing emotional challenges are characterized by more constructive thought

303

patterns, and teachers find it easier to catch and to identify faulty appraisals and correct

304

maladapted construals in the workplace (Zeidner, 2009). In addition, it is possible that teachers

305

who are more emotionally competent would also feel that they have more control over their

306

stressful environment because they can confer sense and manage their negative moods associated

307

with stress more adaptively.

308

Similarly, in line with earlier studies, our correlational data found that greater perceived stress

309

scores were related in the expected direction to higher levels of burnout symptoms (Maslach,

310

2003). Specifically, greater perceived stress was significantly associated with greater emotional

311

exhaustion and depersonalization and lower feelings of personal accomplishment. The present

312

study found some preliminary support for the idea that perceived stress mediated the influence of

313

EC on specific subscales of teacher burnout, suggesting a complex process. The mediation

314

analyses revealed that perceived stress partially mediated the relations between EC and all

315

burnout symptoms. The results of this study might point to one plausible explanation for the

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:01:8720:2:1:CHECK 5 May 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
316

association between EC and burnout as being related to the reduced perceptions of stress

317

experienced by teachers with high EC. In general, increasing EC in teachers may reduce the risk

318

of job burnout both directly and indirectly by reducing the experience of stress. Emotionally

319

competent teachers may reduce be better equipped to engage with and successfully manage

320

stressful school situations, which in turn would lead to lower symptoms of emotional

321

exhaustation and depersonalization and higher personal accomplishment. More research is

322

needed to examine the underlying mechanism by which teachers with high EC reduce their levels

323

of stress, either identifying and avoiding potentially stressful contexts or interpreting stressful

324

conditions at work in a less stressful way.

325

Nevertheless, these findings may be valuable not only for developing theoretical models

326

and for understanding better the link between EC and occupational stress (Zeidner, 2009), but

327

also for developing more effective stress intervention programmes for teachers (Chang, 2013).

328

Based on our results, it is suggested that EC might play an important role in the process of

329

certain responses to job stress in teachers. In terms of intervention implications, teachers could

330

be taught how to employ specific emotional strategies for managing stress; this in turn might

331

help them to reduce their feelings related to teacher burnout. In this sense, our data support the

332

idea that programmes to prevent burnout involving the enhancement of EC might be useful to

333

facilitate an increased perception of self-appraisal in teaching work. Besides, whereas therapeutic

334

efforts to increase EC may reduce symptoms burnout, perceived stress appears to be an

335

important aspect of burnout in teachers, so it is also of great importance to help teachers who are

336

vulnerable for burnout to provide specific strategies for reducing negative moods provoked by

337

stress. Cognitive behavioral therapy has been shown to be an effective way of reducing work-

338

related stress, even more than the other intervention types (Van der Klink, Blonk, Schene, & van

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:01:8720:2:1:CHECK 5 May 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
339

Dijk, 2001). Further research will determine whether EC interventions, either alone or in

340

conjunction with other stress management interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapies,

341

will enhance psychological outcomes for the classic symptoms of teacher burnout (Farber, 2000).

342

Future work should also be addressed to the positive pole of burnout, work engagement (Bakker,

343

2011), to determine when and under what circumstances EC work better for increasing

344

individuals’ positive and fulfilling attitude toward work in teachers.

345

Finally, several limitations of the current study should be mentioned. First, although our data

346

provide preliminary evidence for the mediation model proposed, due to data being collected in a

347

single wave of measurement it is impossible to determine the directionality of any causal

348

relationships between variables. A closer examination of the nature of this relationship should be

349

performed using prospective studies. Second, self-reported measures of EC may not reflect

350

actual emotional ability. To address this issue, further studies might consider the inclusion of

351

other approaches complementary to the use of self-report for measuring EC, such as peer reports

352

or performance measures, which provide valuable information about people’s emotional

353

reactions in the work sphere. Finally, it is important to underline that our participants were based

354

on a purposive sampling and, hence, their representativeness is questionable.

355

Despite these limitations, it appears that EC may have an important place in the educational

356

psychologist´s repertoire of occupational well-being and may suggest possible avenues for

357

improving future stress management programs to assist distressed teachers. Although school

358

climate factors are considered to be one of the prominent causes of teacher burnout (Aloe et al.,

359

2014; Grayson & Alvarez, 2008), our results, in line with prior studies, support the importance of

360

individual differences with regard to EC in the development of teacher burnout. While

361

organization-focused interventions in educational settings are important in preventing burnout,

attention to the development of protective personal resources taking into account the perspective

364

of interaction between personal characteristics and contextual variables in teaching (Aloe et al.,

365

2014; Chang, 2013).

366

Finally, as high stress levels and poor EC continually rank as the most prominent causes why

367

teachers become dissatisfied with the profession and end up leaving their positions (Chang,

368

2009), paying more attention to the development of these personal resources might help teachers

369

employ EC in their professional and personal relationships and contribute to greater positivity at

370

work (Garner, 2010; Jennings, 2011). Future research could examine this issue by training

371

people in EC and observing how such training impacts on teacher burnout symptoms over time.

372

Elucidating which protective resource factors contribute most to a better subjective well-being at

373

work, and how to develop these aspects, is a promising approach to the enhancement of teaching

374

skills and merits serious attention.
