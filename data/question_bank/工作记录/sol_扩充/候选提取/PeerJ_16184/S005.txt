Background: The incidence and aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic brought about a

drastic change in health professional education around the world. Traditional classrooms
made way for online classrooms in order to ensure that learning continued in a safe and
secure environment. However, how well health professional students perceived and

accepted these changes have not been fully gauged yet. Therefore, this study aims to
evaluate the perception of health professional students about their new educational
climate. Methods: A modiﬁed and validated Dundee Ready Education Environment

Measure (DREEM) questionnaire was used to collect data regarding student perception of
their educational environment. Results: The mean DREEM scores for three time periods

were in the accepted positive range of 101 to 150 indicating that most of the students
perceived the changes positively. The results indicated that most students preferred

blended learning over online learning or face-to-face learning alone. Areas where students
were unsatisﬁed with their learning environment that need improvement were identiﬁed
by poor item-wise scores. Conclusion: Strategic remedial measures for these concerns
need to be developed to improve the quality of education received by the students.

However, the results of our study indicated that most of the students were able to adapt
positively to the new education environment due to the change in the circumstances
during COVID.

INTRODUCTION

51

Health professional education, over the years, has relied on traditional teaching methods and

52

physical classrooms to impart the necessary knowledge and skill sets to future health professional

53

students. Nevertheless, educational institutions all around the world were forced to use e-learning

54

platforms when COVID-19 was declared a pandemic to support students in continuing their studies

55

(Kaul et al. 2021). This inevitable and sudden transformative change tested the flexibility and

56

adaptability of teachers, students, and institutions alike. The pandemic opened several

57

opportunities for the improvement of how medical knowledge is delivered to future health

58

profession students. It served as a platform for the evolution of medical education (Kaul et al.

59

2021).

60
61

A primary challenge brought about by this change in the field of health professional education

62

was the transition from traditional classrooms to online learning techniques such as live meetings

63

and pre-recorded lectures. While educators were expected to impart the same quality and quantity

64

of knowledge as before to students through electronic means, students were expected to

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2023:05:86602:1:1:NEW 3 Aug 2023)

Manuscript to be reviewed
65

understand, conform, and exhibit the expected growth in their skill sets. This posed a variety of

66

problems (Chaudhary et al. 2022; Nimavat et al. 2021). E-learning required students to be more

67

independent, and self-reliant and exhibit a certain amount of self-discipline to keep pace with the

68

curriculum. Further, for health professional students the absence of clinical experience during the

69

lockdown period was a major source of concern (Azlan et al. 2020) along with feelings of

70

loneliness and depression due to isolation (Mujtaba et al. 2023; Tahir et al. 2022). Virtual

71

classrooms limit teacher-student interaction which is an essential element in a health professional

72

education environment. More importantly, students have been discouraged by the reduction in the

73

practical and clinical aspects of learning during the COVID period (Abbasi et al. 2020; Javed et

74

al. 2021). With institutions suspending regular classes, health profession students were at the risk

75

of receiving less than an adequate amount of exposure to all the necessary spheres of knowledge

76

and skills. This stood to weaken their performance both in exams and their ability to become skilled

77

health professional students (Ahmed et al. 2020). To overcome this problem, simulation-based

78

learning has been recommended to counter the absence of direct clinical experience during the

79

pandemic (Abdulrahman et al. 2022).

80
81

Online learning had several distinct advantages too. It introduced flexibility into the students’

82

study routine, providing them with the opportunity to learn according to their convenience. It made

83

way for a student-oriented learning process which permitted them to study at their own pace.

84

Students also showed a preference for recorded lectures over live classes, as they can be repeatedly

85

listened to by students (Kim et al. 2020). Safety during the pandemic, cost and time conservation,

86

and convenience have all been cited to be advantages of online education (Hussein et al. 2020).

87

88

The need for collecting feedback from both teachers and students is essential to understand the

89

type of changes and improvements needed for better delivering the curriculum via e-learning

90

(Nimavat et al. 2021). Moving forward, the future of medical education depends on how well the

91

students, educators, and institutions adapt to the changes. Medical education cannot be restricted

92

to traditional learning methods as online methods be as effective as physical classrooms (Kim et

93

al. 2020). The pandemic has created an opportunity to create a flexible but competent learning

94

environment for students (Nimavat et al. 2021). Currently, the blended learning technique is

95

widely considered as it encompasses both synchronous and asynchronous learning strategies. This

96

will encourage students to be more involved in the process of their education (Lapitan Jr et al.

97

2021). The student’s perception of the learning environment has been assessed globally,

98

regionally, and to some extent locally (Al-Naggar et al. 2014; Bavdekar et al. 2019; Palomo-López

99

et al. 2018). However, research papers are scarce about the comparison between the old and new

100

normal COVID-19 learning environments in health educational institutes, especially its

101

associations with perceptions based on gender and year of study, and academic achievement. An

102

insight may help to analyze whether the new learning environment in which students’ learning

103

took place had been satisfying to them or not. As the educational environment strongly affects

104

students’ achievements, satisfaction, and success, it was important to gather feedback from

105

students regarding their experience in the learning environment. Understanding the changes in

106

perceptions of the learning environment related to student characteristics and year of training may

107

help to give insight and help to formulate interventions that may facilitate positive experiences in

108

education. Therefore, the importance of this study was to assess students’ perceptions of the

109

educational environment during Pre- COVID, during COVID, and Post-COVID and to study how

110

it affected their academic levels and achievements. Therefore, this study aimed to compare

MATERIAL & METHODS

117

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at Northern Border University (NBU), Arar,

118

Saudi Arabia among the health profession students from the colleges of medicine, applied science,

119

and nursing. The ethical approval for the study was obtained from the local committee of bioethics,

120

Northern Border University (Ref: MED-2022-11-1371), and written consent was taken from all

121

the recruited students after explaining to them the purpose of the study. The students were selected

122

using a random sampling method. Students who enrolled in courses in the University after the start

123

of the pandemic and students who passed out from their respective colleges before normal classes

124

resumed after the pandemic were excluded from the study. And only those students were

125

approached for this study, who were enrolled in the different courses of the University during pre

126

COVID era and had taken courses pre-COVID, during COVID, and post-COVID era. Dundee

127

Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) was employed to collect the necessary data.

128

The perception of the students of their educational environment was analyzed before, during, and

129

after COVID-19. DREEM analysis is a widely validated method to analyze educational

130

environments, particularly in Health professional educational institutions (Al-Ahmari et al. 2022;

131

Miles et al. 2012). DREEM analysis was chosen for this study as it provides the possibility of

132

identifying the strengths and weaknesses associated with each institution or country independently

133

and taking focused remedial measures as per individual scores. A modified version of DREEM

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2023:05:86602:1:1:NEW 3 Aug 2023)

Manuscript to be reviewed
134

was used in this study which was verified by an expert medical education team to customize the

135

questions concerning the COVID-19 pandemic. A pilot study of a modified version of DREEM

136

was done among 10 students taken from the health profession colleges of NBU and it showed an

137

adequate level of consistency (Cronbach’s alpha= 0.78). DREEM inventory evaluates student

138

perception under 5 domains constituting a total of 50 items. Items 4, 9, 13,17, 25, 35, 39, 48, and

139

50 are negative statements and hence were reverse scored. The 50 items are scored on a 5-point

140

Likert scale, as follows: Strongly agree – 4.0, Agree – 3.0, Uncertain – 2.0, Disagree -1.0, and

141

strongly disagree - 0. For individual items, a mean score of ≥3.5 is considered a true positive.

142

Items with a mean score of ≤2 indicate problem areas and concerns. A mean score of 2–3 is an

143

item that needs can be bettered for maximum benefit to students. The responses to the

144

questionnaire are used to generate an overall score with a maximum value of 200. Completion of

145

the inventory will be undertaken voluntarily, and data anonymity will be maintained. Items

146

6,8,13,37,41,47 and 50 were modified to reflect the aims of the present study. The five domains

147

of student perception and the maximum possible scores for each domain are,

148

1. Student Perception of Learning (SPL) – 12 items with a maximum score of 48

149

2. Student Perception of Teachers (SPT)- 11 items with a maximum score of 44

150

3. Student’s Academic Self Perception (SASP) – 8 items with a maximum score of 32

151

4. Student’s Perception of Atmosphere (SPA) – 12 items with a maximum score of 48

152

5. Student’s Social Self Perception (SSSP) – 7 items with a maximum score of 28

153
154

The data obtained were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software

155

program (IBM SPSS v.20 Inc., Chicago Il, USA) version 23. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess

156

the internal consistency of the instrument. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for

157

all of the items. For each of the five domains, scores were calculated as the cumulative total of

158

individual responses for all of the items in that domain. The Kruskal-Wallis (one-way analysis of

159

variance) test was used to analyze the difference between the mean scores of the three different

160

groups. The confidence level was put at 0.05 level of significance.

DISCUSSION

191
192

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, students' learning environments had to transition to an online

193

setting, and this had a significant impact on students' subjective happiness. DREEM has long been

194

used to gauge the environment associated with educating medical students. It has been used as an

195

effective tool to identify both strengths and weaknesses in other institutions (Al-Ahmari et al.

196

2022; Miles et al. 2012). In our study too, we utilized DREEM to understand the full impact of the

197

COVID pandemic on health professional students studying in different colleges of Northern

198

Border University in Saudi Arabia. Data analysis revealed that health professional students have

199

adapted well to their new environment, irrespective of the intensity of the changes around them.

200

This is consistent with previous studies conducted in various universities in Sweden, Pakistan, and

201

other countries (Miles et al. 2012; Syed et al. 2021; Vishwanathan et al. 2021).

203

We observed an overall increase in the DREEM scores of the COVID and post-COVID timeline

204

in comparison to the pre-pandemic period (Figure 1). Though all three timelines reported positive

205

scoring, this observed increase in DREEM scores during and after the pandemic can be attributed

206

to the flexibility introduced into the otherwise tight schedule of health professional students with

207

the introduction of online learning (Lin et al. 2021). This increase was in line with other similar

208

studies in which health professional students aligned with the blended learning principle. Effective

209

time management, where students may choose their own learning pace, was one of the reported

210

advantages of online learning (Vishwanathan et al. 2021). Students felt virtual classrooms to be

211

more relaxed than physical classrooms. However, the overall positive score does not fully reveal

212

the true picture. Several items that received ratings of less than 2 were reported as areas of concern.

213

These areas need to be paid special attention for improvement.

214
215

Students' perception of learning (SPL) showed an increase in scores during and after a pandemic.

216

In the pre-COVID period, students reported being too tired to enjoy learning (Table 1). However,

217

the score improved during COVID when e-learning was introduced. Another area of concern in

218

this domain was the authoritarian nature of teachers. This has been reported in other studies as well

219

(Vishwanathan et al. 2021). Nimavat et al recommended adopting both the synchronous mode

220

(Live classroom, virtual lab, etc.) which will allow students to immediately interact with their peers

221

and the educator during live online sessions, and the asynchronous mode in which students can

222

think through issues later with their classmates via the internet (chat rooms and discussion forums)

223

(Nimavat et al. 2021).

The only domain to report a reduction in scores during the pandemic and post-pandemic timelines

225

was Student Perception of Teachers (SPT). The data obtained highlighted faculty-centered

226

teaching, lack of effective communication skills of the faculty teaching, and the lack of social life

227

as areas of concern (Table 1). During the pandemic and in its aftermath, the social lives of students

228

were severely hindered. Students reported feeling lonely and depressed. Anxiety and frustration

229

due to isolation and lack of communication affected a significant population of health professional

230

students (Aristovnik et al. 2020; Shahrvini et al. 2021). In our current study too, items 19 and 28

231

scored negatively indicating students’ mental health was impacted by the lockdown and ensuing

232

isolation period.

233
234

The item scores in the Student’s Perception of Atmosphere (SPA) domain indicated the presence

235

of a technological gap among both students and teachers. Though this was amplified during the

236

transition from physical to virtual classrooms. Studies indicated the reluctance of teachers to accept

237

and make themselves familiar with the new technology platforms that were made available to aid

238

virtual learning (Nimavat et al. 2021). As a result, e-learning's effectiveness was somewhat

239

diminished. Due to a lack of technology, poor internet access, and resistance to change on the part

240

of both students and teachers, Abbasi et al.'s study in Pakistan concluded that learners prefer face-

241

to-face instruction (Abbasi et al. 2020). Just using online sessions that encompass only pre-

242

recorded lectures and occasional face-to-face sessions may not be enough to increase student

243

participation. Azlan et al recommended improving the efficiency and scope of online education by

244

considering collaborative educational tools and mediums (Azlan et al. 2020). Abbasi et al, in their

245

study, reported how e-teaching and learning experiences limited the number of interactions

246

between teachers and their students (Abbasi et al. 2020). In congruence with this, the score of item

Student’s Social Self Perception (SSSP) score responses in our study provided some useful

252

265

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the survey gathers responses as students reflect on the

303

initial lockdown session. In this context, survey response can be challenging, especially because

304

students are urged to complete several surveys for each phase which is Pre-COVID, During-

305

COVID, and Post-COVID so some information may have been difficult to recall correctly.

306

Secondly, it is questionable whether the student sample is biased in comparison to the entire cohort.

307

Lastly, this survey was lengthy and contained 50 questions to get a complete picture of how the

308

pandemic affected students' lives. Perhaps the survey response rate would have been higher if the

309

survey had been designed with fewer questions and more concise question-wording.

CONCLUSION

312

Students perceived the changes brought about by the pandemic positively to a good extent,

313

however, there are emerging and evolving connections between the physical and psychosocial

314

learning environments related to the use of new information technologies. In particular,

315

psychosocial aspects may affect how satisfied learners are with learning in these settings. A regular

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2023:05:86602:1:1:NEW 3 Aug 2023)

Manuscript to be reviewed
316

training session is recommended for teachers and students alike to remove the reluctance exhibited

317

by them toward new technologies. As teachers should be aware of students’ preferences regarding

318

the learning environment, and both its advantages and disadvantages. Education providers need to

319

incorporate the benefits of online teaching which replaced onsite teaching due to the COVID

320

pandemic and gave an insight into a hybrid teaching, learning, and assessment approach. Further,

321

health professional educational universities should keep in mind the mental and social health of

322

students while designing curriculum and should establish a support system for vulnerable students.

323

Further studies need to be done in other health educational universities to investigate variables that

324

correlate with SPL, SPT, SASP, SPA, and SPSH and to understand the attitude of students about

325

their environment in the wake of the pandemic.
