Article: Assessing the students’ evaluations of educational quality (SEEQ) questionnaire in Oman higher education
DOI: 10.12688/f1000research.157354.2
Version: 2
Reviewer: Suranto Suranto
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2025-06-16
Report ID: 387744
============================================================

1. The background section of this article has presented the fundamental theories regarding the importance of teaching evaluation and the position of SEEQ as an internationally recognized instrument. The references used are quite strong, including Marsh’s research and several other relevant studies. To strengthen the contextual foundation, the author should consider adding recent studies from countries in the Middle East or other developing nations that have conducted validations of the SET/SEEQ instrument. In addition, the research gap needs to be emphasized more explicitly — for example, by stating that to date, no study has comprehensively tested the structural validity and reliability of the Arabic version of SEEQ using CFA in the context of Oman.
2. The use of a quantitative research method with CFA as the primary analytical tool is appropriate for testing the construct validity and reliability of the instrument. The explanation regarding the sampling procedure and the use of AMOS for analysis is also clear. However, the author should provide more detailed information about the back-translation process, including who the translators were, their qualifications, as well as the mechanism of the preliminary instrument testing (number of participants, implementation procedures, and a brief summary of the results). In addition, it is necessary to explain the reason for adopting all 30 items in the SEEQ without modification, or if any modifications were made, which parts were adjusted. Information about the total student population at Muscat College should also be included so that readers can understand the proportion of the sample relative to the overall population.
3. The presentation of the instrument’s reliability results through Cronbach’s alpha values is very good and demonstrates high internal consistency across most subscales. It would be even better if the author provided the CFA results not only in the form of goodness-of-fit indices but also with a table of factor loadings for each SEEQ item. Additionally, the discussion of the reliability and validity results should be further elaborated by explaining which factors contributed the most or the least, and which factors were most responsive to the Omani context.
4. The discussion has addressed important aspects such as the implications of applying the instrument in different cultural contexts and the challenges of language adaptation. However, the discussion would be stronger if it also included theoretical implications for the development of cross-cultural validity studies of teaching evaluation instruments. In addition, the practical implications for higher education institutions in Oman, particularly regarding teaching quality policies and faculty evaluation systems, should be explained more comprehensively. The study’s limitations should also be described in greater detail — for example, regarding the research subjects being limited to a single institution and the absence of external validity testing.
5. The conclusion has effectively summarized the research findings and stated that the SEEQ is suitable for use in higher education in Oman. However, it would be stronger if it included a recommendation for implementing the SEEQ at the institutional level as a tool for monitoring the quality of teaching and learning. Additionally, suggestions for future research should be made more specific — for example, by recommending invariance testing across semesters, institutions, or academic programs to ensure the stability of the factors in various contexts.
