Article: Transnational patterns, social networks and self-help organizations for migrant women
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.17168.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Irina Diana Madroane
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2025-02-21
Report ID: 49942
============================================================

The article contributes to the research area of transnational migration and gender, by examining comparatively how two women migrants in Italy and Bulgaria, a Congolese asylum seeker and a Ukrainian refugee, respectively, employ social capital and build social networks in the transnational space as a way of achieving (self-)empowerment. The women migrants’ use and development of self-help structures and social inclusion strategies are discussed through the lenses of ethnic mobilization and transnational networking practices, which shape and are shaped by the migrants’ actions. Methodologically, the authors opt for an in-depth study of video interviews with the two women migrants (life stories), carried out as part of the COST Action WEMov. The gender-sensitive, qualitative research approach zooms in on the historical and geographical specificities of transnational migration spaces, viewed as relevant for practice-oriented pedagogy and policymaking in support of women migrants. The research design is sound, and the study is coherent, with minor exceptions. Overall, it succeeds at meeting its objectives, bringing to the fore the benefits and limits of ethnic mobilization in juxtaposition with intercultural and transnational relations building, both articulated in relation to gender.
I suggest a clearer phrasing of the third research question and a clarification of why ethnic mobilization is counterposed to gender in this question (I believe the authors mean “transnationalism and gender”, but the wording of the question could still generate some confusion). In the presentation of findings, ethnic mobilization in the host country is mostly counterposed to transnational networking between sending and receiving countries, so there is a question mark regarding the formulation of the research question. Further, while transnational networking practices are indeed correlated to gender in the article, gender also plays a role in ethnic networking, as mentioned in the text, so there is some lack of clarity there, as well. For clarity purposes, too, and if that is the case, it should be specified that “ethnic mobilization” is confined in the study to the host country (otherwise ethnic networks cut across borders and may be considered to have a transnational dimension, as the Bulgarian case actually reveals). Some of these aspects require further theoretical forays and, especially, a strengthening of the connections among them in relation to the empirical work. The difference between the two strategies seems to lie rather in the type of social capital they require, and the authors could make this more explicit in the theoretical presentation. It is also unclear how the discussion of “the intersections between gender and class in ethnic enclaves” in the theoretical review applies to the empirical study. If it is simply an example of transnational relations, that should be specified.  Further, while it apparent that “bridging” social capital plays an important role in the building of weak ties outside one’s ethnic group, the role of “bonding” social capital remains somewhat backgrounded in the analysis of findings.
