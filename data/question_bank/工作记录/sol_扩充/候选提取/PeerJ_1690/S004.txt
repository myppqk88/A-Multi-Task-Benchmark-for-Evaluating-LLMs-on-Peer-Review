Is type-D personality trait(s) or state? An examination of typeD temporal stability in older Israeli adults in the community
Ada H Zohar

Background: Type D personality was suggested as a marker of poorer prognosis for
patients of cardiovascular disease. It is defined by having a score of 10 or more on both
sub-scales of the DS14 questionnaire, Social Inhibition (SI) and Negative Affectivity (NA).As
Type D was designed to predict risk, its temporal stability is of prime importance. Methods:
Participants in the current study were 285 community volunteers, who completed the
DS14, and other personality scales, at a mean interval of six years. Results: The
prevalence of Type D did not change. The component traits of Type D showed rank order
stability. Type D caseness temporal stability was improved by using the sub-scales product
as a criterion. Logistic hierarchical regression predicting Type D classification from Time1
demonstrated that the best predictors were Time1 scores on NA and SI, with the character
trait of Cooperation, and the alexithymia score adding some predictive power. Conclusions:
The temporal stability of the component traits, and of the prevalence of Type D were
excellent. Temporal stability of Type D caseness may be improved by using a product
threshold, rather than the current rule. Research is required in order to formulate the
optimal timing for Type D measurement for predictive purposes.

Type D, "distressed" personality type is characterized by high negative affectivity,

39

coupled with elevated social inhibition, making the Type D person unable to gain adequate social

40

support for the weight of negative affectivity he or she experiences (Denollet, 2005). Individuals

41

with Type D personality are more likely than others to suffer from social anxiety (Kupper &

42

Denollet, 2014). Measured by a self-report questionnaire (DS14; Denollet, 2005) Type D is

43

classified when respondents score 10 or more on each of the component traits, social inhibition

44

(SI) and negative affectivity (NA). Type D personality has been found to be a potent risk factor

45

for hypertension and for cardiac vascular disease (Strike & Steptoe, 2005). One mechanism

46

putting Type D individuals at risk is thought to be that high levels of chronic stress lead to high

47

concentrations of stress hormones, harming the membranes of blood vessels and allowing the

48

build-up of plaque, which in turn raises blood pressure and makes cardiac events more likely.

49

There is some proof of causality; not only does Type D personality raise the probability of

50

cardiac vascular disease (CVD), but addressing the distress of Type D personality patients after a

51

cardiac event leads to significantly lower mortality and morbidity (Denollet & Brutsaert, 2001).

52

Another possible mechanism is that Type- D individuals may engage in less healthy behavior. A

53

study of patients with heart failure in the United States (Wu & Moser, 2013) found that Type D

54

patients were less likely to adhere to their medication. Indonesian coronary heart patients who

55

were Type D engaged less in health behavior than Non-D patients (Ginting, van de Ven, Becker,

56

& Näring, 2014). A study of Dutch patients attending an outpatient cardiac clinic (Schiffer et al.,

57

2005) showed that Type D personality tripled the risk of heart failure and increased the risk for

58

depressive symptoms more than six-fold. A longitudinal study of over 500 cardiac patients

59

(Denollet, Pedersen, Vrints & Conraads, 2013) found significant odds ratio for Type D cardiac

60

patients to suffer a major cardiac event (MACE), i.e. a myocardial infarction, coronary

61

revasculation, or cardiac death. These effects did not hold when using the component traits of

62

Type D, SI and NA as continuous risk factors and depended on the interaction, i.e. both traits

63

being above a cut-off of 10. A meta-analysis showed Type D to confer additional risk or poorer

64

prognosis for CVD patients (Grande, Romppel, & Barth, 2012). It should be noted that in some

65

studies, depression is found to be a better prognostic predictor than Type D status (e.g. Damen et

66

al., 2013). In others, Type D is not associated with poorer health behavior (Habibović et al.,

67

2014).

68

These findings about Type D show a strong effect, but the question whether or not Type

69

D personality is a discrete entity has yet to be addressed empirically. The features associated

70

with Type D, negative affectivity and social inhibition, can arise from multiple continuous traits

71

with different psychological and biological causes. Moreover, if Type D is a discrete entity, one

72

would expect it to have high temporal stability.

73

Temporal stability has been variously defined. In a large-scale study of healthy Dutch

74

twins, Kupper et al. (2011) showed that Type D itself and both of its component traits had stable

75

genetic influences which did not change over the 9 year study. In trait-personality models,

76

temporal stability is usually reported as the correlation of trait scores at different time-points,

77

often called rank-order stability. This can be applied in the current study to the component traits

78

of Type D, NA and SI. However, this measure misses the essence of Type D, i.e. that it is a

79

dichotomous classification, and thus very different from most current personality models. To

80

measure temporal stability of the Type D dichotomous classification, two additional measures of

81

temporal stability were considered: 1) the prevalence of Type D individuals at both time-points,

82

and 2) The proportion of individuals who were classified as Type D at T1 who still qualified for

83

Type D at T2; and the proportion of T1 non-D individuals who still qualified for Non-D status at

84

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:11:7466:1:1:REVIEW 21 Jan 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
T2. Since Type D is defined by an absolute threshold (a score of 10 or more on both of the

85

subscales), it was also possible to examine the temporal stability of the dichotomous

86

classification, by examining an alternate definition of "Dness": examining the criteria points

87

provided by the product of NA and SI scores for temporal stability of this alternate classification.

88

The current study addresses the question of temporal stability of Type D in a non-clinical

89

sample of Israeli adult community volunteers, measured twice at a mean interval of six years. At

90

outset, 1350 volunteers completed the DS14 (Zohar, Lev-Ari, Denollet and Cloninger, 2011). At

91

that time, 24.1% of the participants were Type D positive, and on average the Type D group

92

differed substantially from the Non-D group: they were more alexithymic, reported poorer

93

subjective health, less social support and lower satisfaction with life. There was also a significant

94

association between being Type D positive and having a known medical diagnosis of CVD or

95

diabetes. Individuals who were Type–D positive were significantly different from the Non-D

96

individuals on six of the seven T emperament and Character (TCI) traits: more harm avoidant,

97

less novelty seeking, less reward dependent, less persistent, less self-directed and cooperative. A

98

mean six years later about a quarter of the original sample were available for re-testing.

99

This study wished to examine the following questions: 1. The temporal stability of Type

100

D prevalence. 2. The rank-order stability of the component traits of Type D, SI and NA. 3. The

101

temporal stability of the Type D classification using the accepted criterion. 4. Examining as

102

threshold points the product of the sub-scale scores of the DS14 to see if they provide more

103

stable classifications than the Type D membership. 5. Using the extensive personality scales used

104

at Time1 and described in detail elsewhere (Zohar, Denollet, Lev-Ari and Cloninger 2011) to add

105

to the prediction of Time2 Type D classification.

METHOD

108

Participants

109

Participants were 285 community volunteers, enrolled in a longitudinal study of

110

personality and health. The baseline sample is described in detail elsewhere (Zohar and

111

Cloninger, 2011). In the current study only those participants who had previously agreed to take

112

part in the longitudinal study, and who were still alive, and who had access to the internet were

113

contacted. This included 471 potential participants. Comparing this subset to the original

114

baseline sample on all personality and demographic variables showed that this subset did not

115

differ from the baseline sample in any of the variables except mean age – this sample was on

116

average about two years younger. Of those 471 contacted, 60.1%, or 285, completed the

117

extensive on-line self-report which is the time 2 (T2) data. Of these, 42.4% were men. The

118

participants' age ranged between 45 and 95, with a mean of 62.2. Their education ranged

119

between partial primary school and Ph.D., with a mean of 15.75 years of education, i.e. college

120

education. Most, 68.4% were married, 19.2% were divorced, and 8.4% were widowed. The final

121

sample was comparable to the baseline sample on all personality measures: the TCI traits, Type

122

D traits, and alexithymia.

127

128

to participate in this phase of the study were mailed a link to an online questionnaire.

Measures

130

DS14 (Denollet, 2005): This 14 item questionnaire includes 7 items which measure

131

negative affectivity (NA) and 7 items which measure social inhibition (SI). Type D personality is

132

confirmed when an individual scores 10 or more on both the subscales. The DS14 was found to

133

perform very well in Hebrew (Zohar, Denollet, Lev Ari, & Cloninger, 2011). It showed

134

structural validity in exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, and convergent and divergent

135

validity against other personality scales, in particular the temperament and character inventory,

136

(TCI ; Zohar & Cloninger, 2011).

137

TCI-140: This version of the Temperament and Character Inventory includes 140 items

138

which are answered on a 5-value Likert-like scale. It measures four temperament traits: harm

139

avoidance (HA); novelty seeking (NS); reward dependence (RD) and persistence (PS). In

140

addition it measures 3 character traits: self-directedness (SD); cooperation (CO) and self-

141

transcendence (ST). The TCI-140 performs very well in Hebrew (Zohar & Cloninger 2011).

142

Toronto Alexithymia Scale-20 (TAS20): Bagby, Parker & Taylor (1994) constructed a 20

143

item 5-point response scale for alexithymia which reduces to 3 subscales, difficulty in identifying

144

feelings, difficulty in describing feelings, and externally-oriented thinking. A total score over 61

145

is considered evidence of alexithymia, and a total score of less than 51 is considered evidence for

146

non-alexithymia (Taylor, Bagby & Parker, 1997).

147

148

All data were entered directly onto SPSS via self-report using Qualtrics. Hypothesis

149

testing was conducted using SPSS21.0 for WINDOWS. Only complete reports were considered

150

in this study.

DISCUSSION

222

The current study found support for temporal stability of Type D using a variety of

223

approaches. It found temporal stability for the prevalence of Type D caseness, so that the

224

prevalence at T1 of the current study sample was 25.3%, not statistically different from the

225

prevalence 6 years later, 21.4%. Jellesma (2008) found the prevalence of Type D unchanged

226

over an 18 month span in Dutch schoolchildren; and Ossola et al. (2015) found the prevalence of

227

Type D unchanged in intensive care coronary patients in Italy over a 12 month span. Thus in a

228

variey of contexts, Type D prevalence is a stable characteristic of populations, and the

229

prevalence itself of about 1 in 4 seems to be very similar in many if not in all cultures. A study of

230

Type D in Korea (Lim et al., 2011) showed a remarkably similar prevalence of Type D in

231

healthy cotrols, patients with hypertension and without CVD, and in CVD patients.

232

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:11:7466:1:1:REVIEW 21 Jan 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
The current study also found rank-order stability for the component traits negative

233

affectivity and social inhibition, 0.73 and 0.82 respectively. These correlations are as high as for

234

many other personality traits measured by self-report, and as high or higher than the rank order

235

stability of NA and SI reported by others e.g. Martens et al. (2007) or Spindler, Kruse, Zwisler

236

and Pedersen (2009).

237

Also in support of the entity of Type D, are the results of the binary logistic regression

238

analysis. The best predictors of Type D classification at Time2 were the Time1 DS14 trait

239

scores. The original trait scores of social inhibition and negative affectivity, each measured by 7

240

items, did better than the seven TCI traits (each measured by 20 items) and better than the

241

alexithymia scale (again 20 items). Thus there is a particular affective and social style measured

242

by the DS14 which is relatively robust, and does better than other more elaborate personality

243

scales in measuring these traits, as well as predicting the Type D criterion, i.e. both sub-scale

244

scores 10 or above, six years later.

245

A different but intuitively obvious measure of temporal stability is caseness: what

246

proportion of those classified as Type D at T1 will still be Type D at T2, and what proportion of

247

Non-D will remain Non-D? Is it enough that the Chi-Square value for the association is

248

significant, or do we expect a higher level of temporal stability? The current study found, that six

249

years later, 56.9% of Type D adult community volunteers remained Type D, and 90.6% of those

250

originally Non-D remained Non-D. Comparing this result to others in the extant literature is not

251

trivial, because the research on the temporal stability of Type D is relatively new, and in those

252

studies published, the definition of temporal stability differs between studies.

253

Some studies of CVD patients, report the percentage of patients who remain Type D at retest. In

254

a Swedish study of CVD patients, over 12 months, 6.1% of individuals were Type D at each of

255

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:11:7466:1:1:REVIEW 21 Jan 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
three consecutive testing times (Condén, Rosenblad, Ekselius, and Åslund, 2014); this very low

256

temporal stability may be due to variation in assessment method at the different time-points. In a

257

study of consecutive patients who were admitted into a coronary intensive care unit during a 3-

258

year window, patients were assessed every month for 12 months (Ossola, Panfilis, Tonna,

259

Ardissino & Marchesi, 2015). They found that among depressed patients, the initial prevalence

260

of Type D was very high, 60%, and over 12 months, 29.2% retained their initial Type D status.

261

Among non-depressed patients, the prevalence of Type D was 30%, and at 12 months from

262

initial testing 31.3% retained their Type D status. Thus although the prevalence of Type D was

263

double in the depressed coronary patients vs. the non-depressed, staying true to type was

264

comparable in both groups ~30%. They found that over the 12 months Negative Affectivity was

265

significantly reduced, while Social Isolation was constant over the 12 months of the study. In a

266

German study of CVD patients Romppel et al., (2012) followed healthy controls, CVD inpatients

267

and outpatients over a 6-year interval. The overall prevalence of Type D in their study was

268

similar in the various groups, and was about 26%. About half the patients with Type D, 14.4% of

269

the patient sample, retained their Type D status. They reported that there was more change in NA

270

than in SI over the six years, which accounted for most of the change in "caseness". Jellesma

271

(2008) studied children 8 to 12 years of age sampled through Dutch schools at an 18 month

272

interval, and found that 42.9% of baseline Type D children remained Type D, and 79.3% of

273

baseline Non-D children remained Non-D. These results fall between the lower stability found in

274

CVD adult patients, who may experience their illness and recovery as transformative, and lower

275

than that found in the current study, in older adults whose personality has developed and

276

stabilized. Other studies report the proportion of temporal stability of Type D classification

277

overall, combining Type D membership and Non-D membership (e.g. Pelle et al., 2008). Since

278

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2015:11:7466:1:1:REVIEW 21 Jan 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
Type D individuals are about 1 in 4 or 1 in 5, the a-priori probability of remaining Type D is

279

much lower than the a-priori probability of remaining Non-D, thus overall temporal stability is

280

not an informative measure for the stability of the at-risk-group Type D.

281

Compared to clinical samples, the current study showed high temporal stability, as it

282

extends over a considerably longer time frame, and as more than half of the individuals

283

originally identified as Type D remained Type D. However, the temporal stability of Type D

284

classification was significantly lower than that achieved for using the product of the subscale

285

scores as a cutoff point. The product of the subscale scores showed a highly significant overlap

286

with the classical Type D classification, as well as having higher temporal stability.

287

Coyne and de Voogd (2012) claimed that Type D classification has not been borne out by

288

empirical research even though the component traits are of obvious importance, and suggest

289

using alternative approaches to scoring the Type Dness, based on the quasi-continuous subscale

290

scores, rather than using the Denollet (2005) rule of 10 or more on both sub-scales.

291

Subsequently, (Denollet, Pedersen, Vrints & Conraad, 2013), conducted a five-year longitudinal

292

study of a series of over 500 CVD patients showed that Type D did better at predicting major

293

cardiac events than did its component traits, making the OR for any major cardiac event 1.74,

294

and for cardiac death 2.35. This impressive study showed very strong "positive" results for Type

295

D's predictive validity, but did not address the question of the temporal stability. Was it the Type

296

D status at the beginning of the study that had this major effect? Was the effect different for

297

those who remained stably Type D throughout the five years of the follow-up? If Type D status

298

is at most 30% temporally stable in CVD patientsat what point does being Type D exert its

299

influence? Should being Type D be construed of as a state of heightened risk, or as a trait of

300

individuals at elevated risk?

The current study contributes to existing research on the temporal stability of Type D by

302

examining a community sample and not a CVD patient sample, by using a battery of well

303

validated personality measures, and by having a relatively long inter-test interval. It suggests an

304

alternative criterion for defining Type D, based on the product of the DS14 subscales, which

305

provides better temporal stability in a non-patient sample. In addition, it should be noted, that in

306

this study the original Type D criterion produces higher temporal stability relative to other

307

published results.

308

The results of this study should be viewed with the study limitations in mind, i.e. they

309

may not generalize well to CVD patients, especially those before and after a traumatic cardiac

310

event or a major intervention. These life-threatening experiences may have a dynamic of their

311

own. The current study has other weaknesses: only 60% of those available for testing at T2

312

completed the evaluation and were included at T2, and the mode of self-report changed from

313

pencil and paper at T1, to an online survey at T2. It should be noted however that the only

314

variable that differed between the subset of the initial sample and the complete baseline sample

315

was age. No personality or other demographic variables were different, and thus it is unlikely

316

that there was selective attrition relevant to the personality.

317

Type D is designed to predict risk, thus its temporal stability is of prime importance, and

318

the question of when it should be measured, or which of subsequent measurements is crucial to

319

understanding the behavior and medical risk of CVD patients, requires further research.
