============================================================

This article formulates and discusses important questions that educators and their managers need to consider when selecting a technological didactical solution for applying electronics or robotics educational tools and approaches into educational process. It also provides an overview of current state of the art regarding the various technological solutions available. It tries to put into comparison a few of the notoriously used solutions highlighting their main strengths. The article would certainly be a recommended read for anyone considering implementation of robotics or electronics technology into their educational process, but it also serves as a useful resource with new ideas for some experienced users in the field.
On the other hand, it certainly would not be the only reading resource recommended in such implementation scenario, since technology is only one (and less important) aspect of the story. Much more important would be WHICH pedagogical goals are going to be approached, WHAT is going to be done with the technology and HOW it is going to be used. Practically all of the technologies mentioned in the article are relevant in one or another educational scenario, and thus it is impossible to generalize over them at this level. Most of the time a disadvantage on one end gives some other advantage on another one, so the selection of the technology is very "task-dependent" issue and should be treated as such. Also, I would try to resist an urge or call for unifying the approaches, that the article suggested at some place, as diversity often helps evolution, creativity, and progress in general. For instance, I do not feel so happy about the current trend of all the educational environments for children have a tendency to look like Scratch. On one hand, yes, the child can move from one solution to another more easily and the learning curve for a new technology is faster. On the other hand, programming as such is quite at the crossroads still, and other approaches than the outdated von-Neumann-based control flow programming model need to be heavily investigated and put into use. For example data-flow programming, which allows natural parallelization while still keeping human-readability of the code, to name just one.
I would also like to contest the positive assessment of the Micro:bit solution and its comparison to Arduino in Table 1. It certainly does not fall into the same price category and the versatility of Arduino is higher in several degrees of magnitude. The physical connector system of Micro:bit is a significant obstacle thus many of the workshops with Micro:bit end up using just a LED matrix display and the gyro - i.e. at a level of projects that could well be done on a computer, or let's say on a mobile phone or a tablet, if you like. However, without the extra cost, maintenance, storage, servicing, and all the other extra hassle with the hardware. Arduino platform IS specifically designed for learners contrary to the suggestion in the Table 1, and it does not require any more prior knowledge than Micro:bit. Ranging Arduino as poor in the category of creative time spending is definitely unfair too. It is just different, works different, and gives different set of experiences, which can sometimes be treated more valuable, because with Arduino, students learn about what happens inside. That matters. What value do you see in a child playing to be a wizzard who can impress others by legendary-kind of experiences: "Hey, LED, you should NOW TURN ON!" ...and then the LED turned on, and all the children remembered that till their last days and stayed happy forever. I know what you mean, but my point is that this comparison mixes uncomparable. They are all valid, very useful, and beautiful technologies made for a different purpose, that's it.
