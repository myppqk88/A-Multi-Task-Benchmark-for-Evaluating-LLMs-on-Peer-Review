Article: Qualitative hypotheses
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.19736.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Stefan Reichmann
Status: not approved
Date: 2025-09-05
Report ID: 58123
============================================================

This is a timely contribution in that it seeks to elucidate the relationship between open research methods, most notably preregistration, and qualitative research (or rather, certain aspects of qualitative research). This is achieved by investigating the role of hypotheses in qualitative research (referred to as qualitative hypotheses).
My main criticism of the paper is this: Given that it seeks to elucidate the role of hypotheses in qualitative research, I would expect a thorough engagement with qualitative methodologies, specifically as the contribution contains a section labelled “Historical Background”. This suggests that while the author is a qualitative researcher (or seems to be by their own account), the paper is actually not written from the point of view of qualitative research, but rather tries to accommodate a rather incomplete picture of working qualitatively with registered reports (which is a tool specific to a rather narrow understanding of research).
In particular, qualitative research needs to be thoroughly demarcated from positive science in that each follows very different methodological assumptions. While positive science is characterized by the separation of subject and object, wanting to achieve reliability, reproducibility, and representativeness. This, it tries to achieve through excluding reactivity, e.g. by constructing standardized methods of data collection, and by analyzing data according to statistical models.
Qualitative research is characterized by intervention (i.e., qualitative researchers are in close contact with their field, they often intervene into their subjects lived experience), process, structuring, and theory reconstruction. In this, qualitative research tries to mobilize the contextual effects between interviewer, respondent, field, and situation, instead of trying to eliminate them (this is, after all, the point of standardized research). Qualitative methods are not standardized by definition (which means, indeed, that “methods” might be a bit of stretch). Contextual effects in positive science correspond to the power effects of qualitative research (domination, silencing, objectification, and normalization). I would like to stress that both models of science are necessary, and both have their strengths and weaknesses.
However, the paper evidently tries to analyze (or even colonize) qualitative research from the vantage point of positive science, without any deeper engagement with the methodological assumptions of either. Indeed, the paper contains scant engagement with the epistemological assumptions of qualitative research. Had this been done, the discussion of “researcher subjectivities” and “hypothetical biases”, which very clearly is a projection from quantitative assumptions, could have been omitted and replaced by engagement with a terminology much closer to the letter of qualitative research, namely “lived experience”. Similar to the people under study, qualitative researchers (and all other researchers for that matter) are defined by their lived experience and need to make this explicit in the research process. In so doing, they will inevitably develop (and refine) their own assumptions about their objects (or rather subjects) of study. However, I don’t think likening this process to hypothesis testing as known from quantitative methods serves to elucidate what is going on.
To make this contribution fly, I think the author needs to 1) take the qualitative research tradition (or rather, traditions) seriously, and reconstruct, based on studying original sources (“classic” studies, methods papers, but also text books, which are in ample supply), just how qualitative researchers have theorized their own approaches, following diverse traditions in philosophy, sociology, anthropology, among others, and how these have translated into distinct methodologies. 2) these texts need to be analyzed for what they have to say on “lived experience”, respectively the role of prior knowledge in the qualitative research process. This can be learned either by studying those aspects of empirical papers that describe how qualitative researchers entered into their field, and/or by studying methodological papers or even textbooks with a view towards this question. I suspect that such an analysis will render the original point of the contribution moot, in that it will reveal how qualitative researchers have always been very keen to explicate, or at least elucidate, their prior assumptions when going into the field. This is especially striking given that the contribution purports to talk about the historical background (presumably, of qualitative research), but effectively only introduces qualitative research from the perspective of quantitative research.
Given these obvious omissions, then, it seems especially odd that the author introduces their work, in the abstract, as follows:
“Hypotheses are not common in qualitative research. This can be largely attributed to many qualitative methods not being optimal or suitable for hypothesis-testing, and the epistemological curiosity in qualitative research often lacking interest in testing. Nonetheless, qualitative researchers, too, do often enter research projects with expectations, which are similar to hypotheses.”
I found this very striking because in most methodological and/or empirical traditions labelled “qualitative” (e.g. ethnomethodology, grounded theory, ethnography, phenomenology, to name but a few), the aim is quite explicitly not to test hypotheses, precisely to preserve maximum openness. I am trying to be understated when I say that in that sense, stating that “qualitative research often lack[s] interest in testing” seems to be a gross misinterpretation – qualitative research is not just a different way of doing effectively the same things as quantitative researchers do, but rather its own methodological tradition with its own epistemology. Indeed, in the section labelled “Use and Functions”, the author confounds a methodological approach (grounded theory) with an empirical method (ethnography). While it is true that both are “traditions” in a relatively broad sense, the phrase obstructs a deeper understanding of their differences. Further, the section quotes from an earlier contribution to say that “qualitative research, at least in the presently applied interpretive phenomenological form, is not well suited for null hypothesis significance testing” – this is completely true of course, but as it is not the aim of qualitative research to develop and test hypotheses, there is absolutely nothing wrong with that.
I cannot, then, help but wonder whether a more thorough engagement with the qualitative tradition would have yielded a better approach to the paper as well, in reflexively applying the kind of openness the author demands from qualitative researchers, to – at the very least – make explicit how their own assumptions about qualitative research are largely driven by assumptions from its opponents. Indeed, the author explicitly states that the research into qualitative hypotheses has been driven by a desire to “meet the technical expectations of a registered report”. De facto, then, the approach is not so much about elucidating qualitative hypotheses as it is about remodeling qualitative research in the image of quantitative research. This especially evident in frequent evocations of “the subjective”, for instance in the following sentence: “researchers’ expectations never derive solely from the literature but various sources in our subjective histories”, and further, “I would remove the emphasis on ‘hypothetical biases’—even though they remain relevant for some studies—and replace that with a more epistemically flexible ‘researcher subjectivity’” – without further analysis of what qualitative research actually tries to achieve, this is a roundabout dismissal in that the author retains the links from subjectivity to bias, omitting that qualitative research is essentially about mobilizing the lived experience of researchers.
In summary, I think the contribution lacks a thorough engagement (or even just a definition) of what qualitative research is or tries to achieve. I realize that this would be hard to achieve in a single paper, but I believe that if the contribution wants to be convincing, it needs to do three things: 1) provide a definition, better yet, a historical overview (can be brief) of qualitative research so that the reader can get a better picture of where hypotheses might fit in qualitative research, 2) provide a comparison between the core methodological assumptions of qualitative versus quantitative research, 3) analyze the potential role of qualitative hypotheses based on 1) and 2).
I think Michael Burawoy’s paper on what he calls the extended case method (Burawoy 1998), although certainly opinionated, would be an excellent starting point here in that it attempts 2). In it, Burawoy contrasts the model of positive science (represented by survey research) with reflexive science. In his analysis, positive science is characterized by the separation of subject and object, by reliability, reproducibility and representativeness, and by the exclusion of reactivity, while reflexive science, on the other hand, is characterized by intervention, process, structuring, and theory reconstruction.
