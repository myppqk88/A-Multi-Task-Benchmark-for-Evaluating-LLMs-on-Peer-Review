Point by point responses:

1. Point on clarifying the use of the concepts of 'stakeholders' and 'stakeholder engagement'



We rewrote a part of the theoretical section to refer more clearly to which specific strands of literature we draw from (literature on responsible research and innovation and challenge-led innovation). We now incorporate the role of Robert Edward Freeman in stakeholder engagement theory as well as Arlso Rip and Participatory Technology Development. Additionally, we specify stakeholder engagement within the paper to RRI and innovation literature where stakeholders are used to refer more broadly to societal actors like citizens, food producers, researchers, policy makers, and others. This approach fits the contribution of this paper more precisely, as with the paper we aim to contribute predominately with empirical knowledge on how stakeholder tools are developed in EU-led innovation processes. This is why we add additional theoretical grounding in these two branches, rather than making a connection to the business domain.
"While engagement with various societal actors has become more prevalent in EU policy documents and academic literature, reflections on engagement are also explored in different scientific fields. The concept of stakeholder engagement gained salience in the 1980s in domains such as Business Organisation (cf. Freeman, 2010), Constructive Technology Assessment (Rip, 2018), and Participatory Technology Development (within agricultural development domain). More recently, in 2010s, the increased incorporation of engagement approaches into EU R&I has been a result of the Responsible Research and Innovation concept, which builds on previous research on stakeholder engagement, emphasising inclusion, anticipation, responsiveness and reflexivity in innovation processes (Burget et al., 2017).
Within the European R&I projects, the institutionalisation of RRI took place through the development of methods to engage with the public, as well as making those freely available ( Owen et al., 2021). Within RRI, those to be engaged are often referred to as stakeholders (cf. Koch, 2020) to encompass the different groups and interests to be engaged. As the work on engaging various societal actors in policy grew in importance, it was also integrated into research within the sustainability transitions field, with engagement of stakeholders being emphasised as crucial for achievement of transformative objectives (Huttunen et al., 2022)."

2. Point on restructuring the introduction section



We restructured the introduction to clearly state the research question and while we refer to the existing literature, we do so to highlight the gaps in existing research (e.g. lack of empirical knowledge on participation in RRI and transformative innovation processes, as well as a lack of implementation knowledge on engagement in European R&I). For example: "At the same time, research engaging with RRI tools points to a disparity between theory and practice, as well as a need for more empirical knowledge on implementation of RRI processes (Marschalek et al., 2017)."
In the theory section, we significantly expand on the literature on European transformative innovation, stakeholder engagement (as well as clarifying we draw on sustainability transitions and transformative innovation fields' conceptions of engagement). We elaborate on these in other comments.
Finally, a research question has now been explicitly added: "To achieve that, the study tackles the question: How are engagement tools developed in R&I projects and what are the underlying logics of these processes?"
The length of the introduction was kept within the average of Open Research Europe publishing (500-800 words).

The code tree and interview guide have now been included as appendices to the paper, increasing transparency and allowing for greater replicability.

5. Point on clarifying the data analysis and coding process

We would like to clarify that the study employed a qualitative thematic analysis methodology, drawing on Saldaña’s (2021) framework for manual coding. The term 'code' in this context refers to analytical labels assigned to excerpts of interview transcripts during the process of qualitative data interpretation, not lines of programming code. Atlas.ti was used solely as a tool to support this manual process, by allowing us to organise, retrieve, and compare segments of text based on the assigned codes. No machine learning techniques, lexicometric analysis, or quantitative transformation of the data were used. The entire coding and interpretation process was conducted by the research team, based on close reading and constant comparison of the transcripts.
In response to your suggestion regarding transparency and replicability, we have now included the full coding tree used in our analysis as an appendix. This provides a complete overview of the codes developed during our thematic analysis. We hope this clarifies the nature of our methodological approach.
