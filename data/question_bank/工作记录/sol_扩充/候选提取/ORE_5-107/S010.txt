Point by point responses:

1. Point on tensions, risks, and consequences of stakeholder engagement



We incorporate perspectives on failures and risks of participation specifically in RRI and Mission oriented innovation - these highlight the political resistance and tensions that surface in innovation processes addressing societal issues; even when the participation processes are organised well. Furthermore, we highlight stakeholder fatigue and how the processes of participation can reduce trust of stakeholders when mismanaged.
"Failing to meaningfully engage with actors in these processes can have consequences such as stakeholder fatigue when stakeholders are engaged in extractive ways rather than seeking their input at all stages of projects (Bracken et al., 2015). Additionally, empirical research shows that bureaucratic and political resistance to participative processes regularly means that engagement does no’t translate to policy action (Wesselink et al., 2011). In R&I processes, the input of stakeholders is often not evaluated equally, with citizens’ voices frequently being undervalued. Furthermore, even when meaningful participation processes are designed, the political nature of missions might reinforce tensions and polarisation around certain societal issues (Janssen et al., 2021)."
Additionally, we open up the question of whether the user- and project- centered approaches also correspond to substantially distinct project outputs - e.g. do project-centered approaches result in tools that reproduce institutional logics.

2. Point on clarifying the agri-food domain focus



The focus on food systems related project is a result of all authors working on the CLEVERFOOD project at the time of the project. CLEVERFOOD creates networks of projects targeting food systems within Horizon Europe. We explain this in the manuscript as shown below. The question on sectoral limits and generalisability is answered in the subsequent review point.
"The study focuses on a subset of Horizon Europe projects, namely the projects under the Food 2030 umbrella, attempting to address the challenges of food system transformation. Potential projects were identified via a desk study involving the analysis of all ongoing and recently concluded Horizon Europe food and agriculture projects. This thematic subset of projects is a result of the authors’ work on the CLEVERFOOD project which engages broadly with food system related projects."

5. Point on the potential rigidity of the framework of user- and project-centered approaches



We agree that the project- and user- centered approaches should not be seen as binary or exclusive. Therefore, we add a clarification to the discussion expanding on the existing examples, mentioning that the two approaches can help explain the different rationales at specific stages of tool development, but projects on a whole are rarely exclusively project- or user- centered.
Where we see a strength of the current figure is in presenting the factors affecting the overall approach to tool development (EU R&I structural level and the Project coordination level) and the overall shift towards the user-focused approach. However, in the revised version of the manuscript, we explain in more detail about the spectrum of approaches to tool development.
"At the same time, the finding that different stages of the same tool development can be dominated interchangeably by a project- and user- focused approach shows that these two approaches are not exclusive, but rather serve to explain the predominant rationales at the different stages of a project process. In that sense, few projects can be deemed exclusively project- or user- centered."

6. Point on tensions between open science and commercialisation principles

We expand on this in the revised version of the manuscript by adding a reflection on how questions of tensions in tool development approaches can result in unexpected consequences for the underlying RRI and TIP principles of tool development. We do this in the discussion and propose future research to resolve the issue.
"Therefore, future research could look into whether user- and project-focused approaches result in substantially different tools (e.g. do tools developed by project-centered teams result in tools reproducing institutional logics?). On the same track, looking into how tool development approaches affect their conceptual RRI and Transformative basis could be a promising area of research. For example, while commercialisation approaches are broadly seen as worthwhile in ensuring post-project continuity, these types of strategies could limit the accessibility principles of RRI and thereby reduce the transformative potential of a tool."

8. Point on specifying the administrative burdens of Horizon Europe project implementation



We delve into the issue of how these administrative burdens manifest themselves in more detail in the section Transitioning towards socially robust engagement tools:
"The competitive nature of European R&I funding often pushes consortia to focus on project-focused aspects such as research excellence and deliverables in their project applications, rather than RRI aspects such as inclusion of vulnerable stakeholders, as they feel the former will be valued more highly ( Tabarés et al., 2022). As a result, there is often insufficient time, funding, and institutional support to adopt a fully user-focused approach. Additionally, the tight timelines of EU projects, combined with the precarious employment conditions of many researchers and project staff, further reinforce a project-focused approach.
Since securing future funding is a constant concern, teams often prioritise meeting deliverables deadlines over engaging in iterative, user-focused tool development. This is compounded by the fact that the individuals who draft grant proposals are not always the same as those who later execute the projects. As a result, commitments to stakeholder engagement made at the proposal stage may prove unrealistic in practice, particularly when organisational resources are stretched thin."

9. Point on data saturation and differences in specific properties of specific tools and projects



We agree that our sample included a range of projects across thematic domains and tool formats. However, saturation in this study was assessed specifically in relation to recurring patterns in the structure of the tool development process, participants’ motivations to develop tools, and the challenges and adaptations encountered across development phases. These were the central themes guiding our analysis, and it was on these dimensions that we observed sufficient thematic repetition across interviews.
While there were differences across projects in terms of their goals, outputs, and target user groups, we did not find meaningful differences in how participants described the phased logic of tool development. Both agri-food and bioeconomy projects, for example, followed a similar narrative arc when discussing how engagement tools were conceptualised, prototyped, implemented, and sustained. For this reason, we chose not to add further distinctions (e.g. sector or tool type) to Table 1, as it is intended to provide a descriptive overview rather than support a comparative analysis. We have now clarified this rationale in the revised methods section as follows:
"As our analytical focus was on recurring patterns in how the tool development process was structured, participants’ motivations, and responses to key challenges, saturation was assessed in relation to these themes rather than across sectoral or technological dimensions."

10. Point on specifying the findings in the post-project stage



We appreciate the positive feedback regarding the analysis of post-project challenges. In response to your suggestion, we have revised the relevant section of the manuscript to include concrete examples from our data. These include projects that leveraged their tools as the basis for new project proposals (e.g., Fit4Food leading to CLEVERFOOD, AgroBridges drafting a follow-up proposal), as well as tools adopted or further explored by NGOs, municipalities, or institutional actors (e.g., FOX, ECO-READY).
Regarding the third valorisation pathway i.e., commercialisation, although some interviewees shared that their teams were exploring this pathway, these efforts were at a preliminary or confidential stage at the time of the interviews. To respect participant confidentiality and avoid reporting outcomes not yet realised, we have chosen not to include specific examples of commercialisation in the manuscript.
The aforementioned changes are reflected in the manuscript throught the addition of the following text:
"For example, the CLEVERFOOD project originated from the earlier FIT4FOOD project, serving as a continuation mechanism to expand and consolidate engagement tools. In the case of AgroBridges, the consortium was actively developing a new project proposal to build upon the tools they had created. The FER-PLAY team also reported that they were exploring opportunities to capitalize on the outcomes of their project by applying for new funding that would allow continued development of their tools."
"In the FOX project for instance, a regional partner engaged with their local municipality to explore the possibility of continuing the use of a mobile unit and related app created as part of the project. Similarly, in ECO-READY, the project team discussed the possibility of leveraging strategic partnerships, including with the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, to embed their tools into external platforms, while also using Living Labs to explore future sustainability options."
