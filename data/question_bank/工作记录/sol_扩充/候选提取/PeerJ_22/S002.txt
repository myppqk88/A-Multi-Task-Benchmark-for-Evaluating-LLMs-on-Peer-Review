Poorer Verbal Working Memory for a Second Language Selectively
Impacts Academic Achievement in University Medical Students
Working memory (WM) is often poorer for a second language (L2). In low noise conditions, people
listening to a language other than their first language (L1) may have similar auditory perception skills
for that L2 as native listeners, but do worse in high noise conditions and this has been attributed to the
poorer WM for L2. Given that WM is critical for academic success in children and young adults, these
speech in noise effects have implications for academic performance where the language of instruction
is L2 for a student. We used a well-established Speech-in-Noise task as a verbal WM (vWM) test and
developed a model correlating vWM and measures of English proficiency and/or usage to scholastic
outcomes in a multi-faceted assessment program. Our model predicted significant performance
differences in an assessment requiring communications skills, but not on a companion assessment
requiring knowledge of procedural skills or other assessments requiring factual knowledge. Thus,
impaired vWM for an L2 appears to affect specific communications-based assessments in tertiary
medical students.

Introduction
In medical education, most information is communicated verbally, often

64

to large groups of students. Consequently, listening abilities and language

65

comprehension are critical to learning and require both auditory perception and

66

auditory working memory (WM) skills. WM is defined as “the system for the

67

temporary maintenance and manipulation of information, necessary for the

68

performance of such complex cognitive activities as comprehension, learning,

69

and reasoning...” (Baddeley, 1992, p. 281). One core element of WM, and in

70

particular verbal Working Memory (vWM), is the “phonological loop”, which has

71

been shown to be critical for language acquisition during development, as well

72

as language processing in daily life (Baddeley, 1992). However, it has been

73

widely reported that WM capacity may be limited for students who are learning

74

in an environment where the language of instruction is not their native language

75

(Andersson, 2010; Kroll, Michael, Tokowicz, & Dufour, 2002; Mackey, Philp, Egi,

76

Fujii, & Tatsumi, 2002; McDonald, 2006; Miyake & Friedman, 1998; Service,

77

1992; Service, Simola, Metsänheimo, & Maury, 2002; Sunderman & Kroll, 2009;

78

Tokowicz, Michael, & Kroll, 2004) and this appears to be due to demands on

79

WM resources in the second language (L2) (Service, et al., 2002).

80

The relationship between WM capacity and academic achievement has

81

been well studied in children (Alloway & Elsworth, 2012; Gathercole &

82

Pickering, 2000a, 2000b; Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge & Wearing, 2004;

83

Vock & Holling, 2008) and in university students and adults (Daneman &

84

Carpenter, 1980; Daneman & Hannon, 2001; Swanson, 1994; Tolar, Lederberg

85

& Fletcher, 2009). Whilst the studies in younger learners have shown strong

86

correlations between WM and high academic attainment (Alloway & Alloway,

87

2010; Gathercole, Pickering, Knight & Stegmann, 2004; St Clair-Thompson &

88

Gathercole, 2006), studies of university science students have reported that

89

WM has only weak or indirect effects in predicting academic performance

90

(Krumm, Ziegler & Buehner, 2008; Rohde & Thompson, 2007). Tolar et al

91

(2009) found WM strongly related to the adults’ ability on SAT scores, but

92

effects were reduced when other cognitive factors were controlled for, such as

93

spatial ability. Further, some studies suggest that vWM may not have as great

94

an effect on the students’ processing abilities as the direct effects of the

6

students’ first language (L1), including the ability to suppress L1 influences or

96

the level of L1 proficiency and general language aptitude (for review see Juffs &

97

Harrington, 2011).

98

In addition or in consequence of the poorer vWM for L2, the acoustic

99

environment to facilitate ideal listening conditions may also be crucial for

100

effective learning by L2 medical undergraduates. It has been noted that

101

non-native listeners may have similar speech perception skills as native

102

listeners in low noise conditions, but that these abilities significantly decrease in

103

high noise conditions (Buus, Florentine, Scharf & Canevet 1986; Florentine,

104

Buus, Scharf, & Canevet 1984; Lin, Chang & Cheung, 2004; Mayo, Florentine &

105

Buus, 1997; Tabri, Abou Chacra & Pring, 2011; Takata & Nabelek, 1990). Using

106

the Speech-in-Noise (SiN) task, Mayo et al. (1997) showed that not only was

107

speech perception in noise poorer in L2 learners, but that it was also dependent

108

on the age the L2 was acquired; bilinguals who learnt English after 14 years of

109

age had the worst performance in the SiN task compared to monolinguals and

110

bilinguals who learnt English before 6 years of age. Further, in contrast to the

111

monolinguals, the late bilinguals did not benefit from contextual cues in those

112

sentences that were highly predictive (i.e. sentences in which the subjects could

113

easily guess the target word). Similarly, Buus et al. (1986) found that the noise

114

tolerance level of non-native listeners to understand 50% of the test sentences,

115

increased with years of exposure to English, but never reached the level of

116

tolerance (and achievement) of a native English speaker.

117

There is evidence that the ability to process speech in noise influences

118

the ability to recall academic material. Ljung et al (2010) tested 48 native

119

Swedish university students with open-ended questions about the content of

120

spoken lectures of up to eight minutes presented in broadband noise or quiet, or

121

presented students with 10 paragraphs of lectures in classrooms of differing

122

reverberation times. The subjects’ memory performance was significantly worse

123

under both adverse conditions compared with the quiet condition, even when

124

the students had heard correctly the spoken lectures.

In the present study we examined the relationships between vWM for L2,

134

135

136

137

138

139

140

141

142

143

144

145

146

147

148

149

150

151

152

tests as the Reading Span Test (RST) when applied to L2 learners (Juffs &

153

Harrington, 2011). The ‘Speech-in-Noise’ or ‘SiN’ task tests vWM via the

154

phonological loop through storing, processing and recall of speech in

155

Material and Methods

158

Participants

159

All participants in this study were students enrolled in the MBBS program

160

from 2008-2010 at Monash University. This project was biphasic with all the

161

students, in the first instance, invited to complete a questionnaire asking for

162

information on their personal demographics, English acquisition and usage,

163

musical abilities and two psychometric measures: Perceived Stress Scale

164

(Cohen, 1994) and the Index of Learning Styles Questionnaire (Felder &

165

Soloman, 1994). Stress has been found to have a negative impact on the

166

academic performance of first year medical students, particularly international

167

students (Bagot, et al., 2005) as well as the style of learning adopted by

168

international versus local students, such as deep vs. surface learning styles

169

(Newble & Entwistle, 1986). As mentioned in the Introduction, the international

170

medical students of this course must pass stringent measures of English

171

proficiency prior to enrolment, such as the IELTS or the TOEFL. Therefore, the

172

questions on the survey pertained mainly to measurable English attributes such

173

as ‘In what order did you learn English and your other language’? There was

174

one question on the students’ perceived English and Language Other Than

175

English (LOTE) proficiency which was purely self-rated from a score of ‘0=poor’

176

to ‘4=excellent’.

177

The surveys were distributed at the commencement of each university

178

year in the 1st year of the medical undergraduates’ course. Of the 791

179

questionnaires distributed over the three years, 582 were returned giving a

180

response rate of 73.6%. Participation was voluntary and students could

181

withdraw at any stage.

182

In the second phase of the project, students were asked to participate in

183

a SiN test (described below). As it was not feasible to submit all 582 subjects to

184

this test, we performed a power analysis using GPower 3.0.10, which calculated

185

that we would require 15 subjects in each group to give us an effect size of 0.8

186

at a power level of 90%. We tested a total of 113 subjects. Of these, one

187

subject was excluded from data analysis due to hearing impairments and nine

188

candidates were classed as outliers with means more than two standard

Audiometry Testing

203

At the outset, hearing sensitivity in each subject was measured with

204

audiometry using a Beltone Model 110 Clinical Audiometer, calibrated to

205

present pure tones through calibrated TDH headphones. Hearing was tested

206

one ear at a time at 500Hz, 1000Hz, 2000Hz, 4000Hz, 6000Hz and 8000Hz.

207

The minimum sound level at each frequency was recorded as the threshold in

208

decibels Hearing Level (dB HL) relative to normal hearing sensitivity (ISO,

209

1989). We then calculated the bilateral four tone threshold average from

210

thresholds at 500Hz, 1000Hz, 2000Hz and 4000Hz. Generally, only subjects

211

with binaurally normal hearing (thresholds ≤20 dB HL) were included in data

212

analysis. However, two subjects had small hearing losses in one ear only (<5

213

dB) and one subject had a middle ear infection in one ear. Previous unpublished

214

research in our laboratory (and the fact that these data did not manifest as

215

outliers), has found that isolated unilateral cases such as these do not affect

216

end results and therefore, data from these subjects were included in analysis.

Test Sentences

235

Test sentences came from a standard battery of clinically-used sentences

236

(Bench, Kowal, & Bamford, 1979) adapted for Australian use (the BKB(A) list of

237

sentences). The BKB list contains 192 sentences, each of 4-6 words of no more

238

than two syllables. They are short, simple words and phrases imitating everyday

239

speech and do not include questions or explanations open to interpretation.

240

Also, these sentences contain words that have been shown to be very familiar

241

to non-English speakers (Brouwer, Van Engen, Calandruccio & Bradlow, 2012).

242

Each sentence consists of three keywords critical for comprehension of that

243

sentence. The sentences are pre-recorded in a female voice with an Australian

244

accent in a neutral tone and stored as .WAV files on the computer.

245

Sixty sentences with similar speech reception thresholds (SRTs: the

246

signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at which 50% of the subjects could correctly detect

247

the sentence in background noise) were selected for use in this study. Selection

248

and validation of these sentences have been detailed previously (Burns &

249

Rajan, 2008; Cainer, James & Rajan, 2008; Rajan & Cainer, 2008). The

250

sentences were randomly allocated to one of three lists classed as ‘Low’,

17
251

‘Moderate’ or ‘High’ to denote the level of the masking noise in which they were

252

presented; sentence level was always set to 80dBA.

Masking Noise

254

The masking noise was ‘babble noise’ (BN), created as described

255

previously (Burns & Rajan, 2008; Cainer, et al., 2008; Rajan & Cainer, 2008) to

256

give the illusion of eight voices speaking at once, known as the ‘cocktail party’

257

effect, digitized and stored as .WAV files. Sentences were presented to subjects

258

in a background of one of three noise levels: 1) Low noise level at 78dBA (SNR

259

of +2dB); 2) Moderate noise level at 81dBA (SNR of -1dB); and 3) High noise

260

level at 84dbA (SNR of -4dB). The noise was played continuously throughout

261

each test list and was turned off at the end of each list until just before the start

262

of the next list.

General Procedures

264

For the SiN discrimination task each subject was instructed that they

265

would be presented with three lists of sentences in noise, in succession. Each

266

list would consist of 20 different sentences in a fixed background noise level of

267

low, moderate or high. The order of lists, i.e., test SNRs was randomised

268

between subjects except that the high noise level list was never presented first

269

to ensure subjects did not start with the most difficult condition. The subject was

270

asked to repeat each sentence after it was played to the best of their ability, or

271

to indicate if they were unable to identify it at all, with no time limit imposed on

272

giving the response. The experimenter would score the response and then play

273

out the next sentence. After all 20 sentences in a list had been played, this

274

procedure would be repeated twice more, with a different list of sentences and a

275

different noise level, until all three lists had been tested.

276

Upon confirmation that the subject understood the instructions and was

277

ready to commence, the masking noise appropriate for the first test list was

278

switched on and played by itself for 5s before the first sentence was played.

279

Each sentence was scored as correct only if all three keywords were identified

280

correctly and in correct order. Once the experimenter had scored the response,

281

the next sentence was automatically played 1.5s later, and the test continued

282

until all 20 sentences had been presented. Subjects were given a short break

19
283

between lists. The order of presentation of sentences in each list was

284

randomised by the software so it was unique for each subject. Scoring of

285

performance in each list consisted of recording the percentage of sentences

286

they were able to recall in each list.

Indexing performance in the SiN task: calculating the SNR 50

288

For data analysis, the first step was to calculate the percentage of

289

sentences identified correctly by a subject for each list. This was done using

290

only the middle ten sentences for each noise level for the following reasons:

291

The first five sentences were discarded as training sentences as in our previous

292

studies (Burns & Rajan, 2008; Cainer, et al., 2008; Rajan & Cainer, 2008), and

293

the last five were discarded as some subjects showed signs of fatigue or loss of

294

concentration.

295

Then data from each subject was fitted with a linear function using

296

regression analysis and from the regression equation the midpoint of the

297

function – the SNR at which 50% of the sentences would be detected correctly

298

(SNR50) was determined. These SNR50 data represented the measure derived

299

from the SiN task as a measure of verbal working memory. We also calculated

300

SNR50 using only the last 10 sentences of each list and found generally similar

301

SNR50 effects. We therefore chose to use the middle 10 sentences as least

302

likely to be affected by either training effects or loss of concentration.

Discussion

487

The relationship between verbal Working Memory and academic

488

attainment has been well documented in L1, particularly with young learners

489

(Gathercole, Pickering, Knight, et al., 2004). However, the role of vWM in

490

predicting academic achievement in L2 adults, particularly medical students,

491

has been only occasionally examined with inconsistent effects (see Harrington

492

& Sawyer, 1992; Juffs & Harrington, 2011).

493

The aim of the current study was to explore if L2 vWM plays a role in

494

academic attainment in ESL students. We indexed L2 vWM using a SiN task as

495

a WM verbal test, as such tasks have been well documented to be a good

496

indicator of L2 vWM and because such a task reflected, to a consistent degree,

497

the background conditions occurring in some of the venues in which information

498

was imparted to student doctors in their course. Linguistically, English target

499

speech and English speech noise consist of many common properties (e.g.,

500

phonemes, syllable structures, prosodic features, etc.), which may make it more

501

difficult for listeners, particularly non-native, to segregate target language from

502

background noise and this may contribute to greater informational masking

503

(Van Engen, 2010). Background masking noise can be classed as energetic or

504

informational; energetic masking is thought to affect speech processing at the

505

level of the auditory periphery, whereas informational masking interferes with

506

higher-order processing such as attention and cognitive load. Informational

507

maskers have therefore been often used in working memory tasks to good

508

effect. Hygge et al (2003) found that meaningful irrelevant speech noise

509

significantly impaired recall in a text-reading memory task in 92 native high

510

school students in Sweden.

511

We also examined a number of other factors known or postulated to

512

influence L2 skills, in particular the age at which the participants first learnt

513

English (as their L2) as this factor has previously been shown to influence

514

English learning and proficiency (Johnson & Newport, 1989).

515

In our first analysis, we confirmed that the point of subjective

516

performance (PSE; the SNR50 score) in our SiN task was indeed a good index

517

of verbal working memory for L2 in our student population, with our results

34

1818

PrePrints

35
518

showing that the EFL students had smaller PSE scores than the ESL students.

519

This meant that the EFL medical students were better able to identify simple

520

English words in a noisy background than the ESL medical students. This was

521

an important step as this SiN task is free of L2 proficiency concerns that have

522

been a major criticism of previous studies that have used measures such as the

523

Reading Span Task (Harrington & Sawyer, 1992; Juffs & Harrington, 2011) to

524

show differences in L2 vWM and may be one explanation for the mixed findings

525

of past studies.

526

We then used this index of vWM along with English Language Skills

527

(ELS) as our model to predict academic attainment whilst controlling for the age

528

that English was first acquired by the student (AoAoE).

529

Different language-related factors affect different subcategories of the Objective

530

Structured Clinical Examination assessment

531

In Year 1, this overall model was not a strong predictor of academic

532

achievement, but there was a significant unique contribution of SNR 50 to the

533

OSCE Communications score, indicating that vWM has a role in this

534

assessment, and significant unique contributions of AoAoE and ELS to the

535

OSCE Technical scores indicating that language fluency rather than vWM is

536

involved in academic performance of the latter assessment. It is not surprising

537

that the OSCE subcategories were the only assessments that showed

538

significant correlations. This assessment type, particularly the Communications

539

component, is one that has continually shown major performance differences

540

between L1 and L2 medical students in many different countries and regardless

541

of whether the L1 is English or another language (Fernandez, Wang, Braveman,

542

Finkas & Hauer, 2007; Liddell & Koritsas, 2004; Schoonheim-Klein et al., 2007;

543

Van Zanten, Boulet & McKinley, 2003; Wass, et al., 2003; Woolf, Haq,

544

McManus, Higham & Dacre, 2007).

545

We have also found similar results in a current study of a larger cohort of

546

872 medical students (Mann, Canny, Lindley & Rajan, unpublished), in which

547

we did not measure L2 vWM or proficiency as in the present study. Our findings

548

in this study showed that in the first year of the course, international medical

549

students performed academically worse than their local peers in the OSCE

36

1919

PrePrints

37
550

assessment only, and not the Examinations or Coursework assessments. There

551

were similar findings in the second year of the course; however, some groups

552

did perform worse in all assessments including the OSCEs.

553

The above findings of the OSCE subcategories suggest that specifically,

554

the memorising and automated recalling of technical information may not be as

555

challenging to vWM as the complex task of trying to express conceptual and

556

abstract themes (i.e. higher-order cognitive processing) by the ESL students as

557

posited by Van Merriënboer and Sweller (2010). Similarly, Tyler (2001) suggests

558

that the knowledge and familiarity of a topic will determine how well a

559

non-native speaker will perform. Therefore, factual information that is

560

rote-learnt, such as the OSCE Technical, will be equally easy to recall for both

561

non-native experienced and inexperienced student doctors than unfamiliar

562

abstract or conceptual topics, such as needed in the OSCE Communication

563

tasks, which require good verbal working memory for the L2.

564

Although the impairment of communication skills is more apparent in the

565

2nd year of study, it is important to note that we collated second year data only

566

for the 2008 & 2009 cohorts and not for the 2010 cohort. The dynamics for the

567

years may not be the same and each year should be examined on its’ own

568

basis. Notwithstanding, this model again predicted academic performance in the

569

OSCE Communication assessment, suggesting both vWM and language

570

deficits in the ESL students affect this assessment subcategory in the second

571

year. Similarly, whilst the OSCE Technical model that was found to apply in 1 st

572

year was not overall predictive of academic achievement, there was a

573

significant correlation of vWM for this assessment subtype in 2 nd year. Together,

574

both OSCE subcategories point to L2 vWM impairments in these 2 nd year

575

students. This may be due to the 2nd year curriculum being more difficult than

576

basic first year outlines, and therefore the greater demands on English

577

language skills consequently resulting in poorer performance by the ESL. This

578

is quite possible as Collier (1992) has stated that growth curves on normalized

579

tests tend to flatten as students’ progress in age and grade level and as the

580

school load becomes academically more complex.

581

Overall, our model of L2 vWM (as indexed by the students’ PSE in the

582

SiN task) and English Language Skills was a strong predictor of academic

583

attainment (controlling for the age English was first learnt) for the OSCE

584

Communications assessment subcategory. The fact that the Communications

585

assessment was the only significant model is in itself significant, as although the

586

international students have proven English proficiency (via IELTS or TOEFL),

587

these medical students still perform academically worse than their local

588

counterparts in this assessment, even whilst achieving higher scores for the

589

other subjects.

590

Similar to the fact that we found no effects of L2 vWM on other

591

components of assessments, in a study using L1 participants, Kidd, Watson &

592

Gygi (2007) found only a weak correlation between SAT scores and auditory

593

abilities using SiN tasks. Using a broad WM test battery, Krumm, et al., (2008)

594

also found only small indirect measures of WM as a predictor of academic

595

performance. In contrast, Tolar, et al., (2009) found that WM strongly related to

596

an adult’s mathematical performance, but not when other cognitive factors

597

where controlled for.

598

Verbal WM is not the only factor poorer for an L2 learner. McDonald

599

(2006) reported that late English language learners had, in addition to poorer

600

WM, poorer English decoding ability and lower speed of processing in English.

601

Takano and Noda (1993) posited this slower speed of L2 processing as a

602

temporary decline in thinking ability because the demanding processing load

603

interfered strongly with the L2 subject’s thinking, beyond the normal foreign

604

language processing difficulties experienced by non-native speakers. Takano &

605

Noda (1995) demonstrated that this “foreign language effect” was greater the

606

more the foreign language was dissimilar to the native language, with greater

607

performance differences between, for instance, Japanese and English than

608

German and English, which share similar language roots.

609

It is important to note that only 51-75% of variance in academic

610

attainment is explained by general cognitive abilities (of which processing speed

611

and WM are two cognitive processes) (Rohde & Thompson, 2007). It is not

612

surprising then that correlations among working memory (or vWM) measures,

40

2121

PrePrints

41
613

e.g. reading span, generally tend to be moderate (Tolar, et al., 2009) as seen in

614

the aforementioned studies and the results of this report.

615

Limitations of the study

616

We have discussed our findings in relation to verbal WM as the SiN task

617

is a verbal/auditory task and, therefore, a measure of the phonological loop of

618

WM. We did not employ visual memory tasks, e.g. written examinations, and

619

further research into how the mode of presentation could affect outcomes is

620

required.

621

Further, we had categorised our AoAoE group as having acquired

622

English either before or after the age of 5 years old according to extant

623

literature. In our sample, the age range was 1-12 years, meaning that the

624

majority of subjects in our sample learnt English pre-puberty. Most studies find

625

greater discrepancies with L2 learners who have learnt English post-puberty

626

(~14 years old e.g. Mayo, et al., 1997). Therefore, our results may

627

underestimate the true effect of L2 age of acquisition on advanced learning.

628

Conclusions

629

In summary, our study contributes to the growing research examining

630

why non-native medical undergraduates generally perform academically worse

631

than their native speaker counterparts. The implications are that in a prestigious

632

course such as the MBBS degree, where all students have proven high

633

academic abilities, motivation and expectations prior to commencement, small

634

differences at the early stages could have disproportionate impacts on the

635

medical careers of L2 students, for example, in selection for highly competitive

636

specialist training positions or fellowships. Finally, it highlights an area where

637

international medical students continually to fall down despite rigorous

638

processes and comparable English proficiency.
