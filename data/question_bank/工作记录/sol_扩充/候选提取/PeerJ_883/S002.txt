Association between Virtues and Posttraumatic Growth:
Preliminary Evidence from a Chinese Community Sample after
Earthquakes
Relationship, Vitality, and Conscientiousness are three fundamental virtues that have been
identified recently. This study attempted to explore the relationship between the three
constructs and post-traumatic growth (PTG) in three directions, including indirect trauma
samples without post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), direct trauma samples without
PTSD, and direct trauma samples with PTSD. A total of 340 community participants from
Sichuan Province, Mainland China involved in the study, most of which experienced
Wenchuan and Lushan Earthquake. Results indicated significant and positive correlations
between virtues and PTG. In the indirect trauma samples, vitality explained 32% variance
of PTG. In reference to the direct trauma sample without PTSD, both relationship and
conscientiousness explained 32% variance of PTG; whereas in the direct trauma sample
with PTSD, only conscientiousness accounted for 31% the variance in PTG. These results
preliminarily revealed the role of virtues with important implications for strengths-based
treatment.

Introduction

27

Life-threatening events with potentially irreversible consequences, such as natural

28

disasters, cancer, and bereavement, are the events that often lead to positive changes and

29

transcendence, which is refer to post-traumatic growth (PTG, Calhoun & Tedeschi 2006). A

30

promising research direction is to study how personal strengths affect the manner by which a

31

person responds to and copes with health and well-being related concerns and worries (Hampson

32

& Friedman 2008), such as stressful and traumatic events.

33

Thus far, the most systematic approach for studying personal strengths is the Values in

34

Action Classification developed by Peterson & Seligman (2004), which included 24 character

35

strengths (e.g., hope, self-regulation, and gratitude) that grouped into 6 core virtues (e.g.,

36

wisdom, courage, and humanity). They concepulized virtue as “a property of the whole person

37

and the life that person leads” (p. 87), which is personal strength appreciated by the whole society

38

(Peterson & Seligman 2004). Many studies conducted in the past decade revealed the positive

39

relationship between these positive qualities and mental health (for review, see Niemiec 2013).

40

Accordingly, the relationship between these qualities and PTG was explored using 1,739 samples

41

from different countries (Peterson et al. 2008). Findings indicated that kindness, love, bravery,

42

hope, and religiousness have stronger relations with post-traumatic growth than other strengths

43

(Peterson et al. 2008). However, no correlation coefficient was higher than .35, thus reflecting the

44

weak correlation. A principal component factor analysis revealed a five-factor virtue structure,

45

including interpersonal, fortitude, cognitive, transcendence, and temperance. Correlation analysis

46

showed that all five virtues correlated with posttraumatic growth, but all correlation coefficients

47

were lower than .21 (Peterson et al. 2008).

48

Three issues should be noted about the study by Peterson et al. (2008). First, the virtue

49

structure in the aforementioned study is questionable. Various studies have found that the 24

50

identified strengths can be grouped into different virtues in different cultures (Duan et al. 2012).

51

As a result, the virtue structure should be clarified prior to delineating the function of virtues in

52

facing trauma and should be explored and analyzed further. Duan et al. (2012) adopted the

53

combined etic-emic approach to select 96 cross-culturally equivalent items from the original 240-

54

item VIA-IS using a Chinese sample. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses revealed

55

three virtues, namely, relationship, vitality, and conscientiousness (Duan et al. 2013). Therefore,

56

the relationship between the three virtues and PTG should be re-examined. Second, the research

57

conducted by Peterson et al. (2008) only examined the relationship among the trauma samples,

58

which accounted for 56% of the whole. However, previous studies argued that daily stressors or

59

indirect trauma can likewise facilitate stress-related growth assessed by Post-traumatic Growth

60

Inventory, although to a lesser extent than traumatic events (e.g., LoSavio et al. 2011). According

61

to the review, we hypothesized that there is little difference of PTG between indirect trauma

62

group and direct trauma group. Third, a number of trauma survivors may develop PTSD, whereas

63

others may not (Yehuda & Flory 2007). The above study missed to consider the influence of

64

PTSD to PTG in trauma sample. Furthermore, an inverted-U curve was found between the PTG

65

and PTSD, which suggested that PTG decreased after a moderate level of PTSD (Levine et al.

66

2008). Nevertheless, an increasing interest was noted among mental health professionals in

67

determining the strengths of their clients, such as those with PTSD.

68

To deepen our understanding of the function of strengths in PTG, the relationship between

69

both areas should be explored because relationship, vitality, and conscientiousness might be

70

differentially related to growth in samples of indirect trauma/direct trauma and PTSD/non-PTSD.

METHOD

72

Participants and Procedures

73

The announcement for study participation was published on community bulletin boards,

74

which can be seen by most people who lived in the community. Individuals who were interested

75

to participate were firstly instructed to complete Life Events Checklist (LEC), and only the

76

participants who experienced trauma were qualified to complete the other scale next week. A total

77

of 340 qualified respondents (109 males and 231 females) were recruited from different

78

communities in Dujiangyan area, Sichan, China, where affected by the 2008 earthquake. There

79

were 69 participants aged from 18-25, 101 in the range of 36-35, 112 in 36-45, and 58 above 46

80

years old. Only 51 participants obtain a university or the above degree. As expected, “natural

81

disaster”, “sudden, unexpected death of someone close to you”, and “life threatening illness or

82

injury” were the three most endorsed items listed on the traumatic events questionnaire (Support

83

Information Table 1). The study was approved by the human subjects committee of T.C.M.

84

Hospital Affiliated to Luzhou Medical College. All information collected was anonymous and

85

confidential. Psychological assistance was available to protect the subjects. Data were collected

86

from December 2013 to April 2014.

87

Measures

88

Life Events Checklist (LEC). LEC was used to screen individuals with direct or indirect

89

trauma through 17 potential events (Gray et al. 2004). Participants are requested to rate each

90

event on a 5-point Likert scale (1= happened to me, 2 = witnessed it, 3 = learned about it, 4 = not

91

sure, 5 = doesn’t apply). Participants who indicated at least one traumatic event as “happened to

92

me” were defined as the direct trauma sample, whereas the other three (2–4) were defined as the

93

indirect trauma sample. The participants who select 5 throughout the checklist were excluded.

94

Considering that all participants involved in the present study were earthquake survivors, who

95

lived in communities located in Dujiangyan area, rather than other place that is far away from the

96

earthquake, they should not be treated as persons who lived far away from Sichuan and were

97

affected by the earthquake only through radio and television.

98

Chinese Virtues Questionnaire. Virtues were assessed using the CVQ, which is a 96-item

99

simplified Chinese scale with good psychometric characteristics (Duan et al. 2013; Duan et al.

100

2012). The respondents were requested to rate each item from 1 (very much unlike me) to 5 (very

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (v2014:10:2972:0:1:NEW 4 Nov 2014)

Reviewing Manuscript
101

much like me) on a five-point Likert scale. A high mean score reflects a high degree of the virtue

102

within an individual. In this study, Cronbach’s α values of the three subscales were .91

103

(relationship), .85 (vitality), and .84 (conscientiousness).

104

Post-traumatic Growth Inventory-Chinese (PTGI-C). A 15-item Chinese version of the

105

PTGI (Ho et al. 2004) measures growth following a traumatic event. The measurement requires

106

individuals to indicate the extent of their experiences of changes as a result of crisis, ranging from

107

0 (not at all) to 5 (a very great degree). The reliability and validity of the 15-item version were

108

found accurate in previous studies (Ho et al. 2004). In the present sample, Cronbach’s α of the

109

inventory was .84.

110

PCL-S. PTSD symptoms were evaluated by the 17-item PCL-S. Participants are requested

111

to rate their experience from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Previous studies demonstrated that the

112

Chinese version can be used as a screening questionnaire among the Chinese population (Li et al.

113

2010). Scores of 44 or above indicate a PTSD diagnosis (Blanchard et al. 1996). Responses of the

114

diagnosed PTSD participants also fulfilled the criterions of DSM-IV, including a) “history of a

115

traumatic stressor”, b) “persistent re-experiencing of the traumatic event”, c) “persistent

116

avoidance of stimuli associated with the trauma and numbing of general responsiveness”, and d)

117

“persistent symptoms of increased arousal”. In the current sample, the Cronbach’s α of the whole

118

scale was .93.

DISCUSSION

137

This research is based on a community sample of people who were directly or indirectly

138

exposed to trauma. As expected, there was no difference of PTG between indirect and direct

139

trauma samples. A previous study indicated that a population indirectly hit by an earthquake

140

could still grow after traumatic experiences (Yu et al. 2010). In the present study, some

141

participants directly experienced an earthquake, whereas some might have indirectly experienced

142

this event through their friends, witnessed the death of a close friend, or experienced the

143

psychological distress caused by the death of a family member of a close friend. As a result,

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (v2014:10:2972:0:1:NEW 4 Nov 2014)

Reviewing Manuscript
144

participants are all likely to undergo PTG. In addition, the growth became low with the increased

145

severity of PTSD, partly exapainled by the inverted-U curve between the PTG and PTSD. As

146

reflected by the present result, the correlation between PTG and PTSD was near significant and

147

negative, especially in the PTSD sample (r = -.31, p = .07).

148

The main results revealed the different functions of virtues (i.e., relationship, vitality, and

149

conscientiousness) to PTG. In the indirect trauma samples, only vitality contributed the variance

150

of growth after trauma. In the direct trauma without PTSD sample, relationship and

151

conscientiousness were identified to explain the variance of PTG, whereas in the PTSD sample,

152

only conscientiousness was the significant predictor. Based on these findings, we can speculate

153

the different functions of virtues in different samples. Without directly experiencing trauma, most

154

people are troubled by small stress, and some are indirectly affected by trauma. When similar

155

scenarios occur, vitality can cause individuals to perceive less stress, thus reducing psychological

156

distress (Duan et al. in press). Our previous study identified only the students with higher vitality

157

perceived less stress from minor life events, which consequently introduced less psychological

158

distress (Duan et al. in press). What’s more, individuals with high vitality are more willing to

159

express their concerns to relieve stress and improve mental health (Zhang et al. 2014). After

160

being directly exposed to traumatic events, most people retain psychological balance without

161

significant symptoms of PTSD (Bonanno et al. 2007). Therefore, interpersonal resources related

162

to the virtue of relationship are necessary. Prati & Pietrantoni (2009) demonstrated that social

163

support is significant contributor to PTG using a meta-analysis with 103 studies. Individuals who

164

are rated high in the virtue of relationship are more likely to adopt a supportive mechanism to

165

overcome the predicaments caused by trauma. Finally, conscientiousness, also termed as self-

166

regulation, always facilitates positive mental health and decreases psychopathology (Hagger

167

2010). Duan et al. (in press) found that individual conscientiousness can directly decrease

168

psychological distress, regardless of the level of stress. Consequently, the intrapersonal strengths

Reviewing Manuscript
169

reflected by conscientiousness can be used to regulate emotion, cognition, and behavior to

170

resolve conflicts caused by trauma, thus enhancing growth after trauma.

171

Some limitations should be noted. First, similar to some studies on trauma, the design of

172

this study was cross-sectional rather than longitudinal. Results of this research and the

173

hypothesized functions of virtues in the development process of a traumatic event should be

174

replicated and examined in future longitudinal designs. Second, the direct and indirect trauma

175

samples were divided based on self-report measurements, thus leaving future studies to

176

distinguish the two samples objectively. Besides, indirect trauma sample far away from the

177

location of earthquake need to be examined in future. Third, all data was collected using self-

178

reported method, which may introduce common method bias. Future studies should adopt

179

multiple methods for controlling the bias. Finally, this study is the first to examine the function of

180

virtues in trauma research. More psychological outcomes, including flourishing, depression,

181

anxiety, and so on, should be explored in the future.
