In this study, we examined the relations between dimensions of Perceived Emotional
Intelligence (PEI) and classic constructs, such as social support, on depression, stress, and
subjective well-being indicators (life satisfaction and happiness). The study also sought to
determine whether PEI dimensions accounted for a significant portion of the variance
beyond that of classic constructs in the study of depression, stress, and well-being
outcomes in a sample of 442 unemployed subjects. Results indicated that social support
and all PEI dimensions are found to be significant and negatively related to depression and
stress, and these variables were also found to be significant and positively associated with
life satisfaction and happiness. Additionally, results using regression analysis indicated
that PEI, and specifically use of emotions and regulation of emotions, explain a significant
amount of the variance of all outcomes after controlling for socio-demographics and social
support dimensions. Finally, theoretical and practical implications of these constructs and
their relation with psychological adjustment and well-being in unemployed people are
discussed.

INTRODUCTION
Unemployment remains a major economic and social problem in the European Union.

21

According to the Eurostat database, the unemployment problem has been most acute in Greece,

22

Spain, Portugal, and Italy (Eurostat, 2016). In 2016, Spain has the second highest European

23

Union unemployment rate, after Greece. In short, the national unemployment rate in the first

24

quarter of 2016 for Spain was 21% with 4,791,400 individuals unemployed (INE, 2016).

25

Therefore, unemployment is one of the biggest problems for European countries, not only

26

because of its socio-economic impact, but also because of its psychological consequences. The

27

psychosocial impact of job loss is considered to be a major stressful event with long-term

28

consequences for individuals (Wanberg, 2012). Accordingly, it is well documented that job loss

29

is associated with a significant increase in psychological distress, depression, and worsening

30

mental health (Paul & Moser, 2009; Stankunas et al., 2006), and it is associated with a significant

31

decrease in psychological well-being (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005). Therefore, analysis of personal

32

resources that help to improve the experience of unemployment remains an issue of great interest

33

to researchers and career counsellors (Rey, Extremera & Peláez-Fernández, 2015). One of the

34

main avenues of research has focused on the role of cognitive–affective variables, which would

35

facilitate coping with these demands and would increase the levels of well-being (McKee-Ryan

36

et al., 2005). Numerous psychosocial predictors of mental health and well-being in

37

unemployment have been examined to better understand this relationship. These resources will

38

ameliorate the potentially negative consequences of unemployment and consequently provide

39

protection. Social support and Emotional Intelligence (EI) might be considered two of these

40

psychosocial resources.

41

Social support, psychological adjustment and well-being

42

Social support can be defined as the perception or experience that one is loved and cared

43

for, esteemed, and valued, and part of a social network of communication and mutual assistance

44

(Wills, 1991). A consistent body of research has emerged over recent decades to show that close

45

relations with family, friends, and significant others is a protective factor that helps guard against

46

the deleterious mental and health effects of unemployment (Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir, 2003).

47

Thus, lack of social support is positively related to depressive and anxious symptomatology, both

48

in the general population (Siedlecki et al., 2014) and among the unemployed population (Rey,

49

Extremera & Peláez-Fernández, 2015). Similarly, social support has been found to have a

50

particularly marked effect on how well individuals cope with unemployment, that is,

51

unemployed people cope with the loss of a job more successfully if they have a wide social

52

support network (Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir, 2003; Seblarska, Morse & Gunnesch-Luca, 2009)

53

In sum, the adverse impact of unemployment on the psychological adjustment and well-

54

being of unemployed individuals in supportive social contexts might be less than its effect on

55

unemployed individuals living in less supportive contexts (Rey, Extremera & Peláez-Fernández,

56

2015).

57

Emotional intelligence, psychological adjustment and well-being

58

There are currently two predominant models of EI: mixed and ability models (Mayer,

59

Roberts & Barsade, 2008). Mixed models describe EI as a broad conception of intelligence that

60

combines social skills, traits, and dispositional behavior. On the other hand, Mayer and

61

Salovey’s (1997) ability model of EI involves the ability to carry out accurate reasoning about

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
62

emotions and the ability to use emotions and emotional knowledge to enhance thought, thus

63

enabling the subject to solve social problems and to adapt effectively to the environment (Mayer,

64

Roberts & Barsade, 2008). In the operational definition of EI, these authors distinguish four

65

specific skills: (1) ability to identify and express emotions, (2) ability to use emotions in

66

decision-making, (3) ability to understand emotions, and (4) ability to regulate emotions, both in

67

oneself and in others.

68

A recent line of research has focused on the unique contributions of EI in explaining the

69

psychological adjustment and well-being of different collectives controlling for classic constructs

70

(Zeidner, Matthews & Roberts, 2012). The results of this research indicate that EI is negatively

71

related to stress and depression (Augusto et al., 2008; Salguero, Extremera & Fernández-

72

Berrocal, 2012), and positively related to various indicators of well-being (Gallagher & Vella-

73

Brodrick, 2008; Por et al., 2011). All these findings were obtained in samples of the general

74

population, but data on these relationships for unemployed people remain scarce. Prior studies

75

have found that self-regulatory processes may exert influence on both longer-term affective

76

states and on the mental health of individuals who lose their jobs (Wanberg et al., 2012). These

77

results suggest that affective and personal resources are important to subjective well-being and

78

psychological adjustment during unemployment (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005).

79

In sum, social support and EI might be resources that can help individuals to better cope

80

with stressful situations, such as unemployment, and to increase psychological adjustment and

81

well-being.

82

Motivation for the present study

83
84

Previous literature has confirmed empirical evidence of the relationship between social
support and psychological adjustment and subjective well-being in unemployed people. Despite

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
85

these findings, researchers have not examined the joint contribution of social support and EI

86

dimensions to well-being and psychological adjustment during unemployment. These findings

87

might be significantly useful in the design and development of employment promotion programs

88

and clinical interventions with unemployed people.

89

Given the aforementioned considerations, the purpose of this study was twofold. The first

90

purpose was to examine the relationships between social support, EI dimensions, stress,

91

depression, and indicators of subjective well-being (life satisfaction and happiness) in a sample

92

of unemployed people. Second, we examined the incremental validity of EI dimensions on stress,

93

depression, and well-being outcomes beyond what is accounted for by the influence of social

94

support. According to the aforementioned studies, higher levels of social support and EI

95

dimensions are expected to be negatively associated with psychological distress and depression

96

and positively associated with well-being outcomes. Thus, our study hypothesized that EI

97

dimensions will explain further additional variance in psychological distress, depression, and

98

well-being outcomes after removing any variance explained by social support.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

100

Participants

101

The sample was composed of 442 unemployed people (225 males and 217 females) from

102

two southern cities in Spain, who participated voluntarily and anonymously in the study. The

103

mean age was 32.2 years (SD = 9.9). The average duration of unemployment was 20.28 months

104

(SD = 30.7 months). The marital status of the participants was: 59.8% single, 13.5% married,

105

1.5% divorced, 16.2% widow(er), and 9 % cohabiting.

106

Instruments

107

Social Support

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
108

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) (Zimet et al., 1988)

109

consists of twelve items relating to perceived social support and is answered on a seven-point

110

Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Three separate scores

111

can be calculated for the sources of support: (1) Significant Other, (2) Family, and (3) Friends,

112

which can be added together to give a total social support score. Higher scores indicate greater

113

perceived social support. We used the validated Spanish version (Landeta & Calvete, 2002).

114

Emotional Intelligence

115

The Wong and Law Emotional Intelligence Scale (WLEIS) (Wong & Law, 2002) was

116

used to measure EI abilities. This self-report measure is based on the definition of EI proposed

117

by Salovey and Mayer (1990) and consists of four dimensions: (1) self-emotion appraisal, (2)

118

other-emotion appraisal, (3) use of emotion, and (4) regulation of emotion. Each subscale

119

consists of four items with a seven-point response format, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7

120

(strongly agree). The scale includes items such as: “I am quite capable of controlling my own

121

emotions”. This version of WLEIS has been shown to have good validity and reliability in

122

Spanish populations (Pena, Rey & Extremera, 2012; Rey & Extremera, 2011).

123

Depression, Anxiety and Stress

124

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995),

125

which aims to measure psychological distress was developed according to the tripartite model of

126

anxiety and depression and is a set of three self-report scales designed to measure the negative

127

emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress. Each of the three DASS scales contains

128

seven Likert-type scales. Alpha coefficients are all above .85 and validity has been supported

129

through its correlation with other measures of depression and anxiety (Lovibond & Lovibond,

130

1995). The Spanish version showed satisfactory internal consistency, convergent validity,

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
131

and an acceptable divergent validity (Bados, Solanas & Andrés, 2005). In our study, we used

132

stress and depression subscales.

133

Life satisfaction

134

We used the Spanish version (Atienza, Balaguer & Garcia-Merita, 2003) of the

135

Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) (Diener et al., 1985) to assess perceived global life

136

satisfaction. This scale comprises five self-referencing statements and requires subjects to rate

137

the extent to which they agree or disagree with each statement on a seven-point scale (1=

138

strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Both English and Spanish versions have shown evidence

139

for discriminant validity and appropriate internal consistency (Atianza, Balaguer & Garcia-

140

Merita, 2003; Diener et al., 1985).

141

Happiness

142

The Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS) (Lyubomirsky & Lepper, 1999) is a widely used,

143

4-item global assessment of happiness. Two items request respondents to describe themselves

144

using both absolute ratings and ratings relative to peers, whilst the other two items offer brief

145

descriptions of happy and unhappy individuals and ask respondents about the extent to which

146

each description describes them. Each item was assessed on a seven-point Likert scale (e.g., “In

147

general I consider myself”: 1 = Not a very happy person to 7 = A very happy person). Across 14

148

samples, the SHS has demonstrated good psychometric properties, such as test–retest reliability,

149

discriminant validity, and convergent validity (Lyubomirsky & Lepper, 1999). Furthermore, the

150

Spanish SHS has recently been translated into Spanish with satisfactory psychometric qualities.

151

We used a well-validated Spanish version (Extremera & Fernández-Berrocal, 2014).

152

Procedure

153

Participants who were utilizing the national employment office in a province of southern

154

Spain completed a survey about social resources, emotions, psychological adjustment, and well-

155

being. The survey also included measures of social support, Perceived Emotional Intelligence

156

(PEI), stress symptomatology, depressive symptomatology, life satisfaction, and happiness,

157

along with questions related to socio-demographics variables such as age, gender, and period of

158

unemployment. The order in which participants answered the measuring instruments was as

159

follows: first they responded to questions related to socio-demographics variables, this was

160

followed by the scales of social support, PEI, depressive symptomatology and stress

161

162

level of happiness. Data were collected over four consecutive months with the help of a team of

163

research assistants. All subjects were informed that they would be asked to participate in a

164

research study concerned with personality and emotions, and informed consent was obtained.

165

Respondents received no financial compensation for participation in the study.

166

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical

167

guidelines of the American Psychological Association. The study protocol was approved by the

168

Research Ethics Committee of the University of Jaén.

DISCUSSION

249

Theoretical models of coping with job loss suggest that psychosocial factors may be

250

important predictors of psychological distress and low levels of well-being (McKee-Ryan &

251

Kinicki, 2002). Examining the personal resources that protect people or place them at greater risk

252

for the adverse health consequences of unemployment remains an important focus for both social

253

researchers and career counselors. The main objective of this study was to determine the unique

254

and additional contribution of emotional intelligence skills on psychological distress, depression,

255

and well-being outcomes during unemployment, in combination with the other traditional

256

predictor of levels of distress and well-being during unemployment (social support).

257

Previous research has consistently documented the detrimental effect on mental health

258

and well-being that follows job loss (McKee-Ryan & Kinicki, 2002). In fact, unemployment has

259

traditionally been related to lower levels of well-being and related to higher levels of stress,

260

depression, and anxiety, among others (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005; Wanberg, Kammeyer-Mueller

261

& Shi, 2001).

262

Our results have showed that social support was negatively related to depressive

263

symptomatology and stress symptomatology and positively related to life satisfaction and

264

happiness. Furthermore, a similar pattern was found with EI dimensions. Specifically, all EI

265

dimensions were also negatively related to symptoms of depression and stress and positively

266

related to life satisfaction and happiness in the unemployed. These results are in line with

267

previous research and they extend prior work by underlining that these psychosocial resources

268

might weaken the negative psychological consequences of unemployment (depression and stress)

269

(Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir, 2003; Wanberg et al., 2012). Similarly, these personal resources

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
270

might also promote higher levels of well-being in the unemployed (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005;

271

Rey et al., 2015).

272

Regression analyses indicated that, beyond traditional socio-demographic variables,

273

social support was not a significant predictor of depression and stress levels. However, social

274

support did explain a significant percentage of variance in life satisfaction and happiness. These

275

findings are consistent with the available empirical evidence, according to which social support

276

is one of the main sources of subjective well-being (Siedlecki et al., 2014), and also with past

277

research on the link between social support and higher well-being in unemployed people (Rey et

278

al., 2015).

279

However, our results have shown that one of the dimensions of PEI, use of emotions, was

280

a significant predictor after controlling for socio-demographic variables and social support,

281

except for stress. This PEI dimension is related to a person’s ability to make use of his or her

282

emotions by directing them toward constructive activities and personal performance. An

283

unemployed individual who is highly capable in this ability would be able to encourage

284

him/herself to continuously do better in coping with job loss. Furthermore, those unemployed

285

people that have highly developed abilities to use and reason about emotions would also be able

286

to direct their emotions in positive and productive directions. They might also be better able to

287

control their affective reactions in response to unemployment depression and report higher life

288

satisfaction and happiness compared to their unemployed counterparts with a low ability to use

289

emotions. Thus, after controlling for the demographic variables and other well-known

290

dimensions, such as social support, use of emotional skills explained a significant and additional

291

variance in three out of four well-being outcomes. These results with the unemployed partially

292

replicate the results obtained in previous cross-sectional studies with other samples in which use

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
293

of emotions, assessed by WLEIS, was the most important predictor of all PEI dimensions in

294

predicting depression levels (Shi & Wang, 2007), life satisfaction (Law, Wong & Song, 2004),

295

and happiness (Khosla & Dokaina, 2010).

296

Moreover, after controlling for socio-demographics and social support, the ability to

297

regulate emotions was the only resource that explained significant variance in stress

298

symptomatology. It is tentative to think that unemployed people who are highly capable of

299

regulating their own emotions would be better able to manage stressful situation by carrying out

300

social and cognitive strategies, for example, cognitive reappraisal and emotional acceptance, to

301

effective manage negative emotions (Shallcross, Troy & Mauss, 2015).

302

Hence, it is noteworthy that the predictive power of EI abilities appears to be mostly due

303

to the joint contribution of use of emotions and regulation of emotions. Emotional information

304

plays a critical role in our working lives since active job searches are governed by rules of

305

behavior that are triggered by our emotions. Being able to effectively use emotions to facilitate

306

positive thoughts and to regulate emotions and actions may have an impact on health and well-

307

being, making unemployed people more adaptable to daily life stressors and more able to use and

308

repair emotions for managing conflicts and difficult situations, such as job rejections, financial

309

adversity, and interviews, among others. Individuals with high use and regulation abilities are

310

thought to clearly discern their moods and to access and generate emotions that assist with

311

optimistic thoughts, motivate positive actions, and mediate negative affects and behaviors, all of

312

which should contribute to greater levels of mental health and well-being.

313

Traditionally, positive psychological approaches have considered that healthy people use

314

psychological and social resources (self-efficacy, optimism, resilience, hope, humor, etc.) to

315

cope with stress, anxiety, and depression, as well as to increase their well-being (Seligman &

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
316

Csikszentmihalyi, 2000). Our results are in line with this reasoning and extend previous findings

317

that suggest unemployed people who score highly in emotional skills are better equipped to

318

handle stressful situations during unemployment and to report high psychological adjustment and

319

well-being outcomes.

320

In spite of its contribution to the field, this study is not without certain limitations. First,

321

self-reports were used as a method of assessment of both EI and well-being indicators, which is

322

not the best choice since self-reports are known to be contaminated by inherent shared method

323

variance problems and bias of overestimation (Schutte et al., 2007). Further research with

324

performance measures that test EI and medical health indicators for well-being and distress is

325

needed. Our study included unemployed individuals recruited by purposive sampling methods,

326

which is a non-random sampling technique. A limitation of adopting a purposive sampling is that

327

a small sample size and the non-random nature of the sample may place severe constraints on the

328

ability to generalize findings to the general population. Therefore, these findings need to be

329

replicated with other larger and random unemployed populations in order to generalize our

330

results. Finally, self-emotions appraisal and other-emotions appraisal (two dimensions of the PEI)

331

were not as statistically significant as predictor as were use of emotions and regulation. One

332

possible explanation for these weak associations could be related to the sample size; the use of a

333

greater sample could show more robust associations. A further possibility is that this could be

334

related to the low scores obtained by participants in depression and stress. Therefore, the use of

335

clinical samples with high levels of symptomatology could overcome this limitation.

336

Conclusions

337
338

To conclude, this research lends credence to those intervention programs that focus in
part on stimulating emotional abilities to enhance coping in individuals who experience

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:1:0:NEW 3 Aug 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
339

challenging and stressful situations during unemployment. Even though our research suggests

340

that specific emotional skills might play an important role in quality of life, further research may

341

better examine specific conditions in which EI is relevant through different stages of

342

unemployment, and may better define what objectives and key results are more important (i.e.,

343

active job searching, employability, psychological adjustment). Including EI training in

344

vocational guidance programs that emphasize the use of emotional skills may have the potential

345

to improve psychological functioning during unemployment (Hodzic et al., 2015). Moreover,

346

further mediational research should examine whether the impact of EI on employability is in part

347

a result of improvement in mental health and well-being. Identifying such factors would also

348

provide valuable information to both career counselors and social researchers in order to design

349

effective assistance interventions to improve quality of life during unemployment.
