Abstract
In this study, we examined the relationships between Emotional Intelligence (EI) dimensions and classic
constructs, such as social support for depression, stress, and subjective well-being indicators (life
satisfaction and happiness). Also, this study sought to determine whether EI dimensions accounted for a
significant portion of the variance beyond that of classic construct in the study of depression, stress, and
well-being outcomes in a sample of 442 unemployed subjects. As expected, results indicated that social
support and all EI dimensions were found to be significant and negatively related to depression and
stress. Similarly, social support and EI dimensions were found to be significant and positively associated
to life satisfaction and happiness. We conducted hierarchical regression analyses to examine and
evaluate the separate contribution of socio-demographic variables, social support, self-emotion appraisal,
other-emotion appraisal, use of emotion, and regulation of emotion for the prediction of stress,
depression, life satisfaction, and happiness. Additionally, results of regression analysis indicated that EI,
and specifically the use of emotions and regulation of emotions, explain a significant amount of the
variance of all outcomes after controlling for socio-demographics and social support dimensions. Finally,
theoretical and practical implications of these constructs and their relationship with psychological
adjustment and well-being in unemployed people are discussed.

INTRODUCTION

51

Unemployment is one of the biggest problems of modern societies, not only for its socio-

52

economic impact but also for its psychological consequences. The psychosocial impact of job

53

loss is considered a major stressful event with long-term consequences for individuals (Wanberg,

54

2012). Accordingly, it is well documented that job loss is associated with a significant increase

55

in psychological distress, depression, and worse mental health (Paul & Moser, 2009; Stankunas

56

et al., 2006), and it is associated with a significant decrease in psychological well-being (McKee-

57

Ryan et al., 2005). Therefore, analysis of personal resources that help improve the experience of

58

unemployment remains an issue of great interest to researchers and career counselors (Rey,

59

Extremera & Peláez-Fernández, 2015).

60

One of the main avenues of research has focused on the role of cognitive-affective

61

variables that facilitate coping with these demands and can increase the levels of well-being

62

(McKee-Ryan et al., 2005). Numerous psychosocial predictors of mental health and well-being

63

in unemployment have been examined to better understand this relationship. These resources can

64

ameliorate the potentially negative consequences of unemployment and consequently be

65

protective. Social support and Emotional Intelligence (EI) might be considered two of these

66

psychosocial resources.

67

Social Support, Psychological Adjustment and Well-Being

68

Social support can be defined as the perception or experience that one is loved and cared

69

for, esteemed and valued, and part of a social network of communication and mutual assistance

70

(Wills, 1991). A consistent body of research has emerged over the past decades to show that

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
71

close relations with family, friends, and significant others is a protective factor that helps guard

72

against the deleterious mental and health effects of unemployment (Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir,

73

2003). Thus, lack of social support is positively related to depressive and anxious

74

symptomatology, both in the general population sample (Siedlecki et al., 2014) and among the

75

unemployed (Rey, Extremera & Peláez-Fernández, 2015). Similarly, social support has been

76

found to have a marked effect on how well individuals cope with unemployment, that is,

77

unemployment people cope with the loss of job more successfully if they have a wide social

78

support network (Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir, 2003; Seblarska, Morse & Gunnesch-Luca, 2009).

79

In sum, the adverse impact of unemployment on the psychological adjustment and well-

80

being of unemployed individuals in supportive social contexts might be less than its effect on

81

unemployed individuals living in less supportive contexts (Rey, Extremera & Peláez-Fernández,

82

2015).

83

Emotional Intelligence, Psychological Adjustment and Well-Being

84

Mayer and Salovey´s (1997) ability model of EI involves the ability to carry out accurate

85

reasoning about emotions and the ability to use emotions and emotional knowledge to enhance

86

thought, enabling the subject to solve social problems and adapt effectively to the environment

87

(Mayer, Roberts & Barsade, 2008). In the operational definition of EI, these authors distinguish

88

four specific skills: 1) ability to identify and express emotions, 2) ability to use emotions in

89

decision making, 3) ability to understand the emotions, and 4) ability to regulate emotions, both

90

in oneself and in others.

91

A recent line of research has focused on the unique contributions of EI in explaining the

92

psychological adjustment and well-being of different collectives, controlling for classic

93

constructs (Zeidner, Matthews & Roberts, 2012). The results indicate that EI is negatively related

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
94

to stress and depression (Augusto et al., 2008; Salguero, Extremera & Fernández-Berrocal, 2012)

95

and positively related to different indicators of well-being (Gallagher & Vella-Brodrick, 2008;

96

Por et al., 2011). All of these findings were obtained in samples of the general population, but

97

data about these relationships among the unemployed remain scarce. Prior studies found that

98

self-regulatory processes influence both longer-term affective states and mental health in

99

individuals who lose their jobs (Wanberg et al., 2012). These results suggest that affective and

100

personal resources are important to subjective well-being and psychological adjustment during

101

unemployment (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005).

102

In sum, social support and EI might be a resource that can help individuals to better cope

103

with stressful situations, such as unemployment, and to increase psychological adjustment and

104

well-being.

105

Motivation for the Present Study

106

Previous studies in the literature have confirmed empirical evidence of the relationship

107

between social support with psychological adjustment and subjective well-being among the

108

unemployed. Despite those findings, researchers have not examined the joint contribution of

109

social support and EI dimensions to well-being and psychological adjustment during

110

unemployment. These findings might be significantly useful in the design and development of

111

employment promotion programs and clinical interventions with the unemployed.

112

Given these considerations, the purpose of this study was twofold. The first purpose was

113

to examine relationships among social support, EI dimensions, stress, depression, and subjective

114

well-being indicators (life satisfaction and happiness) in a sample of unemployed people. Second,

115

we examined the incremental validity of EI dimensions on stress, depression, and well-being

116

outcomes beyond what is accounted for by the influence of social support.

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
117

According to the aforementioned studies, higher levels of social support and EI

118

dimensions are expected to be associated negatively with psychological distress and depression

119

and associated positively with well-being outcomes. Thus, our study hypothesized that EI

120

dimensions will explain further additional variance on psychological distress, depression, and

121

well-being outcomes after removing any variance explained by social support.
MATERIALS AND METHODS

122
123
124

Participants
The sample was composed of 442 unemployed people (225 men and 217 women) from

125

two cities in southern Spain who participated voluntarily and anonymously in the study. The

126

mean age was 32.2 years (SD = 9.9). The average duration of unemployment was 20.28 months

127

(SD = 30.7 months). The marital status of the participants was: 59.8% single, 13.5% married,

128

1.5% divorced, 16.2% widow(er), and 9 % couple.

129

Instruments

130

Social Support

131

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988)

132

consists of 12 items relating to perceived social support and is answered on a 7-point Likert-type

133

scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Three scores can be calculated

134

for the sources of support: (1) Significant Other, (2) Family, and (3) Friends. These scores can be

135

added to give a total social support score. Higher scores indicate greater perceived social support.

136

We used the validated Spanish version of the MSPSS (Landeta & Calvete, 2002).

137

Emotional Intelligence

138
139

The Wong and Law Emotional Intelligence Scale (WLEIS; Wong & Law, 2002) was
used to measure EI abilities. This self-report measure is based on the definition of EI proposed

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
140

by Salovey and Mayer (1990) and consists of four dimensions: (1) self-emotion appraisal, (2)

141

other-emotion appraisal, (3) use of emotion, and (4) regulation of emotion. Each subscale

142

consists of four items with a 7-point response format, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7

143

(strongly agree). It includes items such as: “I am quite capable of controlling my own emotions.”

144

The version of WLEIS is proven to have good validity and reliability in Spanish populations

145

(Pena, Rey & Extremera, 2012; Rey & Extremera, 2011).

146

Depression, Anxiety and Stress

147

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale–21 (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), used

148

to measure psychological distress, was developed according to the tripartite model of anxiety

149

and depression. It is a set of three self-report scales designed to measure the negative emotional

150

states of depression, anxiety, and stress. Each of the three DASS scales contains seven Likert-

151

type scales. Alpha coefficients are all above .85, and validity has been supported through its

152

correlation with other measures of depression and anxiety (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995).

153

The Spanish version showed satisfactory internal consistency, convergent validity, and an

154

acceptable divergent validity (Bados, Solanas, & Andrés, 2005). In our study, we used the stress

155

and depression subscales.

156

Life Satisfaction

157

We used the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS; Diener et al., 1985) in its Spanish

158

version (Atienza, Balaguer & Garcia-Merita, 2003) to assess perceived global life satisfaction.

159

This scale comprises five self-referencing statements and requires subjects to rate the extent they

160

agree or disagree with each statement on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7

161

(strongly agree). Both the English and Spanish versions have shown evidence for discriminant

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
162

validity and appropriate internal consistency (Atianza, Balaguer & Garcia-Merita, 2003; Diener

163

et al., 1985).

164

Happiness

165

The Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS; Lyubomirsky & Lepper, 1999). The SHS is a

166

widely used, four-item global assessment of happiness. Two items request respondents to

167

describe themselves using both absolute ratings and ratings relative to peers, while the other two

168

items offer brief descriptions of happy and unhappy individuals and ask respondents the extent to

169

which each description describes them. Each item is assessed on a 7-point Likert scale (e.g., “In

170

general I consider myself”: 1 = Not a very happy person to 7 = A very happy person). Across 14

171

samples, the SHS has demonstrated good test–retest reliability, discriminant validity and

172

convergent validity (Lyubomirsky & Lepper, 1999). The SHS recently was translated into

173

Spanish with satisfactory psychometric qualities. We used a well-validated Spanish version

174

(Extremera & Fernández-Berrocal, 2014).

175

Procedure

176

Participants who were utilizing the national employment office in a province of southern

177

Spain completed a survey about social resources, emotions, psychological adjustment, and well-

178

being that included measures of social support, EI, stress, depression, life satisfaction and

179

happiness, along with questions related to socio-demographics variables such as age, gender, and

180

time of unemployment. Data were collected over four consecutive months with the help of a

181

team of research assistants. All subjects were informed that they would be asked to participate in

182

a research study of personality and emotions, and informed consent was obtained. Participants

183

received no financial compensation for taking part in the study.

184

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
185

guidelines of the American Psychological Association. The study protocol was approved as part

186

of the project Grant PSI2012-38813 by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Jaén.

187

Statistical Analysis

188

Preliminary analyses were carried out to compute descriptive statistics and internal

189

consistency, as well as to detect correlations among social support, EI dimensions, depression,

190

stress, and subjective well-being indicators (life satisfaction and happiness). To see if EI

191

dimensions are related to depression, stress, and subjective well-being indicators even after

192

controlling for the influence of social support, we conducted a three-step hierarchical regression

193

in which socio-demographic variables were entered first (as control variables), followed by

194

social support, and finally EI dimensions (self-emotion appraisal, other-emotion appraisal, use of

195

emotion, and regulation of emotion). These analyses were carried out using the SPSS package

196

(version 20.0; IBM, Chicago, IL).

DISCUSSION

262

Theoretical models of coping with job loss suggest that psychosocial factors might be

263

important predictors of psychological distress and low levels of well-being (McKee-Ryan &

264

Kinicki, 2002). Examining the personal resources that protect people or place them at greater risk

265

for the adverse health consequences of unemployment remains an important focus for both social

266

researchers and career counselors. The main objective of this study was to determine the unique

267

and additional contribution of emotional intelligence skills on psychological distress, depression,

268

and well-being outcomes during unemployment, in combination with other traditional predictors

269

of levels of distress and well-being during unemployment (social support).

270

Previous research consistently has documented the detrimental effect on mental health

271

and well-being following job loss (McKee-Ryan & Kinicki, 2002). In fact, unemployment

272

traditionally has been related to lower levels of well-being and related to higher levels of stress,

273

depression, and anxiety between others (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005; Wanberg et al., 2001).

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
Our results showed that social support was negatively related to depression and stress and

274
275

positively related to life satisfaction and happiness. A similar pattern was found with EI

276

dimensions. Specifically, all EI dimensions were negatively related to depressive and stress

277

symptoms and positively related to life satisfaction and happiness among the unemployed. These

278

results are in line with previous research and extend prior work that these psychosocial resources

279

might be a damper for the negative psychological consequences of unemployment (depression

280

and stress) (Bjarnason & Sigurdardottir, 2003; Wanberg et al., 2012) and to promote levels of

281

well-being of the unemployed (McKee-Ryan et al., 2005; Rey et al., 2015).
Regression analyses indicated that, beyond traditional socio-demographic variables,

282
283

social support was not a significant predictor over depression and stress levels. Social support,

284

however, explained a significant percentage of variance for life satisfaction and happiness. These

285

findings are consistent with the available empirical evidence according to which social support is

286

one of the main sources of subjective well-being (Siedlecki et al., 2014) and with past research

287

on the link between social support and higher well-being among the unemployed (Rey et al.,

288

2015).

289

Our results, however, showed that one of the EI dimensions, use of emotions, was a

290

significant predictor after controlling for socio-demographic variables and social support except

291

for stress. This EI dimension is related to a person’s ability to make use of his or her emotions by

292

directing them toward constructive activities and personal performance. An unemployed

293

individual who is highly capable in this ability would be able to encourage him- or herself to do

294

better continuously in coping with job loss. Also, those unemployed with highly developed

295

abilities to use and reason about emotions would be able to direct their emotions in positive and

296

productive directions. They might be better able to control their affective reactions in response to

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
297

unemployment depression, reporting higher life satisfaction and happiness, compared to

298

unemployed counterparts with low use of emotions. Thus, after controlling for the demographic

299

variables and other well-known dimensions, such as social support, the use of emotions skill

300

explained a significant and additional variance in three of four well-being outcomes.

301

These results for the unemployed partially replicate the results obtained in previous cross-

302

sectional studies with other samples in which use of emotions, assessed by the WLEIS, was the

303

most important predictor from all EI dimensions in predicting depression levels (Shi & Wang,

304

2007), life satisfaction (Law, Wong & Song, 2004), and happiness (Khosla & Dokaina, 2010).

305

Moreover, the ability to regulate emotions, after controlling for socio-demographics and

306

social support, was the only resource that explained significant variance in stress. It is tentative

307

to think that unemployed people who are highly capable in regulating their own emotions would

308

better manage stressful situation by carrying out social and cognitive strategies (e.g., cognitive

309

reappraisal and emotional acceptance) to effective manage negative emotions (Shallcross, Troy

310

& Mauss, 2015).

311

Hence, it is noteworthy that the predictive power of the EI abilities appears to be due

312

mostly to the joint contribution of the use of emotions and regulation of emotions. Emotional

313

information plays a critical role in our working lives because active job search is governed by

314

rules of behavior that are triggered by our emotions. Being able to effectively use emotions to

315

facilitate positive thoughts and regulate our own emotions and actions might have an impact on

316

health and well-being, making unemployed people more adaptable to daily life stressors and

317

more able to use and repair emotions for managing conflicts and difficult situations, such as job

318

rejections, financial adversity, and interviews, among others. Individuals with high use and

319

regulation abilities are thought to clearly discern their moods and to access and generate

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
320

emotions so as to assist optimistic thought, motivate positive actions, and modulate negative

321

affect and behaviors, which should contribute to greater levels of mental health and well-being.

322

Traditionally, positive psychology approaches have considered that healthy people use

323

psychological and social resources (self-efficacy, optimism, resilience, hope, humor, etc.) to

324

cope with stress, anxiety, and depression, as well as to increase their well-being (Seligman &

325

Csikszentmihalyi, 2000). Our results are in line with this reasoning and extend previous findings

326

that suggest that unemployed people who scored high in emotional skills are better equipped to

327

handle stressful situations during unemployment and to report high psychological adjustment and

328

well-being outcomes.

329

In spite of its contributions, this study is not exempt from certain limitations. First, self-

330

reports were used as method of assessment of both EI and well-being indicators, which is not the

331

best choice because self-reports are known to be contaminated by inherent shared method

332

variance problems and bias of overestimation (Schutte et al., 2007). Further research is needed

333

with performance measures of testing EI and medical health indicators for well-being and

334

distress.

335

Also, our study included unemployed individuals recruited by purposive sampling

336

methods, which is a non-random sampling technique. A limitation of adopting a purposive

337

sampling is that a small sample size and the non-random nature of the sample may place severe

338

constraints on the ability to generalize findings to the general population. Therefore, these

339

findings need to be replicated with other larger and random unemployed populations.

340

In sum, this research lends credence to intervention programs that focus in part on

341

stimulating emotional abilities to enhance coping in individuals who experience challenging and

342

stressful situations during unemployment. Although our research suggests that specific emotional

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2016:05:10858:0:1:NEW 7 Jun 2016)

Manuscript to be reviewed
343

skills might play an important role in quality of life, further research may better examine specific

344

conditions in which EI is relevant through different stages of unemployment and may better

345

define what objectives and key results are more important (i.e., active job searching,

346

employability, psychological adjustment).

347

Including EI training in vocational guidance programs that emphasize the use of

348

emotional skills may have the potential to improve psychological functioning in unemployment

349

(Hodzic et al., 2015). Moreover, further mediational research should examine if the impact of EI

350

on employability is in part a result of improvement in mental health and well-being. Identifying

351

such factors also would lend valuable information to both career counselors and social

352

researchers in order to design effective assistance interventions to improve quality of life during

353

unemployment.
