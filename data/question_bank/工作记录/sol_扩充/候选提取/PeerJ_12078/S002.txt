The impacts of COVID-19 have been felt on a global scale, with associated physical

distancing restrictions and economic downturn having ﬂow-on eﬀects for mental health

and wellbeing across the community, and for university students in particular. First-year
pharmaceutical and medical science students completing a common introductory
population health course at an Australian university are routinely surveyed at the

beginning of the semester as part of the course. Survey data inform teaching approaches
based on understanding the ‘real life’ commitments and health proﬁles of students, and

deidentiﬁed data form part of the teaching material. The 2020 student cohort was invited

to complete a second follow-up survey during COVID-19 physical distancing restrictions. A
total of n=126 students completed both the initial and follow-up surveys (50.6% response
rate), and n=99 (39.8% of the total cohort) consented for their data to be included in

research. There was a non-signiﬁcant decrease in student employment; however, 22% of
all students reported loss of work due to COVID-19. There was a statistically signiﬁcant

decrease in the proportion of students undertaking suﬃcient levels of physical activity,

and a statistically signiﬁcant increase in reported family stressors associated with loss of

employment or an inability to gain employment between March and May 2020. Two-thirds
of respondents reported increased stress as an impact of the transition to online learning.
Implementation of holistic strategies, incorporating attention to additional factors

inﬂuencing students’ capacity to engage in study, and which may have long-term impacts
across the life of the degree program, should be considered.

Introduction

43

The impacts of COVID-19 have been felt on a global scale, with the associated social distancing

44

restrictions and economic downturn having a flow-on effect on mental health and wellbeing

45

across all levels of the community. While some positives have been reported anecdotally as a

46

result of social distancing measures, such as the flexibility of working from home, being able to

47

spend more time with household family members, and reduced stress as a result of not having to

48

attend or ferry children to extracurricular activities, there is growing evidence for increases in

49

psychological issues such as anxiety and depression (Fisher et al., 2020).

50

The negative impact of COVID-19 has been felt in the university sector and for university

51

students in particular. This has manifested in many ways, from international students being

52

unable to travel to other countries to attend university, students concerned about graduating on

53

time, first year students adjusting to life at university, and for many students in general,

54

transitioning from on campus, face-to-face modes of delivery to the online learning environment.

55

A study of 1,000 Greek university students identified increased anxiety, depression, and suicidal

56

thoughts associated with the impact of COVID-19 (Kaparounaki et al., 2020). Similar

57

psychological findings were reported in a study of 2,530 Spanish university staff and students,

58

with greater impact for students compared to staff (Odriozola-González, Planchuelo-Gómez,

59

Irurtia, & de Luis-García, 2020). Evidence for psychological distress and an acute stress response

60

was also demonstrated in a study of 1,442 Chinese health students (Li et al., 2020).

61

It is anticipated that other factors, such as the loss of casual income due to the associated

62

economic downturn, housing concerns, food insecurity, the need for social distancing and its link

63

to social isolation, and the competing demands of family responsibilities, further exacerbate the

64

capacity of domestic and international students to thrive at university (Defeyter et al., 2020;

65

Morris, Mitchell, & Ramia, 2020). Additionally, while the shift to online learning may suit some

66

students, it brings with it concerns around equity for students who may not have access to stable

67

internet connections or suitable places to study (Hammond et al., 2020).

68

Since 2015, first year pharmaceutical and medical science students completing a common, on-

69

campus introductory population health course at an Australian university, have been routinely

70

surveyed at the beginning of the semester as part of their enrolment in the course. The survey,

71

largely modelled on national population-based surveys in Australia such as the National Health

72

Survey, collects data about the students’ sociodemographic background, their lifestyle and health

73

behaviours, and chronic disease status. These data are intended to inform teaching approaches

74

that are based on understanding the ‘real life’ commitments and health profiles of students, to

75

better provide teaching environments that enhance students’ learning experience and capacity to

76

learn. Additionally, deidentified data forms part of the teaching content for the course, enabling

77

teaching about concepts such as the social determinants of health and practical skills in working

78

with data. In late March 2020, the university ceased face-to-face education, in line with public

79

health recommendations. As a means of understanding the impact of COVID-19 on students’

80

capacity to engage with university studies during the pandemic, the 2020 student cohort was

81

invited to complete a follow-up survey in the midst of COVID-19 pandemic physical distancing

82

restrictions. This paper describes the outcomes of that survey.

83

Materials and Methods

84

Students enrolled in a first-year introductory population health course across the pharmacy and

85

medical science degrees for the first semester of 2020 (n=249), were invited by email, in the first

86

week of teaching at the start of the course (March 2020), to complete the survey routinely

87

administered at the start of this course each year. Students were subsequently invited by email in

May 2020 to complete a second online follow-up survey. The emails included information about

89

the specific purpose of each survey and the voluntary nature of survey completion was explained

90

to the students. Consent for both surveys was implied by the completion and submission of the

91

survey by the student. Students were asked to complete the survey within three weeks, with a

92

reminder email sent after 7-10 days. Students indicated their consent for their data to be used for

93

research purposes via a single question in each survey. Approval for this study was provided by

94

the University of South Australia’s Human Research Ethics Committee (protocol number:

95

0000034112).

Survey characteristics

97

Students completed two online surveys, the second being slightly adapted from the original

98

yearly-completed survey, to capture the impact of COVID-19 on lifestyle and health behaviours,

99

employment status, and capacity to undertake study. The surveys elicited general

100

sociodemographic information (e.g., age, gender identify, postcode of residence, height and

101

weight), self-rated health, physical activity behaviour, smoking status, fruit and vegetable intake,

102

health service and medication use, mental health diagnoses, family stressors, and income and

103

expenses. Questions in the follow-up survey focused on impacts of COVID-19 such as

104

employment, asking students if their employment status had been impacted by COVID-19, and if

105

so, how they were managing the loss of income; and housing, regarding whether their living

106

arrangements had been impacted. Students were asked to identify the specific factors that had

107

impacted their learning experience during COVID-19 restrictions, and to provide information

108

about the effect of the transition from face-to-face to online learning on their studies. An open-

109

ended question asked students to provide information about the positive and negative impacts of

110

COVID-19 on their lifestyle. The remaining questions required students to provide either a

yes/no answer (e.g., Do you currently smoke?), a specific numerical value (e.g., How many

Data analysis

116

Quantitative data were described using median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous data

117

that were not normally distributed, and proportions for categorical data. Differences in

118

sociodemographic and health-related factors between March and May 2020 were examined for

119

paired data using Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test for continuous data and the McNemar test for

120

categorical data. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25. Statistical

121

significance was set at α=0.05.

122

Results

123

A total of n=126 enrolled students completed both the March and the May 2020 online surveys,

124

representing a 50.6% response rate, and n=99 (39.8% of the total cohort) provided consent for

125

their data to be used for research purposes. Respondents had a median age of 18 years

126

(interquartile range (IQR)=2), and 70.3% were female. Sociodemographic characteristics are

127

reported in Table 1.

128

Almost half (46.5%) of the student cohort were in some form of paid employment at the

129

commencement of semester; however, 22.2% of all respondents reported experiencing loss of

130

employment due to the COVID19 pandemic. It is possible that some students, in contrast,

131

gained employment between the start of semester and May 2020, since there was no statistically

132

significant difference in the proportion of students reporting to be employed between March

133

(46.5%) and May (39.4%) 2020 amongst student respondents.

134

There was a non-significant decrease in the proportion of students reporting having a regular

135

source of income, and a non-significant increase in the proportion of students receiving money or

136

income from other sources, such as an allowance from parents. A total of 9% of the respondents

137

reported having changed accommodation due to the COVID-19 pandemic, however there was no

138

significant change in the reported expenditure on housing costs. Respondents reported a

139

statistically significant 25% decrease in the amount they spent in total each week on housing,

140

transport, food, entertainment and mobile phone bills (p=0.002).

141

There was a significant decrease (p=0.004) in the percentage of students being sufficiently

142

physically active and the percentage of students visiting the dentist (p=0.010), and significant

143

increases for family stressors related to ‘not able to get a job’ (p=0.004) and ‘involuntary job

144

loss’ (p<0.001). There was no significant change in psychological factors, nor were there

145

significant changes for other health-related characteristics, health service use, or family stressors

146

(Tables 2 and 3).

147

The most commonly reported COVID-specific factors related to studying in the online

148

environment were decreased motivation (88.89%), feeling isolated from peers (82.83%),

149

increased difficulties in understanding course content (72.73%), and increased stress (66.67%).

150

The most commonly reported impacts of social distancing measures were lack of interaction with

151

peers (85.86%), deferment of practical classes (65.66%), and lack of communication with

152

teaching staff (57.58%) (Table 4).

153

The open-ended question was completed by nine students (10%). Perceived positive aspects of

154

COVID-19 included being able to spend more time in the fresh air; more time for exercise;

155

preferences for more courses to be available online; having extra time to study due to reduced

156

travel time; and establishing a routine and becoming used to studying at home, albeit

157

accompanied by anxiousness about returning to on-campus learning. Negative perceptions of the

158

impact of COVID-19 related to issues with access to online teaching technologies, that resulted

159

in the student having to withdraw from courses; the home environment not being conducive to

160

study; not being able to maintain an exercise routine due to limited resources; decreased working

161

hours and loss of income; feelings of isolation from fellow students; and juggling parenting

162

responsibilities with study and work responsibilities.

163

Discussion

164

The purpose of this study was to understand the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and

165

associated factors on the capacity of first year university students to engage with university

166

studies. The findings would suggest that while there was a non-significant trend for changes in

167

employment and housing arrangements for the students themselves, family stressors associated

168

with loss of employment or an inability to gain employment were significant. The change in

169

these factors in the two-months between completion of each survey likely reflects the impact of

170

COVID-19 on employment circumstances at the global level.

171

Physical activity, in the form of students meeting physical activity guidelines of 150 minutes of

172

moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per week (UK CMO Guidelines Writing Group, 2019),

173

was significantly reduced in this cohort. This may be due in part to the onset of cooler weather at

174

the time of the pandemic; however, the closure of gymnasiums as well as changes in active

175

transport are also likely to be contributing factors. These findings are similar to those of another

PeerJ reviewing PDF | (2021:03:59110:0:1:NEW 26 Mar 2021)

Manuscript to be reviewed
176

study of university students, that reported a 30% decrease in physical activity compared to the

177

previous year (Gallo, Gallo, Young, Moritz, & Akison, 2020). The significant change in students

178

consulting the dentist may be due to the six-to-twelve monthly cycle of such visits; however, it is

179

likely that this was due to restrictions outlined by the Australian Government, including

180

deferment of all routine dental examinations and treatment (Australian Health Protection

181

Principal Committee, 2020).

182

There was no evidence of change in dietary behaviour nor was there identification of food

183

insecurity in this cohort; however, almost 70% of respondents live with their parents which may

184

be a protective factor. Other research suggests that students living independently are more likely

185

to have low levels of food security (Defeyter et al., 2020). These findings also differ from those

186

of Gallo et al. (2020) who found female university students had a higher energy intake than in

187

the previous year and in comparison to their male counterparts.

188

While there were some positives associated with the pandemic, factors such as decreased

189

motivation, feelings of isolation from peers, and increased difficulties in understanding course

190

content, as well as technological issues, and home environments that are not conducive to study

191

may impact the ability of students to successfully undertake university studies. These factors

192

reflect factors raised in recent studies (Hammond et al., 2020). Further, while there were no

193

significant changes in psychological factors in the current study, students did report increased

194

stress. Many universities have taken steps to support the study needs of students during COVID-

195

19; however, the implementation of strategies that provide a holistic approach, incorporating the

196

additional factors influencing students’ capacity to engage in study, which may have long-term

197

impacts across the life of the degree program, should be considered.

198

Limitations

199

One limitation of this study is that students were asked to self-report a previous diagnosis of

200

mental illness. Instead, application of a validated tool such as the Kessler-10 (Kessler &

201

Mroczek, 1992) may have provided a more accurate impact of COVID-19 on students’

202

psychological wellbeing. The small number of students who responded to the open-ended

203

question means that perceptions of the broader negative and positive impacts of COVID-19 are

204

not generalisable to the student population. Further, this study did not distinguish between

205

domestic and international students, which may have impacted the findings.

206
207

Conclusion

208

This study has described the impact of transition to the online learning environment, and social

209

distancing measures, as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. A number of factors have been

210

identified that likely impact the capacity of students to successfully undertake university studies

211

during pandemic-like circumstances. Longitudinal studies should be undertaken to investigate

212

the impact of such factors on the short and longer-term retention of students and students’

213

achievement at university.
