Article: The effects of teaching strategies on learning to think critically in primary and secondary schools: an overview of systematic reviews
DOI: 10.12688/f1000research.158087.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Keji Fan
Status: approved with reservations
Date: 2025-02-22
Report ID: 360693
============================================================

This overview provides a timely and important examination of current evidence synthesis in the field of critical thinking instruction. It points out issues such as the lack of consideration of adverse effects and the risks of bias assessment. However, several areas need to be addressed to enhance the overall quality:
Introduction
1. This section clearly defines key terms, such as critical thinking and teaching strategies. However, the justification for providing an overview of systematic reviews (SR) needs further clarification. Although the authors suggest that the overview will inform the design of their intervention,
Be Smart about Your Health
, it would strengthen the argument if they justified the necessity of the overview from both a methodological and a content-specific perspective regarding critical thinking instruction.
Methods
2. One of the objectives of this overview is to "inform the development of a framework for types of teaching strategies." However, pre-specifying teaching strategies could present potential issues. For instance, what if the included SRs identify additional teaching strategies? How can you check that the teaching strategies considered are comprehensive? What criteria were used to develop the list of teaching strategies, particularly given that some may overlap?
3. Does the overview account for publication bias, particularly with studies that remain unpublished? If not, this should be acknowledged in the limitations section. Additionally, it would be useful to discuss whether and how the inclusion of grey literature could alter your findings.
4. While you have used Doyle's framework on cognitive tasks, it is unclear how this framework relates to the development of your teaching strategies. Further explanation is needed. Additionally, some may argue that memorisation is not a critical thinking skill. How might this affect your study?
5. I think it is a good idea to consider SRs in terms of risks of bias, sub-group effects and adverse effects. However, more justification is needed to explain why these aspects are important. In other words, why should we consider these factors? How might they influence the field of critical thinking instruction? A brief discussion on why previous reviews failed to consider adverse effects would also be beneficial. Is it possible that primary research included in these systematic reviews overlooked these factors?
Results
6. A summary paragraph outlining the specific aspects of the SRs you considered would be helpful. Why did you choose these aspects, such as countries, educational levels, and subject areas? It would also be useful to discuss how SRs conducted in these countries might inform the East African context. Are certain components transferable to your teaching environment? Furthermore, as educational levels differ across various educational systems, would it be biased to categorise studies solely by educational level?
7. The categories in the tables may need rephrasing to make each one distinct. For example, in Table 3, the first category is "multiple subjects." How does this differ from other categories (I can see that there are explanations in the text)? Additionally, please check the consistency of the data between the text and the table. For instance, in the text, it states that "many of the reviews (112; 34.4%) included studies in multiple school subjects," whereas in the table, the number for multiple subjects is 113 (34.7%).
Discussion
8. While including countries and educational levels as categories to describe the current state of SR in critical thinking instruction is useful, it would be beneficial to discuss the possible reasons for these patterns. For example, why are critical thinking teaching reviews more or less prevalent in certain countries?
9. The broad focus of the overview is well-justified; however, a discussion on how specific teaching strategies might differ or be transferable when teaching critical thinking in the context of health education would add value. Is critical thinking instruction in areas such as language, mathematics, or social-emotional learning the same as in health education?
10. I agree with your justification that reported effect sizes should not be used to compare the effectiveness of teaching strategies due to their heterogeneity. This point could be made clearer in Figure 3, where average effect sizes from meta-analyses are presented. Could this be misleading if the average effect sizes are shown here?
