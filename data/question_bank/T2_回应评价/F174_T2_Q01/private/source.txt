Reviewer 2
Basic reporting

In the abstract, information like independent variable(s), dependent variable(s), expected results and actual results all should be included. However, the authors didn’t include briefly stated results in the abstract. In addition, as an expected factor which may influence the results of the study, the age of English language acquisition was left behind in the abstract too.

 The paper also emphasizes the role of age of language acquisition in speech perception, mentioning several differences in the ability to process speech between monolinguals and late bilinguals. It would be better if the authors show expected and actual results about the effects of age of second language acquisition in the abstract of the paper.
RESPONSE: Thank you for this advice. We have introduced a sentence in the Abstract to indicate that late bilinguals did worse in the speech in noise task. 
 In the introduction, the article should make more clarification about what the theoretical and empirical significance of the current study is and how the study can contribute to the understanding of the impact of verbal working memory on academic attainment, particularly for English as second language users.
RESPONSE: Thank you, this is a valid point suggested by both reviewers and which has now been addressed in the response to Editor, point 1.

The hypothesis/hypotheses of the study should be more clearly defined. It seemed in the abstract that the authors hypothesized poorer working memory under high noisy contexts significantly reduced the academic performance for L2. But the paper neither clearly stated the hypothesis/hypotheses or research question(s) in the discussion, nor did it explain why and how other constructs such as English proficiency (e.g., AoAoE, PEP) related to the hypothesis/hypotheses or research question(s).
RESPONSE: Please see response to Editor, point 2.
 Besides, the paper discussed in great details about why English proficiency would not be a confounding variable in the relationship between vWM and academic performance because all international students have to take standard tests in order to get the admission. However, this is a fair weak argument since passing stringent measures of English proficiency prior to enrolment, such as the IELTS or the TOEFL, would not guarantee that international students would performance as well as their local counterparts in academic achievements. In addition, in later examination, the paper extended to consider English proficiency as an indicator of academic achievements in the research design. It could be very confusing for readers to identify the role of English proficiency in this article.
RESPONSE: As the reviewer notes, the pre-entry English proficiency tests (IELTS or TOEFL) are highly stringent and designed to be so to ensure that English proficiency is high. We did not claim that this was a guarantee that international students will do well academically – we mean that such stringent tests, coupled with a face-to-face interview with English proficiency being evaluated, are the current criteria used to gauge English proficiency of international students. Our paper shows that there were significant negative correlations between seven of the nine test variables and the SNR50 and significant positive correlations between the other two variables (Stress) and Age of Acquisition of English (AoAoE), and the SNR50. Ultimately, we found that SNR50, AoAoE and English proficiency predicted academic performance. Thus, our paper suggests that a multi-factorial English proficiency test should be developed to generate a multi-dimensional metric that may better predict if a student who has learnt English as a second language will do worse academically.
We would note (p.24) that “Waters & Caplan (2005) have argued that traditional measures of WM do not relate to on-line processing of sentences which they postulate to be due to a specialised WM system; we believe that tasks such as the SiN task are likely to be better evaluators of WM in online processing of whole connected speech.”

Moreover, the evidence exhibited in the article does not sufficiently support the relationship between the “ability to process speech and to recall academic material.” Only one study (e.g., Ljung et al., 2010) from line 118 to 124 was cited for illustration. Given that this relationship is crucial for developing hypothesis/hypotheses, more relevant research from the extant literature would be better to enhance the argument.
RESPONSE:  Thank you for this comment. We had done an extensive literature search and to the best of our knowledge there is only this study that has explicitly examined the recall of academic material in high noise conditions, thereby demonstrating that vWM is impacted by speech in noise perception abilities. This especially highlights the novelty and importance of our study and was the reason why we conducted this research. The Ljung study supports point 2 of our hypotheses which states ‘that the students with lower SiN results will also have lower academic scores in their different assessments’. As stated above we note (p.24) the Waters & Caplan (2005) argument that traditional measures of WM do not relate to on-line processing of sentences; we believe that tasks such as the SiN task are likely to be better evaluators of WM in online processing of whole connected speech.

6. In terms of the limitations of the study, the within-subject experimental design may result in participants’ fatigue particularly when each participant in the study was required to listen and verbally repeat 60 sentences in total under three noise backgrounds (high, moderate and low). The author explained that high noise group would not be the first condition in order to putting the participants in a difficult situation at the very beginning. However, there is still a possibility that the participants who should have performed well scored low in the last group because of fatigue.
RESPONSE: We believe this is really not at all valid a concern. First, in other studies in the laboratory we routinely test subjects in FIVE such tests, with test lists randomised in order and we find no fatigue effects. Second, in the current study we used only 3 lists, each list takes 2-3 minutes to complete and we give subjects a 2 minute rest between one list and the next, and finally the three lists were randomised in order and analyses of the test order (not presented because the results were null effects) showed no fatigue effects.

7. The use of everyday clinically-used sentences may pose a threat to ecological validity. Given that students are taught academic contents, which are different semantically with everyday language (e.g., academic lecture use more long sentences and professional vocabulary), in undergraduate medical courses, they may not be able to fully emerge in experimental settings.
RESPONSE: The sentences are not clinically-valid; they are simple everyday sentences derived from an auditory test base (the BKB list as we detail) developed for testing in children and adults. Clearly if meant for testing with children, the sentences are not clinical at all. We would argue in fact that if we can find deficits in everyday language, then surely the effects must be even more pronounced when complex clinical language is used. Further, doctors on wards and other such situations are often going to encounter everyday language as the background noise from other patients and other non-medical hospital staff.
 
Validity of the findings

1. From line 367 to 374, when conducting the multiple regressions, the authors used five items significantly correlated to SNR50 that pertained to English proficiency and/or usage, such as AoAoE, PEP, MSE. However, there were insufficient justifications and supportive literature review to explain why the authors did this subdividing and how did these five items pertained to English proficiency and usage.
RESPONSE: Thank you for this comment. The five items were taken directly from the questionnaires completed by the students. The questions on the surveys pertained to measurable English usage questions such as ‘At what age did you learn English?’ where answers were the number written by the student, or ‘When you were growing up, how often did your mother speak English at home?’  where answers were based on Likert scales e.g., 1=never to 5=very often. These questions were mainly chosen because there is evidence in the current literature that these factors may influence academic attainment (e.g. parental influences, music, age of English acquisition). 
 2. In line 395, the authors did the principle component analysis for the five items pertaining to English proficiency and usage to rule out the threat of multicollinearity. What’s the VIF and tolerance for the new scale renamed “English Language Skills” (ELS)? Have the multicollinearity problem been solved successfully?
RESPONSE: Yes, after completing the factor analysis all VIF and tolerance figures were well within the normally accepted bounds and there was no evidence for multicollinearity as already stated in Line 412.
 3. The authors indicated that the new ELS was an approximation of the students’ overall English proficiency. How did they draw out this conclusion? Is there any empirical evidence or related literature to support this point?
RESPONSE: As stated the new ELS was an ‘approximation’ as measured by our survey and therefore arbitrary. We do not suggest that this can be generalised to all English proficiency measures. However, we believe it to be a reasonably good measure of the students’ skills given it asks how often they speak the language and how they themselves rate their proficiency.
4. In the discussion section, the authors classified the background noise into 2 groups: energetic and informational, which a) did not have literature to support the categorization; b) were ambiguous in the way that how the categorization fit into the hypothesis and the design of the experiment. According to the article, the author mentioned the use of headphone to play noise in line 232 and “babble noise” in line 219, but it is not clear what category do these belong to.
RESPONSE: We had presented only one reference to this because it is a very well established understanding in the field of auditory sciences. We have now expanded to include a few more references (e.g., Bronkhorst, 2000; Brungart, 2001; Brungart et al., 2001; Lutfi, 1990; Rheebergen and Versfeld, 2005; Scott et al., 2004; Simpson and Cooke, 2005; Van Engen, 2010; van Engen and Bradlow, 2007). It is also equally well established by these studies that energetic masking is typically non-speech sounds such as saw-toothed sounds, or single tones, or high-frequency noise. As babble noise is in essence speech, it is considered as informational masking in the field and the literature we have now included will demonstrate this to the reader. We have now revised this paragraph to avoid confusion.
