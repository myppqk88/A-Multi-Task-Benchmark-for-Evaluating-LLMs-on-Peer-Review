Dear Reviewer, we would like to thank you for your valuable and insightful comments. We have proceeded with major revisions on our manuscript, following most of your points, which we found well raised. These changes have bolstered our arguments, slightly extending the text’s wording, without changing its meaning. Please find below our responses to your comments in detail.
The article uses quantitative data to map skill mismatches in selected regions, and discusses reasons as to why these geographical variations arise.
In fact, it would benefit the article if it could focus even more on this phenomenon and its explanations. The introduction is interesting because it juxtaposes more traditional human capital theory with arguments derived from geographical political economy. However, while one could debate the outcomes of this intellectual tension, this is not really the core of the article (human capital theory is though dismissed as simplistic and positivist – a language I would myself avoid given the convincing literature on the overall benefits of education and higher skills). The focus of the article is not to estimate the benefits from education. Instead, I would wish for a stronger focus on the geography of skill mismatches in the introduction. This also would open to the real empirical geographical question in the article – what does the geography of skill mismatches look like, and why do differences between regions arise across Europe?

Apart from the above, we follow your suggestion and enhance our focus on the uneven geography of skill mismatches. In doing so, we have advanced the aim and the specific questions in the Introduction but also have adapted the subsequent sections accordingly. Especially in the Theoretical and Conceptual Section, we now focus more on the socio-spatial drivers of skill mismatches and explain the reasons why geographical variations arise. This is detailed when addressing your second point. Lastly, in the Introduction we put more emphasis on what the EU is doing to address skill mismatches.

To clarify the source for our calculated LQs, we now stress in the Methodological Section:
“Data on the matching between highly educated workers and low, medium, and high skill-level occupations are directly calculated by Eurostat,  based on its Labour Force Survey.”
Further below, we now add in regard to the reliability of the mismatch indicator:

Of course, Eurostat’s methodology carries some inherent weaknesses, such as that skill mismatches are judged solely based on the correspondence between a worker’s education attainment level (following the ISCED typology) and an occupation’s demanded skill level (following the ISCO typology). Although our study focuses mainly on the ERUA regions and countries, our findings can be considered more broadly. We state the reason for this in the Methodological Section, which is that the countries under study were chosen not only because of the ReERUA Project, but also because they represent all key educational/employment regimes in Europe. Specifically, we write:
“The analysis is conducted at the regional level (NUTS-2) and its geographical scope covers five countries: Greece, Bulgaria, France, Germany, and Denmark. These were chosen on the basis that they represent the main educational/employment regimes in Europe: the universalistic of the Nordic countries, the employment-centered of Continental Europe, the sub-protective of the Mediterranean countries, and the post-socialist of Eastern Europe (Pohl & Walther 2007; Farkas, 2011). While both France and Germany fall under the Continental model, they are both analyzed due to their stark differences in terms of their wage structures (Hein & Vogel, 2009) and educational models (O’Reilly et al., 2019). Given the research’s geographical scope covering all main European regimes pertaining to employment and skills, its findings can be contextualized more broadly and provide insight into the EU as a whole.”
The above is also implied in the Introduction and the Discussion.
The authors caution a warning for fig. 1.1a and b. It is hard what to make of this – is this part of the analysis reliable or not?
This is an extremely important point. To address it and secure the reliability of our analysis, we now clarify in the Empirical Analysis Section that:
“Paradoxically, in both Figures 2a and 2b, LQ scores fluctuate exclusively above 1, indicating over-concentrations in all regions for which data were available. The reason for this is because the phenomenon of highly educated workers being employed in low skill-level occupations is inherently limited. As a result, regions with a small number of such workers, which would exhibit LQ scores below 1, have a non-statistically significant sample, thus data for them is missing. Conversely, the available data allows for observing where highly educated workers are over-concentrated in low skill-level occupations.”
In other words, our observations regarding where highly educated workers in low skill-level occupations are concentrated, we follow a process of elimination. Namely, although we do not have sufficient data to directly observe which regions exhibit under-concentration of this type of skill mismatch, we systematically exclude all other possibilities based on the available data, and we are left with the answer to our problem.
In the discussion section, the authors are looking for different ways to explain the geographical variation in the mismatches. It would be good to know how the authors ended up thinking about these factors.
To address this comment, we have revised several sections of our manuscript. In short, we can state here that we ended up with these based on the insight of the relevant literature. This was the case from the start; however, it is a fact that this was not adequately clarified in our initial submission. Thus, including them in the Theoretical and Conceptual Section was crucial. Doing so, we cover the literature around the role of the dynamism of national economies in skills’ geography in a paragraph starting with:
“Various factors shape the widely documented uneven geography of skills and their mismatches (Alamá-Sabater et al., 2024; Cedefop, 2010). Importantly, regional mismatches can be greatly affected by the robustness (or lack thereof) of the broader national economy.”
Similarly, we write about the effect of regional specializations:
“At the regional level, the dynamism of an economy is equally crucial. Capital- and technology-intensive activities tend to concentrate in already advanced regions, fueling new rounds of uneven development (Vinuela-Jimenez et al., 2010). As such, metropolitan areas with a diverse economic base concentrate human capital, cutting-edge technology, and finance-related activities, while economically backward and declining regions become even more disconnected from knowledge and innovation (Feldman et al., 2021). Similarly, developed industrial regions tend to attract new activities, workforce, and capital, as they further industrialise (Boudeville, 1968), although in many cases they also spill over positive effects to their surrounding areas (Perroux, 1955).”
The role of institutional endowments is scrutinized throughout this section, as for instance in the part we write:
“Such segmentation is crucially sustained by dual human capital development mechanisms. Namely, next to a robust education system, a training system that focuses more on vocational training and apprenticeship initiatives is formed, serving as a channel for precarious labour (Teixeira, 2019).”
Apart from the above, we lay out the relevant literature’s insight into the effect of crises of various types on the geography of skill mismatches in the paragraph starting with:
“Beyond the national and regional factors, sudden shocks of economic or other nature, as well as structural transitions, directly contribute to increasing skill mismatches (Cedefop, 2015).”

To address this crucial comment, which was among the most important we received, we proceeded with major revisions on our manuscript. Specifically, following your suggestions, we added a correlation analysis (through Pearson’s R) to our implemented methodologies. Specifically, bivariate correlations have been conducted between the LQ
L

The results of the correlation analysis are presented in detail in a correlation matrix (Table 1). We believe that with the above we make our arguments in the Discussion more convincing, since they are now clearly evidence-based.
To complement this, we should always keep in mind that the LQs illustrate the spatial variation of a phenomenon (relative), and not how important the phenomenon itself is (absolute). In fact, it would be good to know the absolute level too – how sizeable are the mismatches around European regions? This would be a way to illustrate the absolute importance of the phenomenon.
Please note that apart from the LQs that are presented in Figures 2-4, we also include a table in the Annex that displays all raw data based on which we calculated the LQs. Specifically, in this table we lay out the shares of highly educated workers per skill-level occupation within each region under study. This way, the reader has a clear view of the magnitude of the phenomenon under study. We also considered including the absolute volumes of the above, however, that would make the table much bigger, which we decided to avoid.

In the revised version of the text, we toned down the statement about “strong interventions”, writing instead that:
“To break this pattern of uneven development, policy interventions are required.”
Nevertheless, in the revised Conclusions policy recommendations are more targeted and concrete, drawing directly from the findings of the Empirical Analysis. Specifically, they revolve around the significance of: knowledge spillover and alignment between education and innovation needs; entrepreneurial activity rechanneling into underdeveloped regions; and lagging regions’ re-specialization in more dynamic high value-added and high-skill sectors. For each one of those, we provide specific examples and/or cases.
