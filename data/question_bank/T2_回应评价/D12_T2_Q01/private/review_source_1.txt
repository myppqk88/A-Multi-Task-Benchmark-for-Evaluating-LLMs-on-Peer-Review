Article: Investing in human capital in the era of European Universities: A comparative analysis of skill mismatches across the regions of the ERUA countries
DOI: 10.12688/openreseurope.16470.1
Version: 1
Reviewer: Martin Henning
Status: not approved
Date: 2024-01-02
Report ID: 36271
============================================================

This article approaches a very interesting and important topic: the geography of skill mismatches in Europe. The article uses quantitative data to map skill mismatches in selected regions, and discusses reasons as to why these geographical variations arise.
In fact, it would benefit the article if it could focus
even more
on this phenomenon and its explanations. The introduction is interesting because it juxtaposes more traditional human capital theory with arguments derived from geographical political economy. However, while one could debate the outcomes of this intellectual tension, this is not really the core of the article (human capital theory is though dismissed as simplistic and positivist – a language I would myself avoid given the convincing literature on the overall benefits of education and higher skills). The focus of the article is not to estimate the benefits from education. Instead, I would wish for a stronger focus on the
geography of skill mismatches
in the introduction. This also would open to the real empirical geographical question in the article – what does the geography of skill mismatches look like, and why do differences between regions arise across Europe?
I would also recommend a revision of the literature review in the same fashion (section: Human capital and its uneven spatial distribution). Right now, this section focuses on human capital theory and some sources of mismatches. The discussion makes a valid point around the importance of institutions (in fact, one could probably argue that labour market and education institutions are among the most variable factors across the countries within the single market). Moreover, the geographical discussion treats the issue of regional divergence and uneven regional development. On this topic, there is already a sizeable literature, and this should also not be the core discussion of this paper. While I would actually like to challenge some of the arguments in a within-European context (for example, that growth is a trade-off between regions, the peculiar connection made between vocational training and precarious labour and the new rounds of “Fordist-influenced spatial organization” – within Europe the Fordist era was in fact characterized by decreasing regional economic disparities), this is besides the point.
Instead, I would like to recommend a stronger focus on the geographies of mismatches and why they arise. The paper would make a stronger contribution if it could remain with this focus. In particular, I would welcome a discussion about the dimensions that the authors later return to as explaining the geography of mismatches: dynamism of national economies, regional specializations, local institutions and demographic structure. In such a context, for example, the discussion about the global production chains and their spatialities is interesting, but it does not quite explain the geography of mismatches.
In the empirical part, the use of the different LQs is ingenious. However, it would be great if the authors could comment on the reliability of the mismatch indicator: is there a risk that we simply misjudged the level of skills needed in jobs, or that they changed? And where in the economy do the most important mismatches arise? I believe that this could be easily described using the available data, as an introduction to the geographical analysis. It would also be good if the authors explained whether the patterns could be taken to be general – or are the results a feature of the regions/countries that selected themselves into the university initiative?
The authors caution a warning for fig. 1.1a and b. It is hard what to make of this – is this part of the analysis reliable or not?
In the discussion section, the authors are looking for different ways to explain the geographical variation in the mismatches. It would be good to know how the authors ended up thinking about these factors (linking to the theoretical section above). Secondly, it is a bit hard for the reader to evaluate the relevance of the factors. Here, I would recommend the authors to provide empirical evidence. If not in terms of regressions, so at least displaying the co-variation between the factors and mismatches in regions. For example, one could display a scatterplot between the size of the mismatch in regions, and the demographic structure. I believe that relevant indicators could be calculated for all the dimensions. Such an exercise would make the arguments much more convincing and interesting.
To complement this, we should always keep in mind that the LQs illustrate the spatial variation of a phenomenon (relative), and not how important the phenomenon itself is (absolute). In fact, it would be good to know the absolute level too – how sizeable are the mismatches around European regions? This would be a way to illustrate the absolute importance of the phenomenon. Also, but this is maybe too much of an extension, it would be very interesting to know if the mismatches actually have consequences for regional growth (or, are the mismatches rather a result of growth because growing regions attract labour that maybe cannot be accommodated immediately?). Using the lag structure of the data, such an experiment could be made.
In the policy recommendations, the authors argue for “strong interventions”. However, the interventions that they mention seem to be only vaguely related to the findings of this paper (except maybe for the high-quality career counselling). Instead, I would encourage the authors to develop policy recommendations that are immediately associated with the findings of this paper.
In sum, I find the topic and empirical approach very promising. However, revisions according to the above would give the paper a much stronger basis to argue for particular and precise conclusions concerning the geography of skill mismatches in the European Union. I do hope that the authors find these comments constructive for further work.
